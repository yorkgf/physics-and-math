# 2.3 Market Research — Vocabulary

> AP Business with Personal Finance · Topic 2.3 · CED 2026

---

## Core Terms

1. **Market research** (EK 2.3.A.1) — The systematic process of gathering, analyzing, and interpreting information about a market, including potential customers and competitors, to guide business decisions.

2. **Quantitative data** (EK 2.3.A.2.i) — Numerical information that can be measured and expressed using numbers, such as sales figures, percentages, or survey ratings.

3. **Qualitative data** (EK 2.3.A.2.ii) — Non-numerical information expressed in words, images, or observations, such as customer opinions, motivations, or feelings.

4. **Desirability** (EK 2.3.A.3.i) — Whether a product or service solves a real customer problem or fulfills a genuine need (problem-solution fit).

5. **Feasibility** (EK 2.3.A.3.ii) — Whether the business has or can acquire the resources, technology, and capabilities needed to deliver the product or service.

6. **Viability** (EK 2.3.A.3.iii) — Whether the product or service can generate sustainable profit and long-term financial success.

7. **Secondary-source research** (EK 2.3.B.2) — Analysis of data that already exists, such as government reports, industry publications, or competitor websites, to understand the broader market landscape.

8. **Primary-source research** (EK 2.3.C.1) — Collection of new, original data directly from target customers through methods like surveys, interviews, experiments, and observations.

9. **Business hypothesis** (EK 2.3.C.2) — A specific, testable assumption about a business idea (e.g., "Customers prefer Feature A over Feature B") that is fed INTO a research method for validation.

10. **A/B testing** (EK 2.3.C.7) — A controlled experiment that compares two versions (A and B) of a single variable—such as a webpage headline or pricing—by randomly assigning real customers to each version and measuring which performs better.

11. **Survey** (EK 2.3.C.4) — A structured set of questions distributed to a large number of respondents to collect primarily quantitative data on preferences, behaviors, or demographics.

12. **Data visualization** (EK 2.3.D.1) — The graphical representation of data through charts, graphs, or maps to make patterns, trends, and comparisons easier to understand.

---

## Additional Terms

1. **Focus group** (EK 2.3.C.5) — A moderated discussion with a small group of target customers (typically 6-10) to explore attitudes, perceptions, and reactions to a product or concept.

2. **One-on-one interview** (EK 2.3.C.5) — A direct, in-depth conversation with a single respondent to gather detailed qualitative insights, often including follow-up questions.

3. **Experiment** (EK 2.3.C.6) — A controlled test in which a researcher deliberately changes one variable while holding others constant to observe the effect on behavior or outcomes.

4. **Observation** (EK 2.3.C.6) — A research method in which a researcher watches and records customer behavior in natural settings without altering the environment or interacting with subjects.

5. **Bar chart** (EK 2.3.D.3) — A chart that uses rectangular bars of varying heights to compare discrete categories or data points.

6. **Stacked bar chart** — A bar chart in which each bar is divided into segments representing sub-categories, showing both the total and the composition of each category.

7. **Line graph** — A graph that connects data points with lines to display trends or changes over a continuous period.

8. **Pie chart** — A circular chart divided into slices that represent the proportion or percentage share of each category within a whole.

9. **Sample bias** — A systematic error that occurs when the sample of respondents is not representative of the broader target population, leading to skewed or unreliable results.

10. **Research method selection** (EK 2.3.C.3) — The process of choosing which primary research method (survey, interview, focus group, experiment, A/B test, observation) to use based on the types of data needed to test a specific business hypothesis.

11. **Research limitations** (EK 2.3.C.9) — The reality that businesses cannot always accurately predict customer or market responses and must sometimes innovate with limited, unclear, or contradictory data — no research method eliminates all uncertainty.

## Trap Pairs

| Pair | Why Confused | How to Tell Apart |
|------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Quantitative vs. Qualitative** | Both are types of data collected during research. | Quantitative = numbers you can count and graph (e.g., "72% prefer X"). Qualitative = words and images you interpret (e.g., "I like X because..."). |
| **Secondary vs. Primary** | Both aim to understand the market. | Secondary = data that already exists (government stats, industry reports). Primary = data you collect yourself (surveys, interviews). |
| **Hypothesis vs. Finding** | Both relate to the research cycle. | Hypothesis = the assumption you feed IN before testing (e.g., "Feature A is preferred"). Finding = the result handed BACK after analysis (e.g., "Feature B was preferred 70%-30%"). |
| **A/B Test vs. Observation** | Both study customer behavior. | A/B test = you deliberately change something (Version A vs. B) and compare outcomes. Observation = you watch behavior without changing anything. |
| **Survey vs. Interview** | Both collect customer input. | Survey = large sample, primarily quantitative, fixed questions, little or no follow-up. Interview = small sample, primarily qualitative, open-ended, with follow-up questions. |
| **Desirability vs. Viability** | Both assess whether to pursue a business idea. | Desirability = "Do customers want this?" (problem-solution fit). Viability = "Can we make money doing this?" (profitable path). A product can be desirable but not viable. |

## Key Relationships

### Research Method Hierarchy

```
Market Research
├── Secondary-source research (landscape scan, cheap, fast)
│   → Industry reports, government data, competitor websites
│   → Use FIRST to understand the broad market
│
└── Primary-source research (your own customers, targeted)
    ├── Surveys — quantitative, large population, fixed questions
    ├── Interviews — qualitative, small sample, open-ended + follow-up
    ├── Focus groups — qualitative, group dynamics, moderated discussion
    ├── Experiments — behavior, controlled variable changes
    ├── A/B testing — behavior, real-world, two versions compared
    └── Observations — behavior, natural settings, no interaction
```

### Desirability → Feasibility → Viability Pipeline

```
Desirability (Do they want it?)
        ↓
Feasibility (Can we build it?)
        ↓
Viability (Can we profit from it?)
```

A business idea must pass through all three in this order. A product that is desirable but not feasible should not be pursued. A product that is feasible but not viable will not sustain the business.

### Data Integrity Chain

```
Large, representative sample
        +
Neutral question phrasing
        ↓
Reliable, valid findings
        ↓
Sound business decisions
```

Sample bias or leading questions corrupt findings at the source.

### Visualization Selection Guide

```
Bar chart       → Compare distinct categories (e.g., sales by region)
Stacked bar     → Compare totals AND composition (e.g., revenue by product by quarter)
Line graph      → Show trends over time (e.g., monthly website traffic)
Pie chart       → Show share of a whole (e.g., market share percentages)
```

## Must-Know Formulas and Lists

| Item | Detail |
|------|--------|
| **A/B Test Math Example** | Version A: 7 sign-ups out of 9 visitors = 7/9 ≈ 78%. Version B: 3 sign-ups out of 8 visitors = 3/8 ≈ 38%. Version A wins by ~40 percentage points. |
| **Survey Base / Denominator** | Always verify the denominator. 30 out of 120 respondents (25%) is different from 30 out of 200 respondents (15%). Never report a raw count without the base. |
| **Desirable → Feasible → Viability** | The required order of evaluation. Desirability tests customer need first; feasibility tests operational capability second; viability tests financial sustainability last. |
| **Secondary before Primary** | Always conduct secondary research first—it is cheaper and faster and helps refine the questions you ask in primary research. |
| **Sample Bias Red Flags** | Convenience sampling (only surveying friends), voluntary response bias (only passionate people respond), undercoverage (missing a key demographic). |
