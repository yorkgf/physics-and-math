# 4.4 Strategic Frameworks: Porter's Five Forces and SWOT Analysis

## Core Terms

| # | Term | Definition | Connects To 
|---|---|---|---
| 1 | Porter's Five Forces | A framework developed by Michael Porter to evaluate the competitive intensity, attractiveness, and potential profitability of a competitive environment | 4.4.A.1 
| 2 | Competitive rivalry | The intensity of competition among existing businesses in a market, determined by the number of competitors, product differentiation, and pricing power | 4.4.A.3 
| 3 | Threat of new entrants | The risk that new businesses will enter the market and increase competition; determined by barriers to entry | 4.4.A.4 
| 4 | Barriers to entry | Obstacles that make it difficult for new businesses to enter a market, such as high capital requirements, brand loyalty, regulations, and economies of scale | 4.4.A.4, 1.3 
| 5 | Threat of substitute products | The risk that customers will switch to alternative products that meet the same need but are not direct competitors | 4.4.A.5 
| 6 | Customer (buyer) power | The ability of customers to influence pricing and terms, shaped by the number of customers, acquisition costs, and switching costs | 4.4.A.6 
| 7 | Supplier power | The ability of resource providers to raise input costs, shaped by the number of suppliers and the cost of switching to alternatives | 4.4.A.7 
| 8 | Switching costs | The monetary and psychological costs a customer incurs when changing from one product or brand to another | 4.4.A.6 
| 9 | SWOT analysis | A framework evaluating internal (Strengths, Weaknesses) and external (Opportunities, Threats) factors that influence a business's ability to accomplish its goals | 4.4.C.1, 4.4.C.2 
| 10 | Strengths | Internal advantages a business controls, such as core competencies, brand recognition, skilled employees, and financial resources | 4.4.C.3 
| 11 | Weaknesses | Internal disadvantages a business faces, such as missing competencies, limited funds, outdated technology, and poor customer service | 4.4.C.4 
| 12 | Opportunities | External factors beyond a business's control that may contribute to success, such as market growth, technology advancements, and favorable regulations | 4.4.C.5 
| 13 | Threats | External factors beyond a business's control that may negatively impact the business, such as rising costs, natural disasters, and disruptive innovation | 4.4.C.6 
| 14 | PESTEL | A framework analyzing external macro-environmental factors: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, and Legal | 4.4.D.2 

## Additional Terms

| # | Term | Definition | Connects To |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | Competitive environment | The overall market conditions in which businesses compete, including the number and strength of competitors, customers, and suppliers | 4.4.A.1 |
| 16 | Product differentiation | The degree to which a product is perceived as unique or distinct from competitors' products | 4.4.A.3, 1.3 |
| 17 | Pricing power | A business's ability to raise prices without losing customers to competitors | 4.4.A.3 |
| 18 | Economies of scale | Cost advantages that businesses obtain due to their scale of operation, where cost per unit decreases as output increases | 4.4.A.4, 3.4 |
| 19 | Intangible assets | Non-physical assets such as brand recognition, reputation, intellectual property, and company culture | 4.4.C.3, 4.4.D.1 |

| 21 | Disruptive innovation | An innovation that fundamentally changes the way customers meet their needs, often displacing established businesses | 4.4.C.6 |
| 22 | Input costs | The prices paid for raw materials, component parts, and resources needed to produce goods or services | 4.4.A.7 |
| 23 | Resource market | The market in which businesses purchase the inputs they need to produce their products (e.g., grain market, labor market) | 4.4.A.7 |

## Trap Pairs

| Pair | Term A | Term B | Key Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Strengths** | **Opportunities** | Strengths = internal factors the business CONTROLS (e.g., skilled employees). Opportunities = external factors the business CANNOT control (e.g., market growth). The key test: can the business directly change it? |
| 2 | **Weaknesses** | **Threats** | Weaknesses = internal disadvantages (e.g., limited budget). Threats = external risks (e.g., new competitor enters). Weaknesses can be fixed by the business; threats must be responded to. |
| 3 | **Competitive rivalry** | **Threat of new entrants** | Rivalry = competition from EXISTING businesses. New entrants = competition from businesses that DON'T EXIST YET but could enter the market. |
| 4 | **Customer power** | **Supplier power** | Customer power = buyers' ability to push prices DOWN. Supplier power = sellers' ability to push input costs UP. Both squeeze the business's margins from different sides. |
| 5 | **Substitute products** | **Direct competitors** | Substitutes = different products that meet the same need (e.g., food truck vs. restaurant). Direct competitors = same type of product (e.g., restaurant vs. restaurant). |
| 6 | **Porter's Five Forces** | **SWOT analysis** | Five Forces = evaluates ONLY the external competitive environment (industry attractiveness). SWOT = evaluates BOTH internal (S, W) and external (O, T) factors. Five Forces is an input to the "T" in SWOT. |
| 7 | **Barriers to entry** | **Switching costs** | Barriers to entry = obstacles for NEW BUSINESSES entering the market. Switching costs = costs for CUSTOMERS changing brands. Both protect existing businesses but from different threats. |
