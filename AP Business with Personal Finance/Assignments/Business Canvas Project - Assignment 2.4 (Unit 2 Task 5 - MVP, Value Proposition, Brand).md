## Before You Start: The Framework

**Seven rules from the 2.4 lesson that this worksheet runs on:**

1. **Raw material ≠ product** (EK 2.4.A.1). A raw material is the input you start with; the product is **what the customer pays for**. Keep this straight the whole way — it is the most common way a Product section goes wrong.
2. **You are joining a six-stage sequence in the middle.** Ideation → Validation → **Design → Messaging** → Production → Launch. 2.4 does Design (Section 2), Messaging (Section 3) and the start of Branding (Section 4). Your 2.3 findings are the research that the value proposition must be built **from** — not decorated with.
3. **MVP = the simplest sellable version that lets real customers vote with real dollars.** It is not a prototype shown to friends; it is an offer with a price and a way to commit. **Product-market fit** = demand sufficient to **generate profit**.
4. **Design is three dimensions, priced from cost data.** **Functionality** (how it works), **user experience** (how the customer encounters it), **attributes** (measurable features). And the design sets the cost: parts, delivery time, hours.
5. **Value proposition = who + what problem + why us** — a statement built from market research. **Positioning** = how the customer perceives you **relative to rivals** (both of them, not your favourite one).
6. **A brand is a name, term, design, symbol or combination that identifies and differentiates** your product; branding has **three jobs** — distinguish, raise awareness, generate loyalty. It can travel through any of those forms. **Trademarks** give legal protection against imitation; **generic products** are the honest alternative for buyers who prioritise cost over brand.
7. **Brand equity is earned, not bought** — the added financial and competitive value a product gains because customers recognise, trust and prefer it, built through **consistency across every touchpoint**. It is also what a copycat cannot photocopy, which is why Section 5 asks you to be copied on purpose.

> **Strong-answer method (all through this worksheet):**
> **State the evidence (money or number) → name the concept (LO/EK) → tie it to YOUR product, YOUR customer, YOUR price.**

**Four standing clauses carried over from 1C and 2.1 — they apply here in full:**
1. **The asterisked tests must be able to fail**, and behaviour beats attitude. *"Would you buy this?"* is an opinion; *paying, pre-ordering, returning, referring* are evidence. If you did not run the test, mark it **PENDING** with the recording plan — never write a verdict with nothing behind it.
2. **Numbers must be checkable and consistent.** One unit cost, one unit price, one currency, across 1.8, 2.1, 2.3 and this file. State fixed/variable split; state LTV horizons; the MVP's cost per unit feeds 2.5 directly.
3. **Concept application, not metaphor.** Do not call a logo a "brand identity" if it does nothing for the customer; do not call a free trial a "MVP test of product-market fit" — an MVP must be capable of producing a **sale**.
4. **Self-check integrity.** Tick a box only if you can point at the sentence that earns it.

## Important Notes

1. **Same business idea, same team, same customer** as every prior assignment. If your MVP says something that contradicts your 2.1 profile or your 2.2 tactic, that is a finding — **document the revision** and say which checkpoint was wrong.

2. **Two asterisks in one task.** *MVP used to assess product-market fit* **and** *branding/brand identity* both require hypothesis testing; the *value proposition* does not — it needs research and reasoning. Do not trade them: a brand test is cheap (show five people and ask what promise they heard) and a fit test needs money in the loop.

3. **This is the feed into 2.5 Price.** Section 2's cost per unit, contribution per unit and the price you actually offered at **become** the pricing-strategy input. If you leave 2.4 without a costed unit, 2.5 will be opinion. The tag promised *"half of retail"* — 2.5 asks where the \$95 came from.

4. **Negative and partial results are the point.** "Half of 10 paid once and none returned" is a strong answer with a change attached; "everyone loved it" is the weak one. The CED wants the loop, not the win — and 2.4's own lesson is New Coke at scale (\$12 million, 200,000 people, pulled in three months) and Dyson's Contra Rotator (great reviews, poor sales, discontinued) plus the cancelled \$2.5 billion electric-car programme: **a documented stop is good management, not failure.**

5. **Ethics and claims.** A product sold to classmates is sold to people who trust you, and some of them may be minors. Every claim about health, safety, authenticity or certification must be provable — and where a category is regulated (food, cosmetics, account-based services), the licence is part of the product, not a footnote. If the honest version of your claim would not sell, that is a product problem to fix, not a wording problem.

6. **Case discussion:** [[2.4 Dyson - Case Handout]] — watch *"The Dyson Story"* and note (a) why the **Japan \$2,000 G-Force** was an MVP rather than a prototype, (b) why every manufacturer's refusal pushed him to **build his own factory** (1.8 supply chain), (c) which stage failed for the **Contra Rotator** given good reviews, and (d) whether a cancelled **\$2.5 billion** project is failure or portfolio management.

7. **Team logistics:** every member must be able to state the unit cost, the fit criterion, the verdict and the brand promise unaided — the exam-day FRQ is written **individually**, and 15% of the score is spent on exactly this project.
