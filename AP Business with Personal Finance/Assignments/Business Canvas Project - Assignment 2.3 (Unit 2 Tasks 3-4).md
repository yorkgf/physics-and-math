## Before You Start: The Framework

**Eight rules from the 2.3 lesson that this worksheet runs on:**

1. **Market research = systematically gathering, analyzing, and interpreting information about a market — customers *and* competitors — to guide decisions** (EK 2.3.A.1). Its job is to convert *"I think"* into *"the data shows."* If a piece of research cannot change a decision you were about to make, do not pay for it.
2. **Two data types, and you need both** (EK 2.3.A.2.i–ii). **Quantitative** = numbers you can count and graph (*"72% prefer X"* — answers *how many?*). **Qualitative** = words, images, observations (*"I like X because…"* — answers *why?*). **Quantitative identifies the pattern; qualitative explains it.**
3. **Three gates, in order** (EK 2.3.A.3.i–iii): **Desirability** (do customers actually want it — problem-solution fit) → **Feasibility** (can we build and deliver it with our resources, tech, time) → **Viability** (does it produce revenue greater than cost over time, sustainably). A product can be desirable but not feasible; feasible but not viable. **All three must pass, and desirability is checked first.**
4. **Secondary before primary** (EK 2.3.B.2–B.3). **Secondary = data that already exists** (government, commercial, academic, internal), collected by someone else for a different purpose. It is **cheap, fast, broad** — cheap reconnaissance that reveals PESTEL factors, market size, trends, customer segments, and competitor positioning. Its weaknesses: it may not answer *your* question, it may be out of date, and **you did not control how it was collected.**
5. **A business hypothesis is an assumption stated so it can be tested** (EK 2.3.C.2) — written **before** the data, in **variable → measure → expected number** form. It does three jobs: it **focuses** what to measure, makes results **interpretable** (supported / not supported), and **prevents post-hoc rationalization.** *A hypothesis is not a wish.*
6. **Match the method to the data the hypothesis needs** (EK 2.3.C.3–C.7). Don't use a survey to answer a *why* question. Don't use a focus group to count behavior. **A/B testing is the gold standard for "will they actually buy?"** because the answer comes from behavior, not from opinions about behavior.
7. **Honest data has two pillars** (EK 2.3.C.8): a **sample large enough and representative enough** for the population you actually care about, and **neutral question phrasing.** A biased question with 1,000 respondents gives you 1,000 useless answers; a neutral question with 15 carefully chosen respondents gives you 15 honest ones. **And always report the base** — 30 of 120 is 25%, not 15%.
8. **Research reduces uncertainty; it does not eliminate it** (EK 2.3.C.9). A survey showing 72% would buy does not mean 72% **will** buy. A 40-point gap over two Saturdays does not prove causation. **Each layer adds confidence; none guarantees the future.** Part of your grade in Section 4 is naming what your own test **cannot** claim.

**Chart selection is decided by the question, never by the look** (EK 2.3.D.1–D.3):

| If you want to show… | Use | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| Values compared across categories | **Bar chart** | x = categories, y = the measured value; compare by height — the workhorse of the exam |
| Total **and** composition within each category | **Stacked bar chart** | bar height = the total; segments = what's inside |
| Change over time | **Line graph** | **time goes on the x-axis** |
| Proportions of a whole | **Pie chart** | best with **3–5 slices**; more than 5 becomes unreadable |
| Relationship between two measured variables | *(scatter plot — not one of the four Appendix types)* | reveals clusters and dead zones; use it in Section 2, not in the Appendix |

> **Strong-answer method (all through this worksheet):**
> **State the finding as a number with its base → name the method and EK that produced it → say what decision changed because of it.**

> **Model — Theo's:** assumption = *buyers will pay more for a documented inspection*; testable form = *"If I add an inspection tag, at least 60% of bikes will sell within one week at $95."* Notice it has a **variable** (the tag), a **measure** (sell rate), a **number** (60%), and a **clock** (one week). >
> **Hypothesis ≠ finding.** The hypothesis is the assumption you feed **in**; the finding is the result handed **back** (*"Version B was preferred 70/30"*). Write the finding only in Section 4 — never in the same sentence as the hypothesis.

### 3B. Choose the method from the data the hypothesis needs (EK 2.3.C.3–C.7)

| Method | Data type | Best for | Weakness | Right for my H1 / H2? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Survey** | Quantitative | Preferences, satisfaction, demographics across a **large** group; fixed questions, little follow-up | People may not answer honestly (social desirability bias) | |
| **Interview (1-on-1)** | Qualitative | Deep motivation; open-ended **with follow-up** | Small sample, not statistically generalizable | |
| **Focus group** (6–10, moderated) | Qualitative | Attitudes, reactions, generating ideas; adds **group dynamics** | Loudest voice distorts; not a count | |
| **Observation** | Behavioral | What people **actually do**, in natural settings, without intervening | Tells you *what*, never *why* | |
| **Experiment** | Behavioral | Cause and effect — change one variable, hold the rest constant | Hard to control everything in the real world | |
| **A/B test** | Behavioral, real-world | **Will they actually buy?** Two versions, one variable, defined metric | Needs enough units; short windows mislead | |

> **Write the justification, not the choice.** For each hypothesis: *the data I need is ____ (quant / qual / behavior), therefore the method is ____, and the method I rejected is ____ because ____.*
>
> **Theo's method chain, in order:** secondary sweep (60 listings, free) → survey (200 local commuters, "how important is a documented inspection, 1–5") → **A/B test**: 17 bikes, **9 tagged at $95 vs. 8 untagged at $75**, two consecutive Saturdays, same location, same display style → **12 follow-up interviews** with tagged-bike buyers. Quantitative found the pattern; qualitative explained it.

### 3C. Build the instrument — and defang every question

Write the actual questions / the actual two versions you will show. Then audit them.

| # | Your question or version | Leading? (Y/N) | If Y — the neutral rewrite |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | | | |
| 2 | | | |
| 3 | | | |
| 4 | | | |

> **The biased → neutral model, from the lesson:**
>
> | **Biased (leading)** | **Neutral** |
> |---|---|
> | "Don't you think inspected bikes are safer?" | "How important is a documented inspection when buying a used bike?" |
> | "Wouldn't you prefer a bike with a warranty tag?" | "Which features matter most when choosing a used bike: price, inspection tag, brand, or appearance?" |

## SECTION 5: NEW COKE AUDIT — Three Mistakes, Applied to You

*AP Topic 2.3 · LO 2.3.A, 2.3.C · EK 2.3.A.2, 2.3.A.3, 2.3.C.8 · [[2.3 New Coke - Case Handout]]*

| # | The mistake Coke made | Where it could happen **to us** | What we did about it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Sample design flaw** (2.3.C.8) — a **sip ≠ a full can.** Tiny controlled samples didn't reflect how people actually drink Coke | | |
| 2 | **Missing the key variable** — focus groups asked about **taste**, never about **emotional attachment**. *"We are not selling a soft drink. We were selling a little tiny piece of people's lives."* | | |
| 3 | **Confirmation bias** (2.3.C.8) — the CEO's mind was already made up (*"it was new Coke or no Coke"*), so contradicting data was ignored | | |

> **The translation, in one line each:** your instrument may be measuring a **sip** when the decision is a **full can** (a 10-second glance at a listing vs. a parent's actual $95 purchase); your best variable may be the one **you never asked about** (stolen-bike fear was not in Theo's survey — the interviews found it); and if you already believe the answer, your questions will quietly be built to confirm it.
>
> **Mistake #3 is the one only you can fix.** Write it here, honestly:

## Important Notes

1. **Same business idea, same team, same customer.** You are not researching a new venture — you are testing the claims you committed to in the Unit 1 Final Canvas and profiled in 2.1. If the findings say the idea is wrong, **that is a success condition, not a failure.** Document the revision.

2. **Secondary first, always** (EK 2.3.B.3). Section 2 before Section 3 — cheap reconnaissance sharpens the questions you then pay to ask. Teams that survey before they read arrive at 100 questions nobody needed and a sample nobody can defend.

3. **Pre-set or it doesn't count.** Success criteria (3A), the neutral rewrite pass (3C), and the sample plan (3D) must exist **before** the first response. A criterion written after seeing the data is a story, not a test — this is precisely how confirmation bias destroyed New Coke at 200,000-person scale.

4. **A small honest n beats a large dishonest one.** Fifteen neutral questions aimed at the population you actually named will out-earn 1,000 leading ones sent to friends. State your bias risk and lower your claims to fit — **the limit sentence is what gets the mark** (EK 2.3.C.8, C.9).

5. **The Appendix chart is permanent.** It travels with your canvas into 2.4, 2.5, and Unit 3–4, and it is one of the three Appendix elements the CED grades (with the pitch and the income statement, both due later). Build it once, correctly labeled, from **real** findings — do not redraw it from a nicer number later.

6. **This is the bridge between 2.2 and 2.4.** Your 2.2 sales tactic is now a **testable preference hypothesis** (Section 3A, H2 is the natural home for it). Your findings here become the **MVP's success bar in 2.4** — and the value proposition you write there should quote a number from Section 4, not a feeling.

7. **Team logistics:** split fieldwork by member, but every member must be able to explain the two hypotheses, the method choice, and the verdict unaided — the exam-day FRQ is written **individually** about the project you all know.

8. **AP Exam connection:** FRQ 1 (Business Canvas Project Exam-Day Validation, **15% of your score, 25 minutes**) draws on LO **2.3.B** and **2.3.C** and asks how hypothesis testing informed your decisions. FRQ 3 (Business Concept Application) draws on **2.3.B, 2.3.D**. Section 4 and Section 6 are literally that question, pre-written.
