# 2.3 Market Research
### AP Business with Personal Finance · Unit 2

Lesson deck — **concepts + practice**

---

## Today's goals

By the end of this lesson you can:

1. Explain what market research is and how it uses **quantitative and qualitative data** to test **desirability, feasibility, and viability** — and why research continues after launch (LO 2.3.A)
2. Use **secondary-source research** to scan the competitive landscape cheaply (LO 2.3.B)
3. Design **primary-source research** — match the method to the hypothesis, keep data honest, and know that research does not guarantee a prediction (LO 2.3.C)
4. Select the right **data visualization** to communicate findings to stakeholders (LO 2.3.D)

**Running example continues:** Theo / Trued Cycles.

---

## Opening scenario

A buyer holds up his phone showing a **$75 bike** online and asks why Theo charges **$95**. Theo points at the **orange inspection tag**. The buyer shrugs and walks.

> That night Theo writes: *"An identical bike with a documented inspection tag sells for twenty dollars more, and faster."*

Today that's a **guess**. This topic turns guesses into **evidence**.

---

## What is market research?

**Market research** = the process of **collecting and analyzing information** to guide business decisions about customers, competitors, and the market.

> It replaces **"I think"** with **"the data shows."**

- Every business decision — pricing, product design, targeting — improves when it rests on evidence instead of instinct.

---

## EK 2.3.A.1 — Why it matters

Without research, a business is guessing:

| Guess | Evidence |
|---|---|
| "People want this product" | Survey: 72% of target customers say they'd buy |
| "Our price is right" | Competitor scan: median price is $89, ours is $95 |
| "Customers love us" | NPS score: 62 (promoters minus detractors) |

> **Research turns assumptions into testable claims.**

---

## Quantitative vs. qualitative data

| | **Quantitative** | **Qualitative** |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Data expressed in **numbers** | Data expressed in **words, images, or observations** |
| Answers | "How many?" "How much?" "How often?" | "Why?" "How do you feel?" "What was your experience?" |
| Example | 72% of 200 surveyed would buy | "I like the color but the seat is too hard" |

> **Quant = count it. Qual = understand it.**

---

## Quantitative vs. qualitative — Theo examples

**Quantitative:**
- 60 marketplace listings scraped — median price $82
- 7 out of 9 tagged bikes sold in one Saturday = **78%**

**Qualitative:**
- 12 one-on-one interviews → "The tag makes me trust it's not stolen"
- A buyer saying "I'd pay $10 more if I knew it was safe"

> **The numbers tell you WHAT happened. The words tell you WHY.**

---

## Desirable / Feasible / Viable

| Criterion | Question it answers | Theo example |
|---|---|---|
| **Desirable** | Does the customer actually want this? (problem-solution fit) | Do buyers value the inspection tag enough to pay more? |
| **Feasible** | Can we actually build / deliver it? (resources, tech, time) | Can Theo inspect and tag 15+ bikes per week with current tools? |
| **Viable** | Is there a profitable path? (revenue > cost, long-term) | Does the $20 premium cover the cost of inspection and tagging? |

> **All three must pass.** A desirable product you can't build is a dream. A feasible product nobody wants is a waste.

---

## Desirable / Feasible / Viable — the framework

Think of it as three gates:

1. **Desirable** — "Do they want it?" → research with customers
2. **Feasible** — "Can we do it?" → audit your resources and capacity
3. **Viable** — "Does the math work?" → model revenue vs. cost

> **Gate order matters:** desirability first (no point building something nobody wants), then feasibility, then viability.

---

## Desirability — problem-solution fit

**Desirable** = the product solves a **real customer problem** and the customer values the solution enough to choose it over alternatives.

- Test with **customer interviews, surveys, observation**
- Look for: willingness to pay, switching behavior, emotional response

> *Theo's desirability question: "Will buyers pay $95 for a bike with an inspection tag when a $75 bike without one is available?"*

---

## Feasibility — resources, tech, time

**Feasible** = the business has (or can acquire) the **resources, technology, and time** to deliver the product.

- Test with **internal audits, capacity analysis, supplier checks**
- Look for: bottlenecks, skill gaps, supply constraints

> *Theo's feasibility question: "Can I inspect, tag, and photograph 15 bikes per week with one helper and a Saturday schedule?"*

---

## Viability — profitable path

**Viable** = the business model produces **revenue greater than cost** over time, creating a sustainable path.

- Test with **financial modeling, break-even analysis, pricing research**
- Look for: margins, customer acquisition cost, repeat purchase rate

> *Theo's viability question: "Does the $20 premium on tagged bikes cover inspection labor, tag materials, and still leave profit?"*

---

## Real-world case — New Coke (1985)

**Coca-Cola** in the 1980s: owned 60% of the cola market after WWII → less than 24% by 1983.

**Pepsi Challenge:** blindfolded taste tests → people overwhelmingly chose Pepsi.

Coca-Cola's response: **Project Kansas** — create a sweeter Coke to beat Pepsi.

> They spent **$12 million** (today's dollars) testing with **200,000 people.** New Coke won every taste test. They launched it.

**Three months later, they pulled it.**

---

## New Coke — what the research showed

**Methods used:**
- **Sampling surveys** (quantitative): 200,000 people tasted New Coke vs Classic vs Pepsi
- **Focus groups** (qualitative): discussed the new flavor

**Results:**
- New Coke **"overwhelmingly beat both Pepsi and Coke Classic"**
- One bottling partner **threatened to sue** if they didn't release it

> The data said **launch it.** The data was **right about taste** — and **wrong about everything else.**

---

## New Coke — where the research failed

| What they measured | What they MISSED |
|---|---|
| Taste preference (sip test) | **Emotional attachment** to the original brand |
| "Do you prefer A or B?" | "How would you feel if Classic **disappeared forever?**" |
| Small samples in a controlled setting | Real-world full-can drinking experience |
| Quantitative data (how many prefer it) | Qualitative data on **why** people love Coke |

> **The research measured the product. It didn't measure the brand.**

---

## New Coke — three research mistakes

**1. Sample design flaw** (EK 2.3.C.8)
- "They only gave out a small amount — little samples didn't reflect how people drank Coke in real life"
- A sip ≠ a full can in your kitchen

**2. Missing the key variable**
- Focus groups asked about taste but NOT about emotional attachment
- "We are not selling a soft drink. We were selling a little tiny piece of people's lives."

**3. Confirmation bias** (EK 2.3.C.8)
- CEO Goizueta's mind was already made up: "it was new Coke or no Coke"
- He ignored data that contradicted his belief
- **Not neutral** → not honest research

---

## New Coke — the lesson

**8,000 angry calls per day** to Coca-Cola headquarters.
Demonstrations. Letter-writing campaigns. Media frenzy.

Coca-Cola brought back the original as **"Coca-Cola Classic"** within 3 months.

> **Research ≠ guaranteed prediction.** 200,000 data points and $12 million said "launch." The market said "no."

A former Coca-Cola executive: *"We did not know what we were selling. We are not selling a soft drink. We were selling a little tiny piece of people's lives."*

---

## Research after launch

Research does not stop when the product launches. **Post-launch research:**

| Purpose | What it does | Theo example |
|---|---|---|
| **Track changes** | Monitor shifts in customer preferences or market conditions | Buyer tastes shift from mountain bikes to city commuters |
| **Spot openings** | Identify new market opportunities or unmet needs | Parents start asking for kid-sized bikes with the same tag |
| **Build strategies** | Use data to adjust pricing, promotion, or positioning | Raise tagged-bike price to $100 after confirming demand holds |

> **A business that stops researching after launch is guessing again within months.**

---

## EK 2.3.A.4 — research is ongoing

> **Research is not a one-time event — it's a continuous cycle.**

The market moves. Customers change. Competitors react. What was true in January may be false by June.

- **Pre-launch:** test desirability, feasibility, viability
- **Post-launch:** track, spot, adjust
- **Ongoing:** the cycle never ends

# LO 2.3.B — Secondary-Source Research

*What's already out there — and how to use it cheaply.*

---

## What is secondary-source research?

**Secondary-source research** = collecting and analyzing data that **already exists** — gathered by someone else for a different purpose.

Sources include:

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Government** | Census data, labor statistics, trade reports |
| **Commercial** | Industry reports, market research firms (Statista, IBISWorld) |
| **Academic** | University studies, published journals, working papers |
| **Internal** | The company's own past sales records, customer databases |

> **Key advantage:** it's faster and cheaper than collecting new data yourself.

---

## EK 2.3.B.2 — what secondary research reveals

Secondary research helps a business understand the **landscape before** investing in primary research:

| What it reveals | How Theo uses it |
|---|---|
| **PESTEL factors** | Local regulations on reselling used bikes (legal), e-bike trend (technological) |
| **Market size** | How many used bikes sell per month in his city |
| **Trends** | Growing demand for inspected/refurbished bikes |
| **Customer segments** | Commuters vs. recreational riders vs. parents buying for kids |
| **Competitor analysis** | What other sellers charge, how they position, what they lack |

> **Secondary research = cheap reconnaissance.** It tells you what you're walking into.

---

## EK 2.3.B.3 — secondary is cost-effective

> **Secondary research is the cost-effective first step** — it saves time and money by using data that already exists.

- **Pro:** fast, cheap, broad coverage
- **Con:** may not answer your specific question; data may be outdated; you didn't control how it was collected

> **Always start with secondary.** Only move to primary when secondary leaves gaps.

---

## Theo's secondary move

Theo sweeps **60 marketplace listings** and records three fields per listing:

| Field | What he finds |
|---|---|
| **Price** | Range: $55–$110; median: $82 |
| **Days listed** | Cheaper bikes sit longer; tagged bikes sell faster |
| **Inspection documented?** | Only 8 of 60 mention any inspection |

> **Without spending a dollar on new data,** Theo now knows: most competitors don't document inspections, and there may be a price gap he can fill.

# LO 2.3.C — Primary-Source Research

*Generating new data from real customers.*

---

## What is primary-source research?

**Primary-source research** = collecting **new data directly** from customers, prospects, or the market — data that did not exist before you gathered it.

> **Secondary = what's already out there. Primary = what you go get yourself.**

- Surveys, interviews, focus groups, experiments, observations
- More expensive and time-consuming than secondary
- But it answers **your specific question** with **your specific audience**

---

## Business hypothesis

> **Business hypothesis** = an assumption about a customer, product, or market, **stated so it can be tested.**

| Component | Example (Theo) |
|---|---|
| **Assumption** | Buyers will pay more for a bike with a documented inspection tag |
| **Testable form** | "If I add an inspection tag, at least 60% of bikes will sell within one week at $95" |

> **A hypothesis is not a wish.** It must specify what you expect and how you'll know if you're right or wrong.

---

## EK 2.3.C.2 — why hypotheses matter

Without a hypothesis, research is just collecting data with no direction.

- A **hypothesis focuses** your research: you know what to measure
- It makes results **interpretable**: you can say "supported" or "not supported"
- It prevents **post-hoc rationalization**: deciding after the fact what the data "means"

> **Write the hypothesis before you collect the data.** Not after.

---

## Research methods menu

| Method | Type of data | Best for | Theo example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Survey** | Quantitative | Measuring opinions, preferences across a large group | Online survey: "Would you pay more for an inspected bike?" |
| **Focus group** | Qualitative | Exploring attitudes, generating ideas in a group setting | 6 buyers discuss what "inspection" means to them |
| **Interview** | Qualitative | Deep, one-on-one understanding of motivations | 12 follow-up interviews after the A/B test |
| **Experiment / Observation** | Behavioral | Watching what people actually do (not just what they say) | Count how many people pick up a tagged vs. untagged bike |
| **A/B testing** | Behavioral (real-world) | Comparing two versions to see which performs better | 9 tagged vs. 8 untagged bikes over two Saturdays |

---

## EK 2.3.C.3 — match the method to the data

> **The hypothesis tells you what data you need. The method must deliver that type of data.**

| If your hypothesis asks… | You need… | Use… |
|---|---|---|
| "What do customers prefer?" | Quantitative preference data | **Survey** |
| "Why do customers feel that way?" | Qualitative depth | **Interview or focus group** |
| "Will customers actually buy?" | Behavioral data | **Experiment, observation, A/B test** |

> **Don't use a survey to answer a "why" question. Don't use a focus group to count behavior.**

---

## EK 2.3.C.4 — surveys

**Survey** = a set of questions administered to a sample of respondents to collect **quantitative (or sometimes qualitative) data.**

- Can reach **large numbers** quickly (online tools, paper forms)
- Best for: preferences, satisfaction, demographics, likelihood to buy
- Weakness: people may not answer honestly (social desirability bias)

> *Theo's survey: "On a scale of 1–5, how important is a documented inspection when buying a used bike?" — sent to 200 local commuters.*

---

## EK 2.3.C.5 — focus groups and interviews

**Focus group** = a moderated discussion with **6–10 participants** to explore attitudes and generate qualitative insights.

**Interview** = a one-on-one conversation to get **deep, detailed** qualitative responses.

- Best for: understanding **motivations, feelings, reasoning**
- Weakness: small sample, not statistically generalizable
- Focus groups add **group dynamics** (participants react to each other)

> *Theo's 12 one-on-one interviews revealed: "The tag makes me trust it's not stolen" — a motivation the survey never surfaced.*

---

## EK 2.3.C.6 — experiments and observations

**Experiment** = manipulating one variable while holding others constant to measure cause and effect.

**Observation** = watching and recording behavior without intervening.

- Best for: **what people actually do** (vs. what they say they'll do)
- Weakness: hard to control all variables in a real-world setting

> *Theo's observation: count how many shoppers pick up, inspect, or ask about tagged vs. untagged bikes on the sales floor.*

---

## EK 2.3.C.7 — A/B testing

**A/B testing** = comparing **two versions** (A and B) in a real-world setting to see which performs better on a defined metric.

- One variable changes; everything else stays the same
- Results are **behavioral** — based on what customers actually do
- Common in: pricing, web design, email subject lines, product placement

> **A/B testing is the gold standard for "will they actually buy?" questions.**

---

## Theo's A/B test

**Setup:**
- 17 bikes total: **9 tagged at $95**, **8 untagged at $75**
- Two consecutive Saturdays, same location, same display style

**Results:**

| Group | Sold | Total | Sell rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tagged ($95)** | 7 | 9 | **78%** |
| **Untagged ($75)** | 3 | 8 | **38%** |

> The tag — not the price — appears to drive the sale. But **one test is not proof.** Theo needs more data.

---

## A/B math

### Tagged: 7 / 9 = 77.8% ≈ **78%**

### Untagged: 3 / 8 = 37.5% ≈ **38%**

> A **40-percentage-point gap** in a small sample. Suggestive, not conclusive — but strong enough to justify a follow-up.

---

## Follow-up: qualitative explains quantitative

The A/B test told Theo **what** happened (tagged bikes sold more). The interviews told him **why.**

**12 one-on-one interviews with buyers who purchased tagged bikes:**

| Theme | Frequency |
|---|---|
| "The tag makes me trust it's not stolen" | 8 of 12 |
| "I feel safer knowing someone checked the brakes" | 6 of 12 |
| "It looks more professional" | 4 of 12 |

> **Quantitative data identifies the pattern. Qualitative data explains it.** You need both.

---

## Keeping data honest

Research is only useful if the data is **reliable.** Two key safeguards:

### 1. Large, representative sample
- **Large enough** to reduce the effect of random variation
- **Representative** of the population you care about (not just who's convenient)

### 2. Neutral phrasing
- Questions must not **lead** the respondent toward a particular answer

---

## Biased vs. neutral question phrasing

| **Biased** (leading) | **Neutral** |
|---|---|
| "Don't you think inspected bikes are safer?" | "How important is a documented inspection when buying a used bike?" |
| "Wouldn't you prefer a bike with a warranty tag?" | "Which features matter most when choosing a used bike: price, inspection tag, brand, or appearance?" |
| "Our bikes are the best value — agree?" | "How would you rate the value of this bike relative to its price?" |

> **Leading questions produce useless data.** The answer was baked into the question.

---

## EK 2.3.C.8 — sample size and neutrality

> **The two pillars of honest data:**

| Pillar | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Sample size & representativeness** | Enough respondents, drawn from the population you're studying — not just your friends |
| **Neutral phrasing** | Questions that don't hint at the "right" answer |

- A biased question with 1,000 respondents gives you **1,000 useless answers**
- A neutral question with 15 carefully chosen respondents gives you **15 honest answers**

> **Quality of the question matters more than quantity of responses.**

---

## EK 2.3.C.9 — research does not guarantee prediction

> **Research reduces uncertainty — it does not eliminate it.**

- A survey showing 72% would buy does not mean 72% **will** buy
- An A/B test showing a 40-point gap in two Saturdays does not prove causation
- Market conditions change, competitors react, customer moods shift

> **Research improves the odds. It does not promise outcomes.**

---

## The research confidence ladder

| Level | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| **No research** | Pure guess |
| **Secondary only** | Landscape understanding — what's already known |
| **Secondary + survey** | Quantitative preferences from your target market |
| **Secondary + survey + interviews** | Numbers + motivations |
| **Secondary + survey + interviews + A/B test** | Numbers + motivations + real-world behavioral data |

> **Each layer adds confidence. None guarantees the future.**

# LO 2.3.D — Data Visualization

*Turning numbers into patterns stakeholders can grasp.*

---

## Why visualize data?

> **Data visualizations turn raw data into patterns that stakeholders can grasp quickly.**

- A table of 60 numbers takes effort to read
- A chart of those 60 numbers reveals a pattern in seconds
- The **right chart** for the **right data** makes your case persuasive

> **The goal is not decoration — it's communication.**

---

## EK 2.3.D.1 — four chart types

| Chart type | Best for | Visual |
|---|---|---|
| **Bar chart** | Comparing values across categories | Bars side by side |
| **Stacked bar chart** | Showing total + subcategories within each bar | Bars divided into colored segments |
| **Line chart** | Showing trend over time | Points connected by a line |
| **Pie chart** | Showing part-to-whole (proportions of a total) | Slices of a circle |

> **Pick the chart that best shows your pattern.** Don't pick a pie chart just because it looks nice.

---

## Bar chart — compare discrete categories

**Use when:** you want to compare a single value across categories.

**Example:** Average sale price of bikes by seller type (private, shop, certified reseller).

| Seller type | Avg. price |
|---|---|
| Private | $68 |
| Shop | $85 |
| Certified reseller | $98 |

> A bar chart makes the price gap across seller types instantly visible.

---

## Stacked bar chart — total + parts

**Use when:** you want to show both the **total** and the **composition** within each category.

**Example:** Monthly bike sales by type (tagged vs. untagged).

| Month | Tagged | Untagged | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| January | 12 | 8 | 20 |
| February | 15 | 5 | 20 |
| March | 18 | 4 | 22 |

> The bar height shows the total; the segments show what's inside.

---

## Line chart — trend over time

**Use when:** you want to show how a value **changes over time.**

**Example:** Average selling price of inspected bikes over 6 months.

> If the line slopes upward, the price is rising. If it dips, something changed. **Time goes on the x-axis.**

---

## Pie chart — part-to-whole

**Use when:** you want to show how **parts make up a whole** (proportions, percentages).

**Example:** What share of Theo's sales come from tagged vs. untagged bikes?

| Category | Share |
|---|---|
| Tagged | 70% |
| Untagged | 30% |

> **Limitation:** pie charts work best with **few categories** (3–5). More than that and the slices become hard to read.

---

## EK 2.3.D.2 — pick the right chart

> **The question determines the chart.**

| If you want to show… | Use… |
|---|---|
| Compare values across categories | **Bar chart** |
| Total + composition within each category | **Stacked bar chart** |
| Change over time | **Line chart** |
| Proportions of a whole | **Pie chart** |

> **Don't default to the prettiest chart.** Default to the one that makes your pattern **obvious.**

---

## EK 2.3.D.3 — bar charts in depth

Bar charts are the **workhorse** of data visualization on the AP exam.

- **Horizontal axis (x):** categories (seller type, product, month)
- **Vertical axis (y):** the measured value (price, quantity, percentage)
- **Each bar:** one category's value
- **Compare by height:** taller bar = higher value

> **If the exam asks "which chart best shows…" and the answer involves comparing categories, start with bar chart.**

---

## Scatter plot — Theo's marketplace data

**Scatter plot** = plots individual data points on two axes to reveal **relationships.**

**Theo's 60 marketplace listings: price vs. days listed**

| Pattern | What it means |
|---|---|
| Points cluster at low prices + low days listed | Cheap bikes sell fast |
| Points cluster at high prices + high days listed | Expensive bikes sit unsold |
| **Dead zone above $85** | Almost no bikes priced above $85 sold within 14 days |

> The scatter plot reveals a **price ceiling** Theo didn't know existed. That's the power of visualization.

---

## Exam points to remember

- **Market research** = collect and analyze information to guide decisions
- **Quantitative** = numbers ("how many?"); **Qualitative** = words ("why?")
- **Desirable** (want it?), **Feasible** (can we?), **Viable** (profitable?) — all three must pass
- **Research continues after launch** — track, spot, adjust
- **Secondary** = existing data (cheap, fast); **Primary** = new data (specific, expensive)
- **Hypothesis** = testable assumption, written before collecting data
- **Match the method to the data type** the hypothesis needs
- **Honest data** = representative sample + neutral phrasing
- **Research reduces uncertainty — it does not guarantee prediction** (New Coke: 200,000 data points, $12M, still failed)
- **New Coke lesson:** research measured the product (taste) but missed the brand (emotional attachment) — know what you're NOT measuring
- **Pick the chart that shows your pattern**, not the prettiest one

---

## Stimulus: Two Bucket Detail

**Tomas and Bree Herrera** run **Two Bucket Detail**, a mobile car-detailing service. They offer two bundles:

| Bundle | Includes | Price |
|---|---|---|
| **Inside Out** | Interior vacuum, wipe-down, exterior wash | $65 |
| **Showroom** | Everything in Inside Out + wax, tire shine, window polish | $110 |

They want to know which bundle to promote more heavily. Tomas writes: *"Customers who try Showroom once will prefer it over Inside Out on their next visit."*

They run an **A/B test over 4 weekends** — alternating which bundle gets the banner on their booking page. They also conduct **10 follow-up interviews** with customers who booked Showroom.

---

## Q1 — What is Tomas's sentence?

Tomas's statement — *"Customers who try Showroom once will prefer it over Inside Out on their next visit"* — is best described as which?

- A. A market research finding
- B. A business hypothesis
- C. A customer segment
- D. A market observation

---

## Q1 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** It is an **assumption about customer behavior** (prefer Showroom after trying it) that is **stated so it can be tested** (they can track repeat bookings by bundle). That is the CED's definition of a business hypothesis.

**Trap: A** — a "finding" comes **after** data collection. Tomas's sentence was written **before** the test — it's a prediction, not a result.

> **A hypothesis = an assumption, testable, stated before the data.**

---

## Q2 — What is the 4-weekend comparison?

The 4-weekend test — alternating which bundle gets the banner — is best described as which?

- A. A focus group
- B. A market observation
- C. An A/B test
- D. A customer survey

---

## Q2 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** An A/B test compares **two versions** (A and B) in a real-world setting to see which performs better. Here, Version A = Inside Out banner, Version B = Showroom banner; the metric = which bundle customers book.

**Trap: B** — observation means **watching without intervening.** Tomas and Bree are actively changing the banner — that's an experiment, not passive observation.

> **A/B test = change one variable, measure the difference.**

---

## Q3 — What are Bree's interviews?

Bree's 10 follow-up interviews with Showroom customers are best described as which?

- A. Quantitative data collected through a survey
- B. Behavioral data from an experiment
- C. Qualitative data collected through follow-up questions
- D. A method that eliminates bias from the study

---

## Q3 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** One-on-one interviews that ask customers to explain their experience produce **qualitative data** (words, not numbers). "Follow-up questions" is the key — the interviews dig into the **why** behind the A/B test results.

**Trap: A** — surveys collect structured, often quantitative data from many respondents. Interviews collect **qualitative depth** from fewer people.

**Trap: D** — interviews are valuable but they **do not eliminate bias.** A small sample and the interviewer's presence can both introduce bias.

> **Interviews explain the numbers. They don't sterilize them.**

---

## Q4 — Why research secondary first?

Before launching their A/B test and interviews, Tomas reviews local competitors' websites and pricing. Why is starting with **secondary research** a logical first step?

- A. It guarantees the most accurate data for their specific question
- B. It lets them understand the competitive landscape quickly and cheaply
- C. It is the only way to collect qualitative data
- D. It always provides data about their own customers

---

## Q4 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Secondary research is **cost-effective** — it uses data that already exists to help a business understand the landscape **before** investing in primary research. Tomas can see competitors' prices and bundles without spending a dollar.

**Trap: A** — secondary data may be **outdated or not specific** to Tomas's exact question. It's a starting point, not a guarantee.

**Trap: D** — secondary data is about the **market and competitors**, not necessarily about Tomas's own customers. That's what primary research is for.

> **Secondary first = cheap reconnaissance. Then fill the gaps with primary.**

---

## Q5 — Reading a survey table

**Fernline Nursery** surveys 200 customers about preferred plant types:

| Response | Count |
|---|---|
| Succulents | 120 |
| Ferns | 50 |
| Orchids | 30 |

A manager says: *"30 out of our orchid-loving customers represent 15% of our respondents."*

Is the manager's math correct?

- A. Yes — 30 out of 200 = 15%
- B. No — 30 out of 120 = 25%
- C. No — 30 out of 50 = 60%
- D. Yes — but only if orchid buyers are the majority

---

## Q5 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** The **base** is the total number of respondents: **200.** 30 / 200 = 0.15 = **15%.** The manager's math is correct.

**The trap in the trap:** B calculates 30/120 = 25%, which uses the **wrong base** (succulent lovers, not total respondents). When the exam gives you a percentage, always **check what the base is.**

> **Always check the BASE.** A percentage is only as honest as the denominator.

## Q6 — Which chart for the pottery studio?

A pottery studio tracks monthly revenue from three sources: **classes, studio rentals, and retail sales.** The owner wants to show the **total monthly revenue** and **how much each source contributes.** Which chart type is best?

- A. Line chart
- B. Pie chart
- C. Stacked bar chart
- D. Bar chart (grouped)

---

## Q6 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** A **stacked bar chart** shows both the **total** (bar height) and the **composition** (colored segments within each bar) — exactly what the owner needs: total revenue per month + how much each source contributes.

**Trap: A** — a line chart shows **trend over time** for a single variable. It doesn't show composition within each month.

**Trap: D** — a grouped bar chart compares categories side by side, but makes it harder to see the **total** at a glance.

> **Stacked bar = total + parts.** That's the exact match.

## Q7 — Interpret the scatter plot

Theo plots 60 marketplace listings: **x-axis = price, y-axis = days listed.** Most points above $85 cluster at **14+ days listed.** Most points below $75 cluster at **under 5 days.** What does this pattern suggest?

- A. Higher-priced bikes always sell faster
- B. There may be a price ceiling above which bikes sit unsold
- C. Price has no effect on how quickly bikes sell
- D. All bikes sell within two weeks regardless of price

---

## Q7 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** The data shows that bikes priced **above $85 tend to sit for 14+ days**, while bikes **below $75 sell in under 5 days.** This suggests a **price ceiling** — a point above which demand drops sharply.

**Trap: A** — says the **opposite** of what the data shows. Higher prices are associated with **slower** sales, not faster.

> **Read the axes. Read the cluster. Don't assume the direction.**

## Q8 — Research reduces uncertainty

Which best explains why market research **does not guarantee** a successful business decision?

- A. Research is too expensive to be worthwhile
- B. Customer preferences, market conditions, and competitor actions can change after the research is conducted
- C. Only large corporations can conduct reliable research
- D. Quantitative data is always more reliable than qualitative data

---

## Q8 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Research captures a **snapshot** of the market at a point in time. Conditions shift — customers change preferences, competitors react, external factors (PESTEL) evolve. Research **reduces uncertainty** but cannot predict the future with certainty.

**Trap: A** — research has costs, but secondary research is **cheap** and even small-scale primary research can be affordable. Cost does not mean research is worthless.

> **Research improves the odds. It does not promise outcomes.**

## Wrap-up

- **Market research** = collect and analyze info to guide decisions — it replaces "I think" with "the data shows"
- **Quantitative** = numbers; **Qualitative** = words and explanations — you need both
- **Desirable / Feasible / Viable** — all three gates must pass
- **Secondary research** = existing data (cheap, fast); **Primary research** = new data (specific, targeted)
- **Hypothesis** = testable assumption written before collecting data
- **Match the method to the data** the hypothesis needs — surveys for quant, interviews for qual, A/B for behavior
- **Honest data** = representative sample + neutral phrasing — check the base
- **Research reduces uncertainty** — it does not guarantee prediction
- **Pick the chart** that shows your pattern: bar (compare), stacked bar (total + parts), line (trend), pie (proportions)
