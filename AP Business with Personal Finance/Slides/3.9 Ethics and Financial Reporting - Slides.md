# Part 1: The temptations
### LO 3.9.A — Why someone would do it

---

## The six practices the CED names (3.9.A.1)

**3.9.A.1** **Unethical practices in finance** include:

| Practice | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Misuse of funds** | Using company money for unauthorized purposes |
| **Tax evasion** | Deliberately underreporting income or overstating deductions |
| **Embezzlement** | Stealing money entrusted to you |
| **Bribery** | Offering something of value to influence a decision |
| **Lack of transparency** | Hiding relevant financial information from stakeholders |
| **Fraud** | Deliberately misrepresenting data — **including falsifying information on financial statements** |

> **Six items, all testable. Five are acts; one — lack of transparency — is an omission. Remember that split.**

---

## The access problem (3.9.A.2)

**3.9.A.2** **Access to large sums of a business's cash creates a situation** where individuals may seek to:

- **embezzle**
- **misuse funds**
- **falsify information**

**for personal gain.**

> **The EK is about *opportunity*, not character.** DK Coffee Lab's bookkeeper does not need to be a bad person — she needs to be the only person who reconciles the account.

---

## Who is exposed, and to what

| Role at DK Coffee Lab | Access | Risk created |
|---|---|---|
| **Bookkeeper** | Records every transaction | Embezzlement; falsified entries |
| **Purchasing manager** | Chooses suppliers | Bribery; kickbacks |
| **CFO** | Prepares statements for the bank | Fraud; lack of transparency |
| **Owner** | Sets the numbers reported | Tax evasion |

> **Control design follows from this table: whoever records a transaction should not also be the one who approves or reconciles it.**

---

## The three motives to misstate (3.9.A.3)

**3.9.A.3** A business or its financial managers may be able to:

| Motive | How the misstatement helps |
|---|---|
| **Influence stock prices** | Better numbers → higher share price → valuable to owners holding stock |
| **Secure better terms on loans** | Healthier-looking financials → lower rate, larger loan (3.5.C.7) |
| **Avoid taxes** | Lower reported profit → lower tax bill (3.6.A.8) |

by **portraying its financial situation to stakeholders and to the government as better or worse than it really is.**

> **Note "or worse."** Understating is also fraud — a business may look weak to win a subsidy, defer a payment, or invite a takeover. **Direction does not decide ethics; falsification does.**

---

## Why "better *or worse*" matters

| Direction | Who is deceived | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Overstate profit** | Investors, lenders | Higher share price, better loan terms |
| **Understate profit** | Government | Lower taxes |
| **Understate assets/income** | Regulators, competitors, buyers | Sympathy, subsidies, a cheaper acquisition price |

> **3.9.A.3 lists *three* motives: stock prices, loan terms, taxes. Any of them can be served by moving the numbers in *either* direction. Name the motive, not the direction.**

# Part 2: The guardrails
### LO 3.9.B — Law and professional codes

---

## The law (3.9.B.1)

**3.9.B.1** **Misusing funds, tax evasion, embezzlement, bribery, and fraud are illegal in most countries**, but **laws and punishments vary from country to country**.

> **Two halves, both testable: (1) these are illegal in *most* countries — not all; (2) penalties *vary* by country. An answer that claims universal identical treatment is wrong.**

---

## Audit and market regulation in the U.S. (3.9.B.2)

**3.9.B.2** Under **U.S. law**, **publicly held corporations** are required to **submit financial records annually for auditing by independent accounting firms**.

**Financial market regulations** are designed to:

- ensure **investors have access to accurate information**
- **protect investors from fraud**

| Term | Role |
|---|---|
| **Auditing** | The **process of reviewing financial records** — performed by **independent** firms |
| **Independent** | The auditor must not be the company being audited |
| **Financial market regulations** | Rules ensuring accurate disclosure and investor protection |

> **"Independent" is the load-bearing word. An audit performed by the company's own accountants is not an audit — it is a review by the interested party.**

---

## Three layers of protection

| Layer | Who imposes it | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Law** | Government | Makes the practices illegal; mandates annual audits of public corporations |
| **Financial market regulations** | Regulators | Ensure accurate information reaches investors; protect against fraud |
| **Professional ethics codes** | Professional organizations | Bind members to honesty, integrity, transparency, objectivity, confidentiality |

> **Law punishes after the fact; regulation requires disclosure before it; professional codes bind the individual. Three different mechanisms, one shared goal.**

---

## Professional ethics codes (3.9.B.3)

**3.9.B.3** **Professional organizations** for accountants and financial managers **maintain professional ethics codes for their members**. Such codes emphasize:

| Value | What it demands |
|---|---|
| **Honesty** | Truthful in all professional communications |
| **Integrity** | Ethical conduct even under pressure |
| **Transparency** | Open disclosure of relevant financial information |
| **Objectivity** | Judgment not compromised by bias or pressure |
| **Confidentiality** | Protection of sensitive client information |

> **Five words — learn them as a set. Questions ask "which value does this scenario violate?" and the answer is always one of these five.**

---

## The professional codes in practice

| Scenario at DK Coffee Lab | Value at stake |
|---|---|
| The CFO says "just move this expense to next quarter" | **Integrity**, **objectivity** |
| A bookkeeper posts an entry she knows is wrong to keep her job | **Objectivity** (pressure compromised judgment) |
| Management omits a lawsuit from the notes to the statements | **Transparency**, **honesty** |
| An accountant shares a client's payroll data with a friend | **Confidentiality** |

> **Notice: none of these requires an outright theft. Codes of conduct govern the *small* compromises, which is where most fraud begins.**

---

## Internal controls (3.9.B.4)

**3.9.B.4** **Many businesses develop internal mechanisms** to prevent unethical behavior:

| Mechanism | What it prevents |
|---|---|
| **Codes of conduct** | Ambiguity — staff know what is expected |
| **Audit requirements** | Undetected misstatement |
| **Cash-handling processes** | Embezzlement — separation of duties, dual signatures, reconciliations |

> **Internal controls are *preventive*, not punitive. They are the business's own design, not a legal requirement (that is 3.9.B.2).**

---

## Ethics codes vs. codes of conduct — the distinction

| | **Professional ethics codes** | **Internal codes of conduct** |
|---|---|---|
| **Who writes them** | **Professional organizations** | **Individual businesses** |
| **Who they bind** | **All members of the profession** | **That business's employees** |
| **Scope** | Industry-wide standards | Company-specific rules |
| **EK** | 3.9.B.3 | 3.9.B.4 |

> **Scale is the tell: profession-wide vs. one company. Same word "code," different author and different reach.**

---

## 🔑 Exam points to remember — Ethics and financial reporting

| # | Point |
|---|-------|
| 1 | **Unethical practices** in finance: **misuse of funds, tax evasion, embezzlement, bribery, lack of transparency, and fraud** (including falsifying statements) |
| 2 | **Access to large sums of cash** creates the opportunity to embezzle, misuse funds, or falsify information **for personal gain** |
| 3 | Businesses or managers may misstate to **influence stock prices, secure better loan terms, or avoid taxes** — portraying the situation as **better or worse** than it is |
| 4 | These practices are **illegal in most countries**, but **laws and punishments vary** |
| 5 | Under **U.S. law**, **publicly held corporations** must submit records **annually for auditing by independent accounting firms** |
| 6 | **Financial market regulations** ensure investors have **accurate information** and are **protected from fraud** |
| 7 | **Professional ethics codes** emphasize **honesty, integrity, transparency, objectivity, and confidentiality** |
| 8 | **Internal mechanisms** — **codes of conduct, audit requirements, cash-handling processes** — prevent unethical behavior |

---

## Three exam tells — ethics

**Tell 1 — "Act or omission?"** Taking money, bribing, or falsifying a number = a **commission** (misuse, embezzlement, bribery, fraud). Hiding something relevant = **lack of transparency**. Both are on the CED's list; only one involves a false statement.

**Tell 2 — "Theft or deception?"** Embezzlement is **stealing money you have access to**. Fraud is **misrepresenting the financial data**. A bookkeeper who takes $5,000 from the register is embezzling; a CFO who records a fake $500,000 sale is committing fraud.

**Tell 3 — "External rule or internal rule?"** Audits and financial market regulations come from **law and regulators**. Codes of conduct, audit requirements, and cash-handling processes are the **business's own**. Professional ethics codes come from **professional organizations**, not from any individual company.

## Practice — Stimulus

At **DK Coffee Lab**, the bookkeeper — who both records transactions and reconciles the bank account — has:

- transferred **$47,000** of company cash to a personal account over 14 months
- recorded **$85,000** of fictitious wholesale sales to make the year look profitable before a loan application
- accepted **$3,000** from a supplier to keep using that supplier
- omitted a **$120,000** lawsuit from the notes to the financial statements

The company is privately held and has **no external audit**.

---

## Practice — Q1

> Which term best describes the bookkeeper's $47,000 transfer?

> A. Fraud
> B. Embezzlement
> C. Lack of transparency
> D. Tax evasion

---

## Practice — Q1 answer

**Answer: B.**

**3.9.A.1–A.2**: **embezzlement** is stealing money from an organization by someone **entrusted with access to its funds**. The bookkeeper had legitimate access and diverted the cash.

**Traps:**
- **A** — fraud is **misrepresenting financial data**; theft of cash is a different act (though both can occur together)
- **C** — lack of transparency is **hiding information**, not taking money
- **D** — tax evasion is underreporting income or overstating deductions to reduce taxes owed

> **Access + taking = embezzlement. Records + lying = fraud.**

---

## Practice — Q2

> The $85,000 of fictitious sales was recorded **before a loan application**. Which motive from 3.9.A.3 does this illustrate?

> A. Avoiding taxes
> B. Securing better terms on loans
> C. Reducing the stock price
> D. Increasing transparency

---

## Practice — Q2 answer

**Answer: B.**

**3.9.A.3** names three motives — **influence stock prices**, **secure better terms on loans**, and **avoid taxes**. Inflating revenue ahead of a loan application targets the second, and it exploits exactly the lender behaviour in 3.5.C.7 (lower perceived risk → better terms).

**Traps:**
- **A** — inflating revenue *increases* taxable profit (3.6.A.8); it works against this motive
- **C** — nothing about a private company's loan application relates to share price
- **D** — fictitious sales are the opposite of transparency

> **Match the misstatement's *direction* to the motive it serves. Higher revenue cannot serve tax avoidance.**

---

## Practice — Q3

> The supplier payment of $3,000, and the omission of the $120,000 lawsuit, are best described respectively as:

> A. Bribery and lack of transparency
> B. Embezzlement and fraud
> C. Fraud and bribery
> D. Tax evasion and lack of transparency

---

## Practice — Q3 answer

**Answer: A.**

**Bribery** = offering something of value to influence a decision (3.9.A.1) — here the supplier paid to keep the contract. **Lack of transparency** = **hiding** relevant financial information from stakeholders; omitting a material lawsuit is an omission, not a false entry.

**Traps:**
- **B** — no cash was taken from the company for personal use, and the lawsuit was omitted rather than falsified
- **C** — reversed
- **D** — no tax motive appears in either act

> **Omission = transparency. Active falsehood = fraud. Something of value for a decision = bribery.**

---

## Practice — Q4

> DK Coffee Lab is **privately held**. Which statement about the legal and regulatory requirements it faces is correct under 3.9.B?

> A. It must submit records annually for audit by an independent accounting firm, because all businesses must
> B. The annual independent audit requirement under U.S. law applies to **publicly held** corporations; DK may still adopt internal mechanisms such as codes of conduct, audit requirements, and cash-handling processes
> C. No law applies to private companies, so the conduct described is legal
> D. Professional ethics codes legally bind every employee of every business

---

## Practice — Q4 answer

**Answer: B.**

**3.9.B.2** ties the annual independent-audit requirement to **publicly held corporations**. **3.9.B.4** separately notes that **many businesses** — public or not — develop internal mechanisms such as **codes of conduct, audit requirements, and cash-handling processes**. And **3.9.B.1** establishes that misuse of funds, embezzlement, bribery, and fraud are **illegal in most countries** regardless of corporate form.

**Traps:**
- **A** — the audit mandate is specific to publicly held corporations
- **C** — private status does not legalise theft, bribery, or fraud
- **D** — professional ethics codes bind **members of the professional organization**, not every employee

> **Separate three things: what the law requires, what regulators mandate, and what a business chooses to adopt.**

---

## Trap patterns to watch

| # | Trap type | Where it appears |
|---|-----------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Embezzlement and fraud as synonyms** — theft vs. deception | Q1 |
| 2 | **Omission treated as fraud** — hiding is *lack of transparency* | Q3 |
| 3 | **Direction of misstatement ignored** — inflating revenue cannot serve tax avoidance | Q2 |
| 4 | **Audit requirement applied to all businesses** — it targets publicly held corporations | Q4 |
| 5 | **"Legal because private"** — the practices are illegal in most countries | Q4 |
| 6 | **Ethics codes as law** — professional codes bind members, not employees generally | Q4 |
| 7 | **Tax evasion vs. tax avoidance** — evasion is illegal; avoidance (deductions, credits) is legal | throughout |
| 8 | **Professional codes vs. internal codes of conduct** — professional organization vs. one company | 3.9.B.3 / B.4 |
| 9 | **Internal controls as punishment** — they are **preventive** by design | 3.9.B.4 |

## Wrap-up — 3.9 Ethics and Financial Reporting

**What we covered:**
- **Unethical practices**: **misuse of funds, tax evasion, embezzlement, bribery, lack of transparency, and fraud** — including falsifying financial statements
- **Access to large cash sums** creates the *opportunity* — the incentive is **personal gain**
- Businesses misstate to **influence stock prices, secure better loan terms, or avoid taxes**, by portraying results as **better or worse** than reality
- These practices are **illegal in most countries**, but **laws and punishments vary**
- In the U.S., **publicly held corporations** must submit records **annually for auditing by independent accounting firms**; **financial market regulations** ensure accurate information and protect investors
- **Professional ethics codes** emphasize **honesty, integrity, transparency, objectivity, confidentiality**
- Businesses use **codes of conduct, audit requirements, and cash-handling processes** as preventive internal mechanisms
- **Separation of duties** — the person who records should not be the person who reconciles — is the structural fix for 3.9.A.2
