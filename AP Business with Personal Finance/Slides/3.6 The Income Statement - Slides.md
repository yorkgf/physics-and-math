# Part 1: What the statement is
### LO 3.6.A — Components and categories

---

## The income statement, defined (3.6.A.1)

**3.6.A.1** An **income statement** — also called a **statement of profit and loss** — is a financial statement that:

- compares a business's **total revenue to its total costs**
- **over a time period**
- to determine the business's **net profit or loss**

It **typically provides multiple periods** (years, quarters, or months) of data for comparison.

> **Two phrases carry the EK: "over a time period" (not a point in time) and "multiple periods for comparison."** A single-period statement cannot show a trend.

---

## Three categories, plus three named items (3.6.A.2)

**3.6.A.2** An income statement organizes information into **three major categories**:

| Category | What it holds |
|---|---|
| **Revenue** | Money generated by core activities |
| **Cost of goods sold (COGS)** | Direct cost of producing what was sold |
| **Operating expenses** | Indirect costs of running the business |

And it **also identifies**: **interest**, **taxes**, and **nonrecurring expenses**.

> **Three categories, three named items. Nonrecurring expenses (a one-time lawsuit settlement, a natural-disaster loss) are shown separately *because* they are not expected to repeat.**

---

## Revenue (3.6.A.3)

**3.6.A.3** For income statement purposes, **revenue** is the income generated by a business's **core activities** — sales of goods and services.

**DK Coffee Lab, year 1:**

| Source | Amount |
|---|---|
| Retail drink and food sales | $296,000 |
| Wholesale beans to three cafés | $46,000 |
| **Total revenue** | **$342,000** |

> **"Core activities" is doing real work in this EK.** Interest earned on idle cash is not core revenue — and the CED's exclusion statement puts non-operating income **out of scope** for this course entirely.

---

## Gross profit (3.6.A.4)

**3.6.A.4** **Gross profit** is the profit earned after deducting **only the direct costs of production**. It is calculated as:

**Gross profit = revenue − COGS**

**DK Coffee Lab:**

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $342,000 |
| − COGS (beans, milk, cups, production wages, roastery costs) | ($96,000) |
| **= Gross profit** | **$246,000** |

> **"Only the direct costs" — gross profit is deliberately *incomplete*. It answers one question: is the product priced above what it directly costs to make?**

---

## Operating expenses — two sub-buckets (3.6.A.5)

**3.6.A.5** **Operating expenses** are the **indirect costs** a business incurs. They are typically organized as:

| Bucket | Includes |
|---|---|
| **Selling expenses** | Advertising costs, salespeople's salaries |
| **General and administrative (G&A) expenses** | Office salaries, rent on office space, insurance |

**Research and development spending is also included in operating expenses.**

**DK Coffee Lab:**

| Bucket | Items | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| **Selling** | Marketing $21,000 + sales staff $20,000 | $41,000 |
| **G&A** | Office salaries $42,000, rent $84,000, insurance $6,000, supplies $6,000, utilities $12,000, maintenance $7,000 | $157,000 |
| **R&D** | — | $0 |
| **Total operating expenses** | | **$198,000** |

> **The CED gives you the two bucket names — use them. "Selling" and "general and administrative" are the vocabulary of the exam.**

---

## Operating profit (3.6.A.6)

**3.6.A.6** **Operating profit** is the profit earned after deducting **COGS and operating expenses**. It represents the business's **net income before interest and taxes**. It is calculated as:

**Operating profit = gross profit − operating expenses**

**DK Coffee Lab:** $246,000 − $198,000 = **$48,000**

> **"Before interest and taxes" is the definition, not a footnote.** Operating profit measures the core business, deliberately excluding how the business is financed and how it is taxed.

---

## Interest expense and pretax income (3.6.A.7)

**3.6.A.7** **Interest expense** is the **cost of borrowing money through loans (including bonds)**.

**Pretax income = operating profit − interest expense**

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Operating profit | $48,000 |
| − Interest expense (9% loan from 3.5) | ($1,200) |
| **= Pretax income** | **$46,800** |

> **Interest is the *only* non-operating item on this statement.** The CED's exclusion statement rules out other non-operating income and expenses — so do not invent gains or losses from asset sales in an answer.

---

## Taxes (3.6.A.8)

**3.6.A.8** **If pretax income is positive**, businesses must pay taxes on that income, and **any tax expense is included on the income statement**.

**DK Coffee Lab:** 25% × $46,800 = **$11,700**

> **Read the conditional: *if pretax income is positive*.** A business with a pretax **loss** pays no income tax that year — which is why a loss year is not the same as a cash disaster.

---

## Net profit — the bottom line (3.6.A.9)

**3.6.A.9** **Net profit** (income after taxes) is the **final dollar amount** reported on a typical income statement:

**Net profit = pretax income − taxes**

It is known as the **bottom line** and represents **the profit or income earned by the business's owners** at the end of a given period.

**The complete DK Coffee Lab income statement:**

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $342,000 |
| − COGS | ($96,000) |
| **= Gross profit** | **$246,000** |
| − Operating expenses | ($198,000) |
| **= Operating profit** | **$48,000** |
| − Interest expense | ($1,200) |
| **= Pretax income** | **$46,800** |
| − Taxes (25%) | ($11,700) |
| **= Net profit** | **$35,100** |

---

## The ladder, one picture

```
Revenue
  − COGS (direct)
= GROSS PROFIT
  − Operating expenses (indirect: selling + G&A + R&D)
= OPERATING PROFIT   ← before interest and taxes
  − Interest expense
= PRETAX INCOME
  − Taxes
= NET PROFIT         ← the bottom line
```

> **Each rung removes a different *kind* of cost: direct cost → indirect operating cost → financing cost → tax. Classify the cost, and the rung is automatic.**

---

## What is explicitly out of scope

The CED's **exclusion statement** for 3.6 removes:

| Excluded | Why it matters for you |
|---|---|
| Adjustments to gross sales to calculate **net sales** (returns, discounts) | Do not deduct returns before revenue |
| The **composition of COGS** in detail | Know that it is direct cost; do not memorise line items |
| **Non-operating sources of income and expenses (except interest)** | No asset-sale gains, no investment income in your answers |
| **Below-the-bottom-line items** | Net profit is the end of the statement |

> **This is unusually explicit for the CED. If an answer choice introduces an asset-sale gain or an extraordinary item, it is probably there to trap you.**

# Part 2: Reading the numbers
### LO 3.6.B — Margins, benchmarks, and change

---

## Who uses the income statement (3.6.B.1)

**3.6.B.1** Income statement information is used by **internal and external stakeholders** to:

| Use | Who |
|---|---|
| **Monitor trends in revenues and costs** | Managers, owners |
| **Evaluate profitability** | Managers, owners, lenders, investors |
| **Inform decisions related to improving future profitability** | Managers |

> **Note what is *not* on the list: "determine the share price."** The income statement informs decisions; it does not price the business by itself (that is 3.5.D.2's valuation).**

---

## Gross profit margin (3.6.B.2)

**3.6.B.2** **Gross profit margin = gross profit ÷ total revenue**

It evaluates **how successfully a business is setting prices and managing its direct costs.**

**DK Coffee Lab:** $246,000 ÷ $342,000 = **71.9%**

> **Gross margin is a pricing-and-supply-chain number.** If it falls, either prices dropped, or direct costs (beans, milk, production wages) rose. Nothing else can move it.

---

## Operating profit margin (3.6.B.3)

**3.6.B.3** **Operating profit margin = operating profit ÷ total revenue**

It evaluates how successfully a business is:

- **marketing and selling** its products
- **administering** the activities of the business
- **controlling operating expenses**

**DK Coffee Lab:** $48,000 ÷ $342,000 = **14.0%**

> **Note the three jobs in the EK — selling, administering, controlling. A falling operating margin with a stable gross margin points at operating expenses, not at pricing.**

---

## Net profit margin (3.6.B.4)

**3.6.B.4** **Net profit margin = net profit ÷ total revenue**

It evaluates **overall profitability**, and describes **the percentage of revenue that flows to owners as income**.

**DK Coffee Lab:** $35,100 ÷ $342,000 = **10.3%**

> **Read that as a sentence: 71.9 cents of every revenue dollar survives direct costs, 14 cents survives the operating costs, and 10.3 cents reaches the owners.**

---

## Three margins, three diagnoses

| Margin | Formula | DK Coffee Lab | Falling margin means… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gross** | gross profit ÷ revenue | **71.9%** | Pricing too low or direct costs too high |
| **Operating** | operating profit ÷ revenue | **14.0%** | Selling, admin, or operating costs are out of control |
| **Net** | net profit ÷ revenue | **10.3%** | Interest or taxes are eating the remaining profit |

> **The margin that fell tells you where to look. This is the single most useful diagnostic skill in 3.6.B.**

---

## Benchmarking — against what? (3.6.B.5)

**3.6.B.5** Margins are typically **benchmarked** against:

| Benchmark | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Projections** | Did we hit our own plan? |
| **Past performance** | Are we improving or slipping? |
| **Competitors' performance** | Are we better or worse than the market? |

> **A 10.3% net margin is not "good" or "bad" in isolation.** A coffee shop and a grocery chain have completely different normal margins — only a benchmark makes the number meaningful.

---

## Percent change — the one formula in 3.6.B (3.6.B.6)

**3.6.B.6** Businesses use the **percentage change equation** to determine the rate of change for **any** data point — income, cost, profit, or profit margin.

**Percent change = [(Current value − Initial value) ÷ Initial value] × 100**

**DK Coffee Lab's net profit margin, year 1 → year 2:**

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Net profit margin | 10.3% | 12.4% |

Percent change = [(12.4 − 10.3) ÷ 10.3] × 100 = **≈ +20.4%**

> **The denominator is always the *initial* (older) value. Using the newer value is the most common calculation error in this whole unit.**

# Part 3: Planning ahead
### LO 3.6.C / 3.6.D — Projections, budgets, and building one

---

## Why plan at all (3.6.C.1)

**3.6.C.1** Businesses **and consumers** face **financial uncertainty** because incomes and expenses fluctuate in response to:

| Force | Example |
|---|---|
| **Changing needs and wants** | A new baby, a new menu |
| **Competitive pressure within markets** | A rival opens across the street |
| **PESTEL forces** | Inflation, a tax change, a new regulation |

**Thoughtful planning can help both businesses and consumers meet financial goals.**

> **Same logic as 3.1.B, applied to a business. PESTEL does not stay in Part 1 — it is the reason planning is necessary at all.**

---

## Businesses plan with projections and budgets (3.6.C.2)

**3.6.C.2** Businesses develop **projected income statements and/or budgets** to plan for revenues, costs, and the resulting profit or loss for a period (month, quarter, or year).

These describe:

- **estimates of all expenses** involved in running the business
- **predicted revenues**, given **projected production and sales levels**

> **Two things are estimated, and one is derived: revenue and expenses are estimated; profit or loss falls out of them.**

---

## Why projections do three jobs (3.6.C.3)

**3.6.C.3** Projected income statements and budgets help businesses:

| Job | What it prevents |
|---|---|
| **Plan for expected costs** | Surprise expenses |
| **Identify funding needs** | Discovering the cash gap too late (the bridge back to 3.5.A.3) |
| **Maintain enough cash to meet current obligations** | Insolvency while profitable |

> **This EK is the direct link between 3.6 and 3.8: a projection is how you discover a cash-flow problem *before* it happens.**

---

## Consumer budgets (3.6.C.4)

**3.6.C.4** Consumers use **budgets** to plan **saving and spending allocations** based on **expected income**.

A consumer budget describes:

- **expected net pay** — income **after taxes and other deductions** — for a period, typically a month or a year
- **all planned savings and expenses, including debt payments**

> **Note "net pay, not gross pay."** Budgeting on gross income is one of the most common real-world errors — and the CED names net pay explicitly.

---

## What budgets reveal (3.6.C.5)

**3.6.C.5** Budgets help consumers:

- **identify spending patterns**
- **assess whether they are meeting their financial goals** — including **managing debt**, **saving for future expenses**, and **donating to charitable organizations whose missions they support**

> **Yes, charitable giving is in the EK. It is a legitimate budget line, not an afterthought.**

---

## Building the statement: current data first (3.6.D.1 / 3.6.D.2)

**3.6.D.1** Businesses use **current financial data** tracking **revenues, expenses, and taxes** to develop income statements.

**3.6.D.2** Businesses **predict future revenues and expenses (and estimated taxes)** to develop **projected** income statements.

| | **Income statement** | **Projected income statement** |
|---|---|---|
| **Input** | Current, recorded data | Predicted revenues, expenses, estimated taxes |
| **Tense** | Past | Future |

> **Same document, different tense. The exam will ask you to build either one from a data set — the ladder does not change.**

---

## Where the estimates come from (3.6.D.3 / 3.6.D.4)

**3.6.D.3 — Future revenues** are estimated from:
- **planned pricing strategies**
- **market research about customer demand and industry trends**

**3.6.D.4 — Future COGS and operating expenses** are estimated from:
- **planned production processes**
- **the cost of supply chain components**
- **other projected expenses**: occupancy, marketing, office and sales salaries, planned R&D

> **Note the pairing: revenue estimates come from the *market* (demand, pricing, trends); cost estimates come from *operations* (process, supply chain, occupancy). Mixing them up is a common FRQ slip.**

---

## 🔑 Exam points to remember — Income statement

| # | Point |
|---|-------|
| 1 | An **income statement** (statement of profit and loss) compares **total revenue to total costs over a period** to determine **net profit or loss**; it typically shows **multiple periods** |
| 2 | It organizes data into **revenue, COGS, and operating expenses**, and also identifies **interest, taxes, and nonrecurring expenses** |
| 3 | **Revenue** = income from **core activities** |
| 4 | **Gross profit = revenue − COGS** (direct production costs only) |
| 5 | **Operating expenses** are indirect: **selling** expenses, **general and administrative** expenses, and **R&D** |
| 6 | **Operating profit = gross profit − operating expenses** = net income **before interest and taxes** |
| 7 | **Interest expense** = cost of borrowing through loans (including bonds); **pretax income = operating profit − interest expense** |
| 8 | **Taxes** are paid **if pretax income is positive**; tax expense appears on the statement |
| 9 | **Net profit = pretax income − taxes** = the **bottom line** = income earned by owners |
| 10 | **Out of scope:** net sales adjustments, detailed COGS composition, non-operating items **except interest**, below-the-bottom-line items |
| 11 | Stakeholders use the statement to **monitor trends, evaluate profitability, and inform decisions** |
| 12 | **Gross margin** = gross profit ÷ revenue → pricing and direct-cost management |
| 13 | **Operating margin** = operating profit ÷ revenue → selling, administering, and controlling operating expenses |
| 14 | **Net margin** = net profit ÷ revenue → overall profitability; the share of revenue flowing to owners |
| 15 | Margins are **benchmarked** against projections, past performance, and competitors |
| 16 | **Percent change = [(current − initial) ÷ initial] × 100** — works for any data point |
| 17 | Uncertainty comes from changing needs, competitive pressure, and **PESTEL**; planning helps businesses **and** consumers |
| 18 | **Projected statements and budgets** plan costs, identify funding needs, and maintain enough cash |
| 19 | Consumer budgets use **net pay** and include all planned savings, expenses, and **debt payments** |
| 20 | Revenue estimates come from **pricing strategy, market research, and trends**; cost estimates come from **production processes, supply chain, and planned expenses** |

---

## Three exam tells — income statement

**Tell 1 — "Which kind of cost?"** Direct → COGS. Indirect operating → operating expenses (selling, G&A, R&D). Financing → interest. Tax → last. The ladder is built from cost *types*, so classify before you calculate.

**Tell 2 — "Which margin is being described?"** Pricing and direct costs → **gross**. Selling, administering, controlling costs → **operating**. Overall profitability and what owners keep → **net**. The EK's wording is the giveaway.

**Tell 3 — "Initial or current value?"** Every percent-change question divides by the **initial** value. And if a margin is compared across two differently sized businesses, dollars are irrelevant — only the margin speaks.

## Practice — Stimulus

**Harbor Light Kayaks**, year 2:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Tour and rental revenue | $480,000 |
| Kayaks and equipment purchased for rentals (direct) | $150,000 |
| Guide wages (direct) | $60,000 |
| Rent, insurance, office salaries (G&A) | $120,000 |
| Advertising and sales staff salaries (selling) | $40,000 |
| Research and development (new route app) | $20,000 |
| Interest on equipment loan | $10,000 |
| Tax rate | 20% |

Year 1 net profit was **$56,000**.

---

## Practice — Q1

> What is Harbor Light's gross profit?

> A. $270,000
> B. $330,000
> C. $250,000
> D. $210,000

---

## Practice — Q1 answer

**Answer: A.**

**Gross profit = revenue − COGS** (3.6.A.4). Direct costs: $150,000 equipment + $60,000 guide wages = **$210,000**. $480,000 − $210,000 = **$270,000**.

**Traps:**
- **B** — subtracted only the equipment; guide wages are **direct labour** (3.4.B.4, cost of sales)
- **C** — also subtracted the $20,000 R&D; R&D is an **operating expense** (3.6.A.5)
- **D** — that is COGS itself, not gross profit

> **Direct labour is COGS. R&D is an operating expense. Both are "costs of the product" in ordinary speech — not in the CED.**

---

## Practice — Q2

> What is Harbor Light's operating profit margin?

> A. 56.3%
> B. 18.8%
> C. 16.7%
> D. 13.3%

---

## Practice — Q2 answer

**Answer: B.**

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Gross profit | $270,000 |
| − G&A (rent, insurance, office salaries) | ($120,000) |
| − Selling (advertising, sales salaries) | ($40,000) |
| − R&D | ($20,000) |
| **= Operating profit** | **$90,000** |

**Operating margin = $90,000 ÷ $480,000 = 18.75% ≈ 18.8%** (3.6.B.3).

**Traps:**
- **A** — that is the **gross** margin (270 ÷ 480)
- **C** — that is pretax income ÷ revenue, one rung too low
- **D** — that is the **net** margin (see Q3)

> **Add all three operating-expense components before dividing — and note that R&D counts even though it is neither selling nor G&A.**

---

## Practice — Q3

> What is Harbor Light's net profit?

> A. $90,000
> B. $80,000
> C. $64,000
> D. $72,000

---

## Practice — Q3 answer

**Answer: C.**

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Operating profit | $90,000 |
| − Interest expense | ($10,000) |
| **= Pretax income** | **$80,000** |
| − Tax at 20% | ($16,000) |
| **= Net profit** | **$64,000** |

**Traps:**
- **A** — stopped at operating profit; interest and taxes are still below it
- **B** — that is pretax income; the tax has not been subtracted
- **D** — taxed operating profit ($90,000 × 0.8 = $72,000) instead of pretax income

> **Tax applies to *pretax income*, never to operating profit.**

---

## Practice — Q4

> Harbor Light's net profit rose from $56,000 in year 1 to $64,000 in year 2. What is the percent change in net profit?

> A. 8.0%
> B. 12.5%
> C. 14.3%
> D. 87.5%

---

## Practice — Q4 answer

**Answer: C.**

**Percent change = [(current − initial) ÷ initial] × 100** (3.6.B.6) = [(64,000 − 56,000) ÷ 56,000] × 100 = (8,000 ÷ 56,000) × 100 = **14.3%**.

**Traps:**
- **A** — the $-change expressed as a percentage of revenue, not of the initial value
- **B** — divided by the **current** value (8 ÷ 64) instead of the initial value
- **D** — the ratio of current to initial (64 ÷ 56 × 100), not the *change*

> **The denominator is always the initial (older) value. Write it down before you compute.**

---

## Trap patterns to watch

| # | Trap type | Where it appears |
|---|-----------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Direct wages left out of COGS** | Q1 |
| 2 | **R&D in COGS** — R&D is an operating expense | Q1, Q2 |
| 3 | **Dropping R&D as "neither selling nor G&A"** — the EK adds it | Q2 |
| 4 | **Tax applied to operating profit** — tax applies to **pretax income** | Q3 |
| 5 | **Change vs. level** — percent change divides the *change* by the initial value, not the new value | Q4 |
| 6 | **Dividing by the current value** in percent change | Q4 |
| 7 | **Inventing non-operating gains** — excluded except interest | throughout |
| 8 | **Margin without a benchmark** — a number alone is not "good" or "bad" | 3.6.B.5 |
| 9 | **Budget = projection** — planned spending vs. expected outcome | 3.6.C.2 |
| 10 | **Budgeting on gross pay** — consumer budgets use **net pay** | 3.6.C.4 |

## Wrap-up — 3.6 The Income Statement

**What we covered:**
- The income statement compares **revenue to total costs over a period** to find **net profit or loss**, and usually shows **multiple periods**
- Three categories — **revenue, COGS, operating expenses** — plus **interest, taxes, and nonrecurring expenses**
- **Revenue − COGS = gross profit**; **− operating expenses (selling, G&A, R&D) = operating profit**; **− interest = pretax income**; **− taxes = net profit**
- Non-operating items other than interest are **explicitly out of scope**
- **Gross margin** → pricing and direct costs; **operating margin** → selling, administration, cost control; **net margin** → overall profitability
- Margins mean nothing without a **benchmark**: projections, past performance, or competitors
- **Percent change = [(current − initial) ÷ initial] × 100** — for any data point
- **Projections and budgets** plan costs, identify funding needs, and protect cash; consumer budgets use **net pay** and include **debt payments**
- Revenue estimates come from **pricing and market research**; cost estimates come from **processes, supply chain, and planned expenses**
