# 1.4 How Business Ideas Originate
### AP Business with Personal Finance · Unit 1

Lesson deck — **concepts + practice**

---

## Today's goals

By the end of this lesson you can:

1. Identify **three sources** of business ideas: gaps, opportunities, and existing businesses
2. Explain what makes an opportunity **viable**
3. Connect **risk** and **return** — and why entrepreneurs choose to take that trade

**Running story continues:** the hallway is gone, the gym is crowded — and now we go back to the **very beginning** of the whole story.

---

## The new problem

The hallway is gone. The gym is crowded.

Both kids built something — but they each started from **one specific moment**: noticing a problem worth solving.

> Every business begins as an **idea**. Some come from an empty shelf. Some come from a new machine. Some come from watching someone else struggle. Where you look **shapes what you find**.

---

## The three sources of business ideas

| Source | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| **Gaps** | A need or want that is **not currently being met** |
| **Opportunities** | A **new or better** way to do something (technology, innovation, change) |
| **Existing businesses** | What is **already being sold** in a market |

---

## Source 1 — Gaps

A **gap** is a need or want that is **not currently being met**.

- An empty shelf in the hallway candy aisle → a gap
- A student who wants a cold drink but nobody sells one → a gap
- A neighborhood with no grocery store → a gap

> **How entrepreneurs find gaps:** they **observe** — watching what people struggle with, what they complain about, what they can't find.

---

## Gaps — the key distinction

A gap is **not** about having no competitors at all.

A gap can exist even when competitors are present — **if no one is meeting a specific need**:

- Five candy sellers in the hallway — but none sell **sugar-free** options → a gap
- Three coffee shops on one street — but none open before 7 AM → a gap
- Many tutoring services — but none in **Spanish** → a gap

> A gap = a **specific unmet need**, not just an empty market.

---

## Source 2 — Opportunities

An **opportunity** is a new or better way to do something — often triggered by a **change**:

- A **new machine** that fills bottles automatically → a vending opportunity
- A **new technology** (like smartphones) → thousands of app-based businesses
- A **social trend** (like remote work) → new demand for home office gear
- A **rule change** (like a school policy) → new markets open or close

- **塔斯汀 (Tastien):** the **国潮 cultural trend** + **抖音 live-commerce** turned "burgers" into "中国汉堡" — one cultural change, a whole new way to sell fast food

> **How entrepreneurs find opportunities:** they look for **change** — and ask "what does this change make possible?"

---

## Source 3 — Existing businesses

Many ideas come from **what already exists** in a market — by copying, adapting, or improving:

- A candy seller copies the **pricing strategy** of the kid across the hall
- A new coffee shop opens by offering what the old one does — **but faster**
- A local delivery service copies a model that works in another city

- **塔斯汀:** its founder was a **华莱士 (Wallace) franchisee** — he **copied** the burger-chain model first, then localized it into "中国汉堡"

> **Replication** — copying what works in one market into another — is one of the most common sources of business ideas worldwide. 塔斯汀 literally copied an existing chain before it innovated.

---

## Replication — the same move, two outcomes

**共享单车 (2016–2017)** proved a *mechanism*, not a market: **scan a QR code → pay by the minute → leave it anywhere.** *(Remember ofo from 1.1 — the mechanism outlived the company.)* Two teams copied it. Same move — opposite outcomes.

| | **共享充电宝** (街电 / 怪兽充电) | **共享汽车** (途歌 TOGO / EZZY) |
|---|---|---|
| **What was copied** | QR scan + 按时计费 + 免押金 rental | the same "rent a shared asset by the minute" idea |
| **Asset cost** | ≈¥100 per power bank (cabinet ≈¥1,000–3,000) | **¥100,000+ per car** |
| **Operating cost** | almost none — it sits on a shop counter | 停车, 加油/充电, 保险, 洗车, 违章, 调度 |
| **How often people need it** | every day — phones die daily | occasional — and rarely near where the car is parked |
| **Deposit** | 芝麻信用 / 支付分 → **免押金** | ¥1,500–2,000 押金 → refund crisis |
| **Outcome** | **worked** — 怪兽充电 listed on Nasdaq (2021) | **collapsed** — deposit queues, fleet sold off |

> **Both were replication. Only one was viable.** They copied the same *mechanism* — but not the same *cost structure*.

**Which source?** 共享充电宝 = **replication + gap** — 机制抄共享单车，缺口是"出门手机没电、附近没处充"；移动支付 / 信用免押是**使能条件**（回答 "why now"），不是 idea 的来源。

## Replication — why the copy failed

**Replication copies the model. Viability decides whether the copy survives.**

**共享充电宝 worked because the gap got *better* on the way:**

- 手机没电 is **high-frequency and high-anxiety** — people pay to make it disappear *now*
- the device is **cheap enough to lose**, and the shop counter does the "parking" for free

**途歌 failed because every number got *worse*:**

- the asset cost **1,000× more**, the operating cost **never stopped**, and utilization was **too low to cover depreciation**
- 押金 was part of the cash engine — so when users demanded refunds, the whole thing unravelled

> **Lesson 1.3 meets 1.4:** demand ≠ viability, and **a proven model ≠ a viable business**. When you replicate, the thing you must copy is the **cost structure** — not just the QR code.

## Combining the sources

Most real business ideas involve **more than one source at the same time**.

- The vending machine idea comes from a **gap** (unmet demand after the hallway closed) **plus** an **opportunity** (a new filling machine)
- A local coffee chain copies an existing model **and** adapts it to local tastes → **replication + gap**
- The same shared-rental mechanism moves from 单车 to **充电宝** → **replication + a better gap** (copied into **共享汽车** instead → failed on cost)

> When you see a scenario, ask: **"Is this about an unmet need, a new possibility, or something already on the market — or more than one?"**

---

## Viability — recap from 1.3

**Viability** = the ability to **survive and stay successful** in a market over time.

An idea is only a starting point. To become a **business**, it must be viable:

- Is there a **real, unmet need** (gap)? Or a change that creates a new possibility (opportunity)?
- Are there enough **willing and able** buyers?
- Can the business **survive** competition, regulation, and cost?

> If the answer to any of these is no → the idea may be interesting, but it **isn't viable**.

## Matching strengths to opportunities

An entrepreneur succeeds by matching **what they are good at** with **what the market needs**.

- Someone who bakes well → a cake business (strength matches a gap)
- Someone who codes well → an app (strength meets a technology opportunity)
- Someone with money and connections → a franchise (resources enable replication)

> A good idea in the wrong hands fails. A good idea **in the right hands** — with the right skills, resources, and timing — becomes a viable business.

---

## Risk — definition

**Risk** = the chance that things will not go as planned — the possibility of **unwanted outcomes** like losing money, time, or effort.

Every business involves risk:

- Will customers actually **buy**?
- Will a competitor **undercut** you?
- Will the cost of materials **rise** unexpectedly?
- Will a new regulation **shut you down**?

> Entrepreneurs don't eliminate risk — they **evaluate** it and decide whether the potential reward is worth the chance of failure.

---

## Return — definition

**Return** = the **gain** (usually money) that an entrepreneur hopes to earn from a business.

When an entrepreneur invests time and money into a business, the return is **what they hope to get back — and more**.

- Invest $500 in candy supplies → earn $800 in sales → **return = $300**
- Invest $10,000 in a food truck → earn $15,000 → **return = $5,000**
- Return is **never guaranteed** — it depends on whether the idea actually works

---

## The risk–return relationship

> **Higher potential return usually comes with higher risk.**

| Business idea | Risk | Potential return |
|---|---|---|
| Selling candy in a busy hallway | Low (small investment, proven demand) | Low (small profit per sale) |
| Opening a restaurant | Medium (bigger investment, competition, food safety) | Medium (higher revenue, higher costs) |
| Launching a tech startup | High (unproven market, big investment, many unknowns) | High (if it works, massive scale) |

---

## Reading the table

The entrepreneur's job is to **judge whether the potential return is worth the risk they're taking**.

- Candy = low risk, low return → safe but small
- Restaurant = medium risk, medium return → bigger stakes, bigger reward
- Tech startup = high risk, high return → could win big or lose everything

> The table doesn't tell you which is "best" — it tells you **what you're trading off**.

---

## The risk–return tradeoff

The **risk–return tradeoff** = a **situation where taking more risk gives you the chance of a higher return, but also a higher chance of losing everything**.

- Low-risk ideas are **safer** but often earn **less**
- High-risk ideas **could** earn a lot — but **could** also fail completely
- The right choice depends on the entrepreneur's **resources**, **skills**, **market knowledge**, and **tolerance for failure**

> "High return, zero risk" = a trap answer on the exam. If it sounds too good to be true, **it is**.

---

**Real example — Copa Di Vino:** "expert" investors said **no — twice** on live TV; the market said **yes** (revenue $60M+). Risk and return are judged by **customers**, not by experts.

**Real example — 密室逃脱 (escape rooms):** the market said **yes — loudly** (long queues, sold-out rooms), yet the business still collapsed. **Experts can be wrong — and so can crowds.** Copa's bet paid off; the escape-room bet didn't. Risk is real even when demand looks hot.

---

## Why entrepreneurs accept risk

Not everyone takes the same risks. Entrepreneurs choose risk because:

- They see an **opportunity** others don't
- They have **resources** (time, money, skills) that make the risk manageable
- They believe the **potential return** justifies the chance of failure
- They are **willing to fail** and try again

> Many successful entrepreneurs **failed first**. What separates them is that they **kept going** — and learned from what went wrong.

---

## Risk vs. Uncertainty — the exam distinction

**Risk** is a known danger. **Uncertainty** is when you don't even know what could go wrong.

| | Risk | Uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| What you know | You can **name** the danger | You **can't** predict what will happen |
| Example | "A competitor might undercut me" | "Something I haven't thought of might destroy my business" |
| How entrepreneurs deal with it | **Plan** for it (insurance, backup suppliers, savings) | **Can't fully plan** — must stay flexible and adapt |

---

## What the exam actually tests

Most exam scenarios describe **risk**, not uncertainty — they give you enough information to **name** the danger.

- If the scenario says "a new competitor is entering the market" → that's **risk** — you can name it
- If the scenario says "something unexpected might happen" → that's closer to **uncertainty** — but the exam rarely goes there

> **Exam rule:** if you can name what could go wrong, it's **risk** — and the question wants you to **plan for it**, not just worry about it.

---

## The entrepreneur's decision — tying it together

An entrepreneur decides to act when three things **come together**:

1. A source of ideas — a **gap**, an **opportunity**, or something in an **existing market**
2. A judgment that the idea is **viable** — enough buyers, survivable competition, manageable cost
3. A belief that the potential **return** justifies the **risk**

> When all three align → the entrepreneur invests time, effort, and money to **start the business**.

---

## How to write a strong exam answer

**Name the source → state why it matters → connect to viability or risk–return.**

- "The gap is that no one sells sugar-free candy" → **not enough** — names the source but stops
- "The gap is that no one sells sugar-free candy, **and** there are 30 students with dietary restrictions who would buy daily — **that makes it viable**" → **strong answer**

---

## The connected-reasoning pattern

The exam rewards **connected reasoning**: source → viability → risk/return judgment.

1. **Source** — "This is a gap because…"
2. **Viability** — "…and it's viable because there are enough willing buyers…"
3. **Risk/return** — "…with low risk and modest return because the investment is small"

> Each piece **builds on the last**. Stopping at step 1 is the most common reason students lose marks.

## Applying PESTEL to the vending idea

The student should check the same outside forces from 1.3:

| Factor | What to check |
|---|---|
| **Political** | Will the school **allow** it? |
| **Economic** | Can students **afford** $1.50 snacks? |

## The environment check — why it matters

- The idea might be great (gap + viable demand + good return)
- But if the school says **no** (political) or requires an expensive **permit** (legal) → the idea **dies before it starts**

> **Lesson 1.3 meets 1.4:** an idea is only viable if the **PESTEL factors** around it allow it to survive.

## Stimulus: ParkClean

**Marcus Chen** lives near a large public park. He notices:

1. The park has **no trash cans** near the picnic areas — visitors carry trash for 15 minutes before finding a bin, and many **leave garbage on the ground**
2. A **new city ordinance** just passed requiring parks to be kept clean or the city will **cut the parks budget**
3. A classmate already runs a small **lawn-mowing service** in the neighborhood and is earning steady money

Marcus is thinking about starting a business to **clean the park on weekends**.

---

## Q1 — Classify the source

The fact that the park has no trash cans near the picnic area — and visitors leave garbage on the ground — is best described as what type of business idea source?

- A. An opportunity created by new technology
- B. A gap — an unmet need that no one is currently serving
- C. An existing business that Marcus is copying
- D. A risk factor that discourages new businesses

<!--
Pause. Ask: is this about something new happening, or about a need that exists but isn't being met?
-->

---

## Q1 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** A gap is a need or want that is **not currently being met**. The park needs cleaning, nobody is providing that service, and visitors are leaving garbage behind — that is an unmet need.

**Trap: A** — there is no new technology in the scenario. A gap is about **observation** (watching a problem nobody is solving), not about innovation.

**Trap: C** — the classmate's lawn-mowing service is a separate business. Marcus isn't copying it; he's seeing a **different** unmet need.

---

## Q2 — Viability check

Which factor is **most relevant** to whether Marcus's park-cleaning idea is viable?

- A. Whether Marcus enjoys being outdoors
- B. Whether someone (the city or park visitors) is **willing and able to pay** for the service
- C. Whether the park has flowers or trees
- D. Whether Marcus's classmates think the idea is cool

---

## Q2 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Viability depends on whether the business can **survive** — and that means there must be **willing and able buyers**. If no one will pay, the idea isn't viable no matter how good it sounds.

**Trap: A** — enjoyment is a **personal motivation**, not a viability factor. A business can be viable even if the owner doesn't enjoy it, and unviable even if they love the work.

> **Viability is about the market, not about the person.**

---

## Q3 — Risk–return judgment

Marcus would need to spend $200 on cleaning supplies and give up his Saturday mornings. The city might pay him $80 per weekend. Which statement best describes the risk–return tradeoff?

- A. The return is guaranteed because the city passed the ordinance
- B. The risk is low (small investment) and the return is modest — a low-risk, low-return opportunity
- C. This is a high-risk, high-return opportunity because the city might change the rules
- D. There is no risk because Marcus is providing a service, not selling a product

---

## Q3 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** $200 is a small investment, $80/weekend is a modest return, and the ordinance supports demand. This is a classic **low-risk, low-return** scenario — small stakes, small but steady gain.

**Trap: A** — "guaranteed" is an absolute. The city could cut the budget, change priorities, or find a cheaper contractor. **No return is ever guaranteed.**

**Trap: C** — the investment is $200 and the scenario gives no reason to expect massive earnings. High-risk, high-return requires **large stakes or large potential gains** — neither applies here.

---

## Q4 — Combining sources

A school cafeteria currently serves only hot food. Students have asked for **fresh salads and cold sandwiches**, but the cafeteria hasn't added them. A student starts selling homemade salads from a cooler outside the cafeteria entrance.

This business idea combines which source(s)?

- A. Opportunity only — the student is innovating
- B. Existing business only — the student is copying the cafeteria
- C. A gap (unmet demand for cold food) combined with the existing market (the cafeteria already serves food)
- D. Neither — selling food at school is not a real business

---

## Q4 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** There is a **gap** — students want cold food and nobody is selling it. There is also an **existing market** — the cafeteria already operates in the same space. The student's idea comes from **both sources at once**.

> **Most real ideas combine more than one source.** The exam rewards you for recognizing **all** the sources present, not just the loudest one.

## Q5 — Matching strengths to opportunity

Two students both notice the same gap: the school has no tutoring service for math. Student A is **excellent at math** and has free time after school. Student B **struggles in math** but has a lot of savings from a part-time job.

Which statement is most accurate?

- A. Student B is better positioned because money matters more than skill
- B. Student A is better positioned because their **strengths match the opportunity**
- C. Both are equally positioned because they both identified the same gap
- D. Neither can succeed because the school doesn't officially allow tutoring

---

## Q5 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Identifying a gap is only the first step. Student A has the **skill** (math ability) and the **availability** (free time) — their strengths **match** the opportunity. Student B has money but **lacks the core skill**.

> A good idea **in the right hands** becomes a viable business. A good idea **in the wrong hands** — with the wrong skills or resources — fails.

## Q6 — Risk and return are connected

A student wants to start a business. They consider two options:

- **Option A:** Buy candy for $50 and sell it at school (small investment, proven demand)
- **Option B:** Spend $2,000 developing a homework app (large investment, unproven demand)

Which statement about the risk–return tradeoff is **most accurate**?

- A. Option B is better because higher investment always means higher profit
- B. Option A has lower risk and lower potential return; Option B has higher risk and higher potential return
- C. Both options have the same level of risk because both are student businesses
- D. Option A is riskier because candy is a physical product that can melt

---

## Q6 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** This is the risk–return tradeoff in its simplest form. Option A is low-risk (small investment, known demand) with low return (small profit). Option B is high-risk (big investment, no proven buyers) with high potential return (**if** the app succeeds, it could scale to thousands of students).

**Trap: A** — "higher investment **always** means higher profit" uses an absolute. Higher investment means higher **potential** return — but also higher potential **loss**.

> **"Always" and "guaranteed" are trap words** — they show up in almost every risk–return question.

---

## Trap patterns to watch

| # | Trap | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Confusing a **gap** with an **opportunity** — gaps are about unmet need; opportunities are about change or innovation | Q1 |
| 2 | **Personal motivation** ≠ viability — viability is about the **market**, not the person | Q2 |
| 3 | **"Guaranteed"** return — absolutes still apply | Q3 |
| 4 | Recognizing only **one** source when the scenario combines two | Q4 |
| 5 | **Identifying a gap isn't enough** — strengths must match the opportunity | Q5 |
| 6 | **"Higher investment always means higher profit"** — absolutes in risk–return | Q6 |

## Wrap-up

- **Three sources** of business ideas: gaps, opportunities, existing businesses
- **Gaps** = unmet needs; **opportunities** = new or better ways (often triggered by change); **existing businesses** = what's already being sold
- **Viability** = can this idea survive in this market?
- **Risk** = chance of unwanted outcomes; **return** = the gain you hope to earn
- **Risk–return tradeoff** = higher potential return usually means higher risk
- **Strengths must match** the opportunity — a good idea in the wrong hands fails
- **Combining sources** is normal — the exam rewards you for naming **all** of them
- **Replication** copies a *model* — the copy only survives if the **cost structure and usage frequency** transfer too (共享充电宝 ✓ / 途歌 ✗)
- **Viability isn't permanent** — a working copy can still be squeezed later by whoever owns the scarce resource (共享充电宝 → 点位 / 入场费)
