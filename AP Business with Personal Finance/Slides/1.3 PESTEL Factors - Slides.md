# 1.3 PESTEL Factors
### AP Business with Personal Finance · Unit 1

Lesson deck — **concepts + practice**

<!--

-->

---

## Today's goals

By the end of this lesson you can:

1. Describe the **six PESTEL factors** that shape every market
2. Apply the PESTEL framework to judge how **attractive** — and how **risky** — a market really is

**Running story continues:** the hallway price war is about to become irrelevant.

---

## The memo

Monday morning — but this time the problem isn't a competitor.

A memo goes up in the hallway:

> **"Starting next week, no food sales anywhere on campus, by order of the new principal."**

Two backpacks, one price war, one cold-drink sideline — and **none of it matters**, because a force **outside the market** just rewrote the rules for both kids at once.

> Neither seller did anything wrong. **The environment moved.**

---

## The PESTEL framework

**PESTEL** = six kinds of **outside forces** that shape the business landscape:

| Letter | Factor |
|---|---|
| **P** | Political |
| **E** | Economic |
| **S** | Social |
| **T** | Technological |
| **E** | Environmental |
| **L** | Legal |

> These factors decide which types of businesses are **viable** in a market.

---

## Inside vs. outside

**Rivals** live **inside** the market. **PESTEL factors** press on it from the **outside**.

- The two kids were busy undercutting each other on price
- Neither thought about the principal — and that's the point
- The principal isn't a competitor; the principal is **the environment**

> The exam expects you to (1) **identify** each factor from a scenario, and (2) **reason** about what it does to a business.

---

## P — Political

Policies and political dynamics that affect market activity.

**Key tools:**

| Tool | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Tax** | Funds government; often discourages the activity being taxed |
| **Subsidy** | Supports / incentivizes a specific activity |
| **Mandate** | Requires a business to do something |
| **Ban** | Prohibits an activity entirely |
| **Tariff** | A tax on imported goods — raises costs for importers |

The principal's memo works exactly like a **ban** — one decision from an authority and an entire market closes.

---

## P — Political (examples)

- A country raising **tariffs** on imported chocolate → costs rise for every chocolate maker that buys abroad
- A **mandate** requiring seat belts in every new car → creates a market no automaker can skip
- A **subsidy** for solar panels → incentivizes businesses to install them
- **Political stability** matters most where it is *missing* — businesses avoid markets where the rules might change with the next government

---

## E — Economic

Conditions of **the economy itself** — not any one company's choices.

**Key indicators:**

- Household **income**
- **Inflation**
- **Unemployment**
- **Interest rates**
- Economic **stability**

---

## E — Economic (hallway examples)

- When **allowance money shrinks**, fewer students have $1.50 to spend → the hallway market shrinks
- When **inflation** raises the supermarket price of a bulk candy box → the seller's cost rises before he sells a single bar

> **Exam tip:** income, inflation, unemployment, and interest rates all point to the same **source** — the condition of the economy itself — **never** to any single company's choices.

---

## S — Social

Trends in **society and culture** that affect consumers.

**Key drivers:**

- **Demographics** — age, income bracket, family size
- **Cultural norms**
- **Lifestyle trends**
- **Population growth**

---

## S — Social (hallway examples)

- A **fitness trend** sweeps the school → half the buyers switch to protein bars → demand for candy fades **without any rival lifting a finger**
- A neighborhood filling with **young families** changes what sells there
- A town growing **older** buys differently than a town filling with students

> **Demographics** = the measurable traits of a population.

---

## T — Technological

Features of a market tied to the **availability of technology**.

**Key drivers:**

- **Internet access**
- **Automation** of production
- **Pace of innovation**

---

## T — Technological (hallway examples)

- The **class group chat** is technology acting on the hallway market — inventory posted online reaches buyers a backpack cannot
- A region without reliable internet **cannot host** an online store
- A factory that automates can produce at costs a manual rival struggles to match

> The **rate of innovation** matters as much as any single tool — markets that adopt technology quickly **punish businesses that move slowly**.

---

## E — Environmental

External environmental conditions that promote or limit market activity.

**Key drivers:**

- **Geography**
- Access to **renewable / non-renewable resources**
- **Waste rules**
- How customers think about **the environment**

---

## E — Environmental (hallway examples)

- The **heat wave** that made cold drinks valuable in 1.2 was an environmental factor **doing the selling**
- A **drought** that ruins a strawberry harvest raises costs for every dessert stand
- Customers who care about **packaging** will pay for the business that listens

> **Geography counts:** a landlocked region cannot host a fishing fleet; a sunny one invites solar energy.

---

## L — Legal

Specific **laws, rules, and regulations** that impact market activity.

**Key areas:**

- **Employment laws**
- **Consumer protection** laws
- **Health and safety** rules
- **Environmental regulations**
- **Intellectual property** protection
- **Antitrust laws**

---

## L — Legal (hallway examples)

- A **food-handling certificate** requirement is a legal factor
- The **patent** from 1.2 that protects a medicine is a legal factor
- **Antitrust laws** (limit monopolies, block anti-competitive behavior) connect straight back to 1.2

---

## Political vs. Legal — the exam's favorite distinction

| | Political | Legal |
|---|---|---|
| Is about | Broad policy **choices** | Specific **laws** to comply with |
| Examples | Tax, subsidy, mandate, ban | Safety code, licensing requirement, employment law |
| Source | Government **decision** | Codified **rule** |

> A proposed **tax** on sugary food = political (policy choice). A **law** requiring a food-handler license = legal (specific rule).

---

## Overlap is real

A single new emissions law can carry political, environmental, *and* legal weight at once.

> The exam asks for the **best fit** — and the **source of the force** decides it.

**Classify by the source of the change** — not by what it costs, what it touches, or its subject matter.

---

## Viability — definition

**Viability** = the ability to **survive and stay successful** in a market over time.

The course uses this word constantly — learn it.

> Put the six factors together and they answer one blunt question: **what kind of business can survive in this market at all?**

---

## Viability — what each factor decides

| Factor | What it decides |
|---|---|
| Political | Which activities are incentivized vs. disincentivized |
| Economic | How much customers, businesses, and government can spend |
| Social | What people **want** to buy |
| Technological | What can be produced, distributed, communicated |
| Environmental | What resources are even available |
| Legal | The cost of operating + the line around what's allowed |

> Most businesses need a strong economy, but **some thrive in a weak one** — like discount stores when incomes fall.

---

## Applying the framework — the method

This is the skill the exam actually tests — **using PESTEL to evaluate a market.**

1. **Identify** which factors are relevant to your product
2. **Assess** how each one affects the market's **attractiveness** *and* its **risks**
3. A business enters where the factors **align** with its model, resources, and customers

> A **tech company** locates where internet and electricity are cheap. A **farm-to-table restaurant** opens where customers value local food. **Same framework, opposite conclusions.**

---

## Applying the framework — the candy seller's next market

The hallway is closed. He's considering the **weekend soccer league**:

| Factor | Reading for the soccer league |
|---|---|
| Political | No rule against food sales yet |
| Economic | Parents at games carry cash and buy freely |
| Social | A captive crowd shows up every Saturday |
| Technological | The group chat moves with him |
| Environmental | Games run outdoors; hot afternoons sell cold drinks |
| Legal | The park requires a simple vendor permit |

Five factors align; one costs paperwork → **an attractive market with known risk.**

---

## How to write a strong exam answer

**Name the factor → state its direction → tie it to this specific business.**

- A factor that's **good news** for one model is **bad news** for another
- Cheap electricity attracts a data center; it does nothing for a tutoring service
- The fitness trend helps a protein bar seller; it hurts the candy seller

---

## When factors change

PESTEL factors **move** — and when they move they can rewrite an existing market **overnight**.

- **Flu season** empties the school → every hallway sale disappears for two weeks
- A **new law**, a **downturn**, a **technology shift** — each redraws the map for businesses already operating
- The memo that opened this video was **one changed factor**, and it ended a market two competitors had spent weeks fighting over

> **Exam signal:** when a question describes a market shifting after a new law, a downturn, or a technology jump, it's testing whether you can **name which factor moved** and **what it did to viability.**

---

## Exam points to remember

- **Rivals are inside the market; PESTEL factors press from outside**
- **Viability** = survive and stay successful over time
- **Classify by the source** of the change — not by what it costs or touches
- **Political vs. legal:** broad policy choice vs. specific rule to follow
- **Overlap is real** — pick the best fit by the source
- **Economic factors** describe the whole economy, never one company
- **"All six equally important"** = wrong answer

# Practice — 6 questions

**3 on one stimulus (GreenBox)** · **3 standalone**

Read → pause → **commit to an answer** → then reveal.

---

## Stimulus: GreenBox

**Elena Reyes** owns **GreenBox**, a meal-prep delivery service in a mid-size city. Three developments have her attention:

1. The city council is **debating a proposal to tax sugary packaged foods**
2. A **fitness lifestyle trend** is growing among adults; **gym memberships have risen for three straight years**
3. A new **state law** will require every food delivery business to hold a **certified food-handler license** within one year

Elena is deciding whether to **expand GreenBox into two nearby towns**.

---

## Q1 — Classify the fitness trend

The growing fitness lifestyle trend and rising gym memberships are best classified as which type of PESTEL factor?

- A. Economic
- B. Social
- C. Technological
- D. Political

<!--
Pause. Ask: what is the source of this change — the economy, or what people choose to value?
-->

---

## Q1 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Social factors are trends in society and culture that affect consumers, including **lifestyle trends**. A fitness trend that changes what people want to eat is a lifestyle trend acting on consumer demand.

**Trap: A.** Rising gym memberships involve people **spending money**, and spending sounds economic. But economic factors describe the **condition of the economy itself** (income, inflation, unemployment).

> **Master rule:** classify by the **source** of the change. A change in what people choose to value is **social**, even when the choice costs money.

---

## Q2 — The paired-concept swap

The proposed tax on sugary packaged foods and the new food-handler license requirement are best classified, **respectively**, as which two PESTEL factors?

- A. Legal, then political
- B. Political, then environmental
- C. Economic, then legal
- D. Political, then legal

---

## Q2 — Answer: **D**

**Why:** A proposed **tax** is a policy choice → **political**. A specific **law** requiring a license → **legal**.

**Trap: A** — **swaps the pair.** The sorting rule: political = broad policy choice (tax/ban); legal = specific rule to follow (license/safety code).

**Trap: C** — a tax affects money, but **its source is a government policy decision**, and source decides the classification.

---

## Q3 — How to use the framework

Which best describes how Elena should use the PESTEL framework in her expansion decision?

- A. Adopt the framework only after choosing which town to enter
- B. Weigh only the factors that favor expansion
- C. Identify the relevant factors and assess how each affects the attractiveness and risks of each town
- D. Treat all six factors as equally important in every market

---

## Q3 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** The framework works by identifying factors **relevant to the specific product and market**, then assessing how each one impacts **attractiveness and risk.** The license law is a cost in every town; the fitness trend may be stronger in one town than another.

**Trap: D.** A scenario will rarely make all six matter **equally** — relevance depends on the business. A delivery service cares about a licensing law; a candy stand doesn't care about internet speeds.

> **"Treat every factor as equally weighted" sounds thorough and reads as a wrong answer.**

---

## Q4 — Reading a data table

The table shows two indicators for two regions:

| Region | Median household income | Unemployment |
|---|---|---|
| North | **+8%** over 3 years | 7% → **4%** |
| South | **−3%** over the same period | 5% → **9%** |

A business selling **premium home fitness equipment** is comparing the two regions. Based on the economic factors shown, which conclusion is **supported by the table**?

- A. Region North currently offers the more attractive economic conditions for a premium product
- B. Region South's population is declining
- C. Region North's residents value fitness more than Region South's residents
- D. The business is **guaranteed** higher profits in Region North

---

## Q4 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** Rising incomes and falling unemployment mean more households can **afford a premium product** — exactly what the numbers show.

**Traps:**
- **C** **invents a social factor** — the table shows income and unemployment and says nothing about what anyone values
- **D** uses **"guarantee"** — the absolutes rule still applies: favorable conditions raise the odds, not certainty

---

## Q5 — Solar-panel subsidies

A country's government begins offering large **subsidies** to businesses that install solar panels. Which statement best describes this in PESTEL terms?

- A. A legal factor, because subsidies are written into law
- B. An environmental factor, because solar panels affect the environment
- C. A political factor that incentivizes a specific business activity
- D. A technological factor, because solar panels are technology

---

## Q5 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** Subsidies are **policy tools** (alongside taxes, mandates, bans); their purpose is to **incentivize** an activity the government wants more of → **political**.

**Trap: B** — classify by the **source of the force**, never by the subject matter it touches. The subsidy *concerns* solar panels, but the *force acting on the market* is a government policy choice.

---

## Q6 — Fiber internet reaches rural areas

A homework-help app operates where most households have high-speed internet. A new **fiber network** then brings fast internet to **rural areas for the first time**. Which statement best describes the effect?

- A. It raises a barrier to entry that protects the app from rivals
- B. A technological factor changed, expanding the app's viable market
- C. It converts the app's service from a want into a need
- D. It is a social factor, because more people can now join online communities

---

## Q6 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Internet access is a **technological** factor. When it spreads, the set of customers a technology-dependent business can serve **grows with it** — a market that was out of reach becomes viable.

**Trap: A** — borrows Topic 1.2 vocabulary. A barrier to entry keeps **new firms out**, and wider internet access is the **opposite**: it opens the market to the app **and to future rivals alike.**

> Recognizing a term from a neighboring topic is never enough — the scenario has to match what the term means.

---

| # | Trap | Where |

- **Viability** = can this business survive here over time?
- **Classify by the source** of the change — that's the master rule
- **Factors move** — when they do, markets get rewritten
