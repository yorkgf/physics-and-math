# Part 1: Why people borrow, and from whom
### LO 3.2.A — Reasons and funding sources

---

## Four reasons consumers borrow

**3.2.A.1** Consumers typically borrow money because they want to spend on goods or services that **exceed their current income and accumulated savings** — a car, a house, college tuition.

They may also borrow:

| Reason | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **To address an emergency** | A medical bill or a car repair that cannot wait |
| **To maintain savings while purchasing** | Keep the emergency fund intact and finance the purchase instead |
| **For convenience** | A credit card for everyday spending, paid off monthly |
| **To buy something too big for current income** | The car, the house, the degree |

> **Borrowing is not automatically a mistake. The exam asks *why* someone borrowed before asking whether it was wise.**

---

## Borrowing creates a liability

**3.2.A.2** Borrowing creates a **personal liability (debt)**. Consumers must **repay the borrowed money (loans) with interest**.

**The interest rate and repayment terms vary based on four things:**

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **The lender** | A bank, a car dealer, a payday lender — each prices risk differently |
| **The type of loan** | Secured vs. unsecured changes the rate |
| **The amount borrowed** | Larger principal, larger total interest |
| **The borrower's credit history** | The strongest single signal a lender has |

---

## Secured vs. unsecured — the rate gap explained

**3.2.A.2** Loans used to buy items that can serve as **collateral** — cars and houses — are **secured loans** and typically have **lower interest rates** than general consumer loans, which are **unsecured**.

| | Secured loan | Unsecured loan |
|---|---|---|
| **Collateral** | Yes — the car or house | None |
| **If the borrower defaults** | Lender can seize the collateral | Lender must pursue repayment (collections, legal action) |
| **Risk to lender** | Lower | Higher |
| **Interest rate** | **Lower** | **Higher** |

> **The whole credit system is priced risk. Lower risk → lower rate. That one sentence explains most of this topic.**

---

## Where the money actually comes from

**3.2.A.3** Financial institutions such as **commercial banks and credit unions** make loans to consumers **and** to businesses, nonprofits, and government entities — **based on the deposits received from individual savers**, businesses, and other entities.

> **Follow the chain: Maya deposits $4,500 (3.1) → the bank lends it out (3.2) → Andre pays interest on it.**
>
> Savers and borrowers are the **two ends of the same pipe**. This is why interest rates for savers and borrowers move together.

---

## Other lenders — including the expensive ones

**3.2.A.4** Beyond banks and credit unions, consumers may borrow from:

| Lender | What it offers |
|---|---|
| **Credit card companies** | Revolving credit — borrow, repay, borrow again |
| **Retail stores** | Store credit cards, "buy now, pay later" offers |
| **Mortgage lenders** | Long-term loans secured by a house |
| **Alternative financial services** | **Payday loans, check-cashing services, instant tax refunds** |

> **"Alternative financial services" is the CED's label for the high-cost end of the market.** In a question, that phrase is a signal: higher risk to the lender, higher rate to the borrower.

---

## Consumer protection laws (3.2.A.5)

**3.2.A.5** Consumer protection laws do three things:

| Requirement | What it prevents |
|---|---|
| Lenders must communicate **credit terms clearly and explicitly** | Hidden fees and surprise rates |
| Laws **govern debt collection tactics** | Harassment and abusive collection |
| Laws **prohibit discriminatory lending practices** | Denying credit on protected characteristics rather than creditworthiness |

> **Key distinction for FRQs: consumer protection laws regulate the *disclosure and conduct*, not the *interest rate itself*.** A high rate can be legal; a hidden rate is not.

# Part 2: How a lender decides
### LO 3.2.B — Creditworthiness

---

## Lenders are pricing one thing: default risk

**3.2.B.1** Lenders face the risk that a borrower will **default** — meaning the borrower **will not repay the loan**.

So they prefer lending to individuals who pose **less risk**, for example consumers with:

| Signal | Why it lowers risk |
|---|---|
| **Low existing debt** | More income free to service a new payment |
| **High income and savings** | A buffer if income drops |
| **A history of repaying loans on time** | Evidence of behaviour, not just promises |

> **Lenders willing to lend to higher-risk individuals — including alternative financial services — typically charge higher interest rates.** The high rate *is* the risk, priced.

---

## What a lender collects (3.2.B.2)

To determine **creditworthiness**, lenders gather:

| Input | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Income** | Can this person afford a payment? |
| **Savings** | Is there a cushion if income stops? |
| **Existing debt** | How much of the income is already committed? |
| **Credit reports** | How has this person handled credit in the past? |

> **Three of these are about *capacity*. The fourth — credit reports — is about *character/history*. Exam questions often test that split.**

---

## Credit reports come from credit bureaus

**3.2.B.3** **Credit reports** are created by **credit bureaus** (also called credit reporting agencies), which **collect information each time a consumer interacts with a financial institution** — for example:

- opening a **savings account**
- securing a **bank loan**
- applying for a **credit card**
- making a **payment**

> **Note the first item: even opening a savings account creates a record.** The report is built from *interactions*, not only from borrowing.

---

## The credit score, and who gets to see it

**3.2.B.4** A credit report includes a **credit score** reflecting the consumer's **past use of credit**.

These reports **can be shared with**:

| Who | Why they want it |
|---|---|
| **Potential lenders** | Will this borrower repay? |
| **Potential employers** | (In some roles) is this person reliable with responsibility? |
| **Potential landlords** | Will rent be paid on time? |
| **Insurance companies** | Risk assessment |
| **Government agencies** | Legal and regulatory purposes |

> **This is the point students underestimate: a credit score is not just a borrowing number. It can affect housing, employment, and insurance.**

---

## Your turn — read the two applicants

> **Applicant A:** income $4,200/month, savings $9,000, existing debt $300/month, credit score 780, 6 years of on-time payments.
>
> **Applicant B:** income $4,600/month, savings $0, existing debt $1,450/month, credit score 610, two late payments in the last year.
>
> Both apply for the same $12,000 car loan.

**Who gets the lower rate, and using which 3.2.B.1 signals?** Then: **what could B do to improve the terms?**

---

## Your turn — answer

**Applicant A gets the lower rate**, despite earning less.

| Signal (3.2.B.1) | A | B |
|---|---|---|
| Existing debt | $300/month — low | $1,450/month — high, much of the income is committed |
| Savings | $9,000 — buffer exists | $0 — no cushion |
| Repayment history | 6 years on time | Two late payments → default risk |

**For B (this is 3.2.C):** pay down high-interest balances, pay every bill on time, minimize credit card use, and make a **down payment** to shrink the loan — all of which lower the lender's risk and therefore the rate.

> **Creditworthiness is not "how much you earn." It is "how likely you are to repay."**

# Part 3: Managing debt
### LO 3.2.C — Strategies that actually work

---

## Why high debt hurts (3.2.C.1)

**3.2.C.1** High levels of debt can harm a consumer's financial condition because **payments on loans reduce the amount of income available to save or use for other expenses**.

And: **larger debts and/or higher interest rates require higher monthly payments.**

> **The compounding trap:** a higher payment leaves less to save → less savings means the next emergency becomes new debt → the payment rises again. Debt is a feedback loop, not a one-time number.

---

## Why repayment fails (3.2.C.2)

**3.2.C.2** Borrowers may face difficulty repaying loans for a variety of reasons:

| Cause | Example |
|---|---|
| **Loss of income** | Job loss, reduced hours, illness |
| **Monthly payments that exceed the consumer's ability to pay** | The payment was affordable at signing, but not after a change |

> **Exam distinction: the *first* cause is external (income changed). The *second* is structural (the payment never fit). Both appear in FRQs — name which one the scenario shows.**

---

## Three moves that manage the impact of debt (3.2.C.3)

**3.2.C.3** Borrowers can manage the impact of debt on their finances by:

| Move | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Maintaining a high credit score** | Unlocks better terms on future borrowing |
| **Seeking better terms on loans** | Refinancing or negotiating lowers the payment |
| **Repaying high-interest-rate loans as quickly as possible** | Credit card debt costs the most per dollar owed |

> **Why high-interest first?** Every dollar repaid on a 24% card is a *guaranteed* 24% return. No investment reliably beats that — this is the mathematical core of debt strategy.

---

## Improving the score (3.2.C.4)

**3.2.C.4** Strategies to improve an individual's credit score:

| Strategy | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Paying bills on time** | Payment history is direct evidence of repayment behaviour |
| **Paying off existing debt** | Lowers the debt-to-income burden the lender sees |
| **Minimizing use of credit cards** | Less revolving balance, less risk signal |

> **Notice what is *not* on the list: closing old accounts, or "checking your score often."** Answer only what the CED lists.

---

## Better terms: compare, and put money down (3.2.C.5)

**3.2.C.5** Borrowers can obtain better terms — **lower interest rates and lower fees** — by:

1. **Comparing the terms offered by different lenders**
2. **Making down payments** on major purchases like a home or car

**A down payment** requires using some income or **saved money** to pay part of the cost of the purchase.

**Andre's car, three scenarios:**

| Down payment | Loan amount | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| $0 | $14,000 | Highest principal, highest total interest |
| $2,000 | $12,000 | Lower principal **and** a lower rate (less risk) |
| $4,000 | $10,000 | Lowest principal and rate — and the monthly payment may fit |

> **A down payment does double work: it shrinks the loan *and* improves the price of the loan.**

---

## When it is unmanageable: assistance and bankruptcy (3.2.C.6)

**3.2.C.6** Individuals who face **negative consequences from unmanageable debt** — such as **property seizures** — may be able to seek **debt management assistance**.

In some cases, borrowers may seek **bankruptcy** — a legal process that **eliminates some debts** and **helps the borrower set up a repayment plan** for other debts.

| Option | What it does | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Debt management assistance** | Restructures and negotiates payments | Fees; still repays most debt |
| **Bankruptcy** | Legally eliminates some debts, sets a repayment plan for others | Severe, long-lasting credit damage |

> **Order matters: assistance first, bankruptcy last.** In an FRQ, recommending bankruptcy as a first step is a scoring error.

---

## 🔑 Exam points to remember — Borrowing

| # | Point |
|---|-------|
| 1 | Consumers borrow to buy what exceeds current income and savings, for emergencies, to preserve savings, or for convenience |
| 2 | Borrowing creates a **liability**; the rate depends on the **lender, loan type, amount, and credit history** |
| 3 | **Secured loans** (car, house) are backed by collateral → **lower** rates; **unsecured** → higher rates |
| 4 | Banks and credit unions lend **out of deposits from savers** — savers and borrowers are two ends of one pipe |
| 5 | **Alternative financial services** = payday loans, check-cashing, instant tax refunds — the high-cost end |
| 6 | Consumer protection laws govern **disclosure, collection tactics, and discrimination** — not the rate level itself |
| 7 | Lenders price **default risk**: low existing debt, high income/savings, on-time history → better terms |
| 8 | **Credit reports are built by credit bureaus from every interaction** — including opening a savings account |
| 9 | Credit reports can be seen by **lenders, employers, landlords, insurers, and government agencies** |
| 10 | Manage debt by **protecting the score, seeking better terms, and killing high-interest debt first** |
| 11 | Improve the score by paying on time, paying off debt, and minimizing card use |
| 12 | **Down payments** shrink the principal **and** improve the rate |
| 13 | Order of escalation: **debt management assistance → bankruptcy**, never the reverse |

## Three exam tells — borrowing

**Tell 1 — "Who bears the risk?"** Every rate question reduces to risk allocation. Collateral, income, savings, and payment history all lower the lender's risk — and therefore the rate. If an option raises the lender's risk, the rate goes **up**.

**Tell 2 — "Which numbers?"** Creditworthiness = **income + savings + existing debt + credit report**. Not "how nice the person is," not "how much they want the car."

**Tell 3 — "What is being asked: why, or what to do?"** 3.2.A asks **why** someone borrowed. 3.2.C asks **what strategy to recommend**. A 3.2.C answer that describes the cause of debt without recommending an action earns little.

## Practice — Stimulus

**Priya** earns **$2,800/month** after tax. She has:
- a **$3,600 credit card balance at 24%**, paying the minimum
- a **$9,000 used-car loan at 7%**, secured by the car
- **$400** in savings

She is considering a **payday loan** to cover a $600 car repair, and has been offered a **store credit card at 29%** for new furniture. Her credit score is **640**. She has never missed a payment.

---

## Practice — Q1

> Why is Priya's car loan charged at 7% while her credit card balance costs 24%?

> A. Car loans are always smaller than credit card balances
> B. The car loan is secured by collateral, lowering the lender's risk
> C. Credit card companies are required by law to charge more
> D. The car loan has a shorter repayment period

---

## Practice — Q1 answer

**Answer: B.**

A car loan is a **secured loan** — the car serves as collateral and can be seized on default. That lowers the lender's risk, so the rate is lower (3.2.A.2). A credit card balance is **unsecured**.

**Traps:**
- **A** — loan size affects total interest, not the rate comparison the CED is making
- **C** — consumer protection laws govern **disclosure and conduct**, not required rate levels
- **D** — term length is not the reason secured loans are cheaper; collateral is

> **Risk, not size, sets the rate.**

---

## Practice — Q2

> Priya's credit score is 640 despite never missing a payment. Which action is most likely to improve her score, according to 3.2.C.4?

> A. Close her credit card account immediately
> B. Pay down the $3,600 credit card balance and keep card use minimal
> C. Take the store credit card to build more credit history
> D. Increase her car loan balance to show she can handle larger loans

---

## Practice — Q2 answer

**Answer: B.**

**3.2.C.4** lists exactly three strategies: **pay bills on time, pay off existing debt, and minimize use of credit cards**. A high revolving balance relative to income is a risk signal even when payments are on time.

**Traps:**
- **A** — closing accounts is not one of the CED strategies, and it reduces available credit
- **C** — adding a 29% card increases revolving capacity and risk signal
- **D** — borrowing more raises the debt burden; it is the opposite of the listed strategy

> **Answer from the CED list. Invented strategies are the most common 3.2.C error.**

---

## Practice — Q3

> Priya takes a payday loan for the $600 repair. Which statement best explains why this is a poor decision?

> A. Payday loans are illegal in the United States
> B. Alternative financial services serve higher-risk borrowers and typically charge higher rates
> C. Payday loans cannot be reported to credit bureaus
> D. Payday loans are only available to businesses

---

## Practice — Q3 answer

**Answer: B.**

**3.2.A.4** classifies payday loans as **alternative financial services**, and **3.2.B.1** notes that lenders serving higher-risk individuals **typically charge higher interest rates**. The rate is the price of that risk.

**Traps:**
- **A** — they are legal (though regulated); the problem is cost, not legality
- **C** — not the CED's stated reason, and credit reporting is not what makes it costly
- **D** — payday loans are consumer loans, not business loans

> **Identify the category first ("alternative financial services"), then the consequence (higher rate).**

---

## Practice — Q4

> Priya's income is cut to $2,200/month when her hours are reduced. She cannot cover the credit card minimum plus the car loan. Which recommendation best fits 3.2.C?

> A. Declare bankruptcy immediately
> B. Ignore the credit card payments until income recovers
> C. Seek better terms, prioritise the highest-interest debt, and seek debt management assistance if payments remain unmanageable
> D. Take a second payday loan to cover the shortfall

---

## Practice — Q4 answer

**Answer: C.**

The escalation in 3.2.C is clear: **maintain the score → seek better terms → repay high-interest debt first → debt management assistance → bankruptcy as a last resort**. Priya's problem is **loss of income** (3.2.C.2), so the response is to reduce the payment burden, not to abandon it.

**Traps:**
- **A** — bankruptcy is a last resort, not a first move; it also does not fix the income problem
- **B** — missing payments damages the score, which raises the cost of every future loan
- **D** — borrowing at the highest rate to service existing debt is how a debt spiral starts

> **Order of escalation is itself a scoring point.**

---

## Trap patterns to watch

| # | Trap type | Where it appears |
|---|-----------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Rate = loan size** — the rate is set by risk, not by the amount | Q1 |
| 2 | **Secured vs. unsecured** — collateral lowers the rate | Q1 |
| 3 | **Invented credit-score strategies** — use only the three in 3.2.C.4 | Q2 |
| 4 | **"Alternative financial services" is neutral-sounding** — it means high-cost lending | Q3 |
| 5 | **Bankruptcy as a first step** — it is the last resort, after assistance | Q4 |
| 6 | **Cause vs. strategy** — 3.2.C.2 explains why repayment fails; 3.2.C.3–C.6 says what to do | Q4 |
| 7 | **Credit score = borrowing only** — employers, landlords, and insurers can see reports too | 3.2.B.4 |

## Wrap-up — 3.2 Borrowing, Credit, and Debt

**What we covered:**
- Consumers borrow to buy beyond current income and savings, for emergencies, to preserve savings, or for convenience
- Borrowing creates a **liability**; the rate depends on the **lender, loan type, amount, and credit history**
- **Secured loans are cheaper than unsecured** — collateral lowers the lender's risk
- Banks and credit unions **lend out savers' deposits** — the two sides of Unit 3 connect here
- Lenders assess **creditworthiness** from income, savings, existing debt, and **credit reports** built by **credit bureaus**
- A **credit score** follows you to lenders, **employers, landlords, and insurers**
- Debt is managed by **protecting the score, seeking better terms, and repaying high-interest debt first**
- **Down payments** shrink the principal and improve the rate
- When debt is unmanageable: **debt management assistance first, bankruptcy last**
