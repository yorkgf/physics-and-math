# Part 1: Why cash needs its own statement
### LO 3.8.A — Definition and purpose

---

## The cash flow statement, defined (3.8.A.1)

**3.8.A.1** A **cash flow statement** is a financial statement used by businesses to show **how cash inflows and outflows impact a business's cash balance over the course of a financial reporting period**.

| | **Income statement** | **Cash flow statement** |
|---|---|---|
| **Question** | Did we earn a profit? | Did **cash** go up or down? |
| **Timing** | Revenue when **earned**, expenses when **incurred** | Cash only when it **moves** |
| **A profitable month with unpaid invoices** | Looks good | Looks bad |

> **The two statements can disagree, and both are correct.** Profit is an accounting measure of performance; cash is a physical fact.

---

## What businesses watch cash *for* (3.8.A.2)

**3.8.A.2** Businesses **closely monitor cash balances** to ensure there is **sufficient cash available** to:

| Obligation | Why it cannot wait |
|---|---|
| **Pay recurring expenses** — payroll, rent | Due on a fixed date, every period |
| **Repay lenders** | Missed payments trigger default |
| **Manage unforeseen expenses** | A broken machine, a burst pipe |

> **Note the third item: unforeseen expenses are exactly what a *reserve* is for (3.1's emergency fund, in business form).**

# Part 2: Inflows and outflows
### LO 3.8.A.3 / 3.8.A.4

---

## Cash inflows (3.8.A.3)

**3.8.A.3** **Cash inflows increase a business's cash balance.** They typically include:

| Inflow | DK Coffee Lab, Q1 |
|---|---|
| **Payments from customers** | $84,000 |
| **Interest or dividends earned on investment assets** | $60 |
| **Proceeds received from the sale of a business's assets** | $800 (old grinder) |
| **Infusions of financial capital** — money raised by taking out a new loan | $12,000 (equipment loan) |
| **Total inflows** | **$96,860** |

> **Note item 3: selling an asset is an inflow. Note item 4: borrowing is an inflow — a loan brings cash *in* now and creates an outflow later.**

---

## Cash outflows (3.8.A.4)

**3.8.A.4** **Cash outflows decrease a business's cash balance.** They typically include:

| Outflow | DK Coffee Lab, Q1 |
|---|---|
| **Payments to employees and suppliers** | $48,000 |
| **Interest expense paid on existing loans** | $300 |
| **Taxes paid to the government** | $2,000 |
| **Money spent to purchase assets** | $9,000 (espresso machine) |
| **Money spent on debt repayment** | $3,000 |
| **Dividends** | $2,000 |
| **Total outflows** | **$64,300** |

> **Three of these are easy to miss: interest paid, taxes paid, and dividends. All three are real cash leaving the business.**

---

## Inflows and outflows, side by side

| **Inflows (+)** | **Outflows (−)** |
|---|---|
| Payments from customers | Payments to employees and suppliers |
| Interest or dividends **earned** on investments | Interest **paid** on loans |
| Proceeds from selling assets | Money spent to **purchase** assets |
| New loans / infusions of capital | Debt **repayment** |
| | Taxes paid |
| | Dividends |

**Three pairs that look alike and are opposites:** interest *earned* vs. interest *paid*; selling an asset vs. buying an asset; taking a loan vs. repaying a loan.

> **The pairs are the traps. Read the verb: earned/paid, sell/buy, borrow/repay.**

---

## DK Coffee Lab's quarter

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Beginning cash balance | $22,000 |
| + Total inflows | $96,860 |
| − Total outflows | ($64,300) |
| **= Net change in cash** | **+$32,560** |
| **= Ending cash balance** | **$54,560** |

> **Structure: beginning balance → inflows → outflows → net change → ending balance. The ending balance must equal the cash line on the balance sheet (3.7) for the same date.**

# Part 3: Reading the result
### LO 3.8.A.5 / 3.8.B — Positive, negative, and what it means

---

## Cash flow can go either way (3.8.A.5)

**3.8.A.5** A business's cash flow **may be positive or negative** at the end of a period.

**DK Coffee Lab's next quarter:** inflows $71,000, outflows $78,500 → **net change −$7,500**, ending cash $47,060.

> **Negative cash flow is not automatically failure — it may be a deliberate investment (buying equipment, building inventory). It becomes a problem when it threatens the ability to pay what is due.**

---

## Who uses it, and for what (3.8.B.1)

**3.8.B.1** The cash flow statement is used to assess a business's **ability to meet its financial obligations**, including its ability to pay:

| Obligation | Whose claim |
|---|---|
| **Employees** | Wages |
| **Suppliers** | Accounts payable |
| **Creditors** | Interest and loan repayment |
| **Shareholders** | Dividends |

> **Four stakeholders, in that order of urgency. Payroll and suppliers come before shareholders — a business that cannot pay wages is in immediate trouble, no matter what the income statement says.**

---

## The central warning (3.8.B.2)

**3.8.B.2** **Negative cash flow may signal that a business is unable to pay its current expenses**, which could lead to **shut down or bankruptcy even if net income is positive**.

**In such a situation, the business may**:

| Remedy | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Raise more funds** | A new loan or equity injection |
| **Collect accounts receivable faster** | Shorten payment terms, follow up, offer a discount for early payment |
| **Obtain better terms from suppliers and lenders** | Longer payment windows, renegotiated repayment schedules |

> **"Even if net income is positive" is the sentence this whole topic exists to teach. Memorise it — the exam quotes it back to you.**

---

## Why profit and cash diverge — three causes

| Cause | Example at DK Coffee Lab |
|---|---|
| **Sales on credit** | A $6,000 catering invoice is revenue today, cash in 45 days |
| **Large asset purchases** | $9,000 for an espresso machine — cash out, but only depreciation hits the income statement |
| **Debt principal repayment** | $3,000 leaves the bank, but principal repayment is **not an expense** on the income statement |

> **Interest is an expense; principal repayment is not. That single distinction explains a large share of profit-versus-cash gaps.**

| Interest received | 40 | Suppliers | 21,000 |
| Sale of roaster | 2,500 | Interest paid | 420 |

## 🔑 Exam points to remember — Cash flow statement

| # | Point |
|---|-------|
| 1 | A **cash flow statement** shows how **cash inflows and outflows** affect the **cash balance over a financial reporting period** |
| 2 | Businesses monitor cash to pay **recurring expenses (payroll, rent)**, **repay lenders**, and **manage unforeseen expenses** |
| 3 | **Inflows**: payments from customers, **interest or dividends earned on investments**, **proceeds from selling assets**, **infusions of capital (new loans)** |
| 4 | **Outflows**: payments to **employees and suppliers**, **interest paid**, **taxes paid**, **purchases of assets**, **debt repayment**, **dividends** |
| 5 | Cash flow may be **positive or negative** at the end of a period |
| 6 | The statement assesses the ability to pay **employees, suppliers, creditors, and shareholders** |
| 7 | **Negative cash flow can force shutdown or bankruptcy even when net income is positive** |
| 8 | Remedies: **raise more funds**, **collect receivables faster**, **obtain better terms from suppliers and lenders** |

---

## Three exam tells — cash flow

**Tell 1 — "Is cash moving?"** Revenue earned on credit, depreciation, and accrued expenses do **not** move cash. If no cash changes hands this period, it is not on this statement.

**Tell 2 — "Inflow or outflow?"** Read the verb. *Earned* interest is an inflow; *paid* interest is an outflow. *Selling* an asset is an inflow; *buying* one is an outflow. *Taking* a loan is an inflow; *repaying* it is an outflow.

**Tell 3 — "Profitable but broke — why?"** The answer always involves timing (credit sales, receivables), big asset purchases, or debt principal. Name the mechanism, not just "they spent too much."

## Practice — Stimulus

**Ridgeway Bike Repair** reports **net income of $18,000** for the year. Yet its cash balance fell from **$31,000** to **$4,000**.

The year included:

- a **$40,000** service contract with a delivery company, billed on **90-day terms** and unpaid at year end
- **$25,000** spent on new diagnostic equipment
- **$15,000** of loan **principal** repaid
- **$6,000** of dividends paid

---

## Practice — Q1

> Which of Ridgeway's transactions is a **cash inflow**?

> A. The $40,000 service contract billed on 90-day terms
> B. $25,000 spent on diagnostic equipment
> C. $15,000 of loan principal repaid
> D. None of the above

---

## Practice — Q1 answer

**Answer: D.**

None of these brings cash **in**. The service contract was **billed**, not collected — no cash has moved (3.8.A.3 requires *payments from customers*). The equipment purchase and loan repayment are both **outflows** (3.8.A.4).

**Traps:**
- **A** — revenue earned on credit is income, not an inflow; the cash arrives when the customer pays
- **B** — buying an asset is an outflow (money spent to purchase assets)
- **C** — repaying debt is an outflow (money spent on debt repayment)

> **"Billed" is the tell. Revenue ≠ cash.**

---

## Practice — Q2

> Which statement best explains how Ridgeway can be profitable yet see cash fall from $31,000 to $4,000?

> A. The income statement must be wrong, because profit and cash cannot differ
> B. Profits were earned on credit and cash was spent on equipment, debt principal, and dividends
> C. Depreciation of the new equipment reduced the cash balance
> D. Paying dividends always causes negative cash flow

---

## Practice — Q2 answer

**Answer: B.**

**3.8.B.2**: negative cash flow can occur **even if net income is positive**. The mechanisms here are exactly the three causes of divergence: **sales on credit** ($40,000 unbilled cash), a **large asset purchase** ($25,000), and **debt principal repayment** ($15,000) — plus dividends.

**Traps:**
- **A** — the statements measure different things; both can be correct simultaneously
- **C** — depreciation is a **non-cash** expense; it never moves cash
- **D** — dividends are an outflow, but they are not automatically the cause

> **Name the mechanism: timing, asset purchase, or principal repayment.**

---

## Practice — Q3

> Ridgeway's largest customer owes $40,000 on 90-day terms. Which remedy from 3.8.B.2 addresses the immediate problem most directly?

> A. Raise a new loan to cover payroll
> B. Collect accounts receivable faster — for example, shorter terms or a discount for early payment
> C. Buy more equipment to increase capacity
> D. Pay the dividends earlier

---

## Practice — Q3 answer

**Answer: B.**

**3.8.B.2** lists three remedies: **raise more funds**, **collect accounts receivable faster**, and **obtain better terms from suppliers and lenders**. The $40,000 receivable is the direct cause of the squeeze, so accelerating collection addresses it at the source.

**Traps:**
- **A** — a valid remedy and would work, but it adds debt rather than solving the timing problem
- **C** — more equipment increases outflows; it is the opposite of the fix
- **D** — paying dividends earlier accelerates an outflow

> **Fix the cause, not the symptom — and prefer the remedy that matches the diagnosis.**

---

## Practice — Q4

> Which group correctly lists **only** cash outflows under 3.8.A.4?

> A. Payments to suppliers, interest paid, taxes paid, dividends
> B. Payments from customers, interest earned, sale of assets, new loans
> C. Interest paid, depreciation, dividends, taxes paid
> D. Purchase of assets, debt repayment, sale of assets, payments to employees

---

## Practice — Q4 answer

**Answer: A.**

All four — payments to suppliers, interest paid, taxes paid, dividends — are listed as outflows in **3.8.A.4**.

**Traps:**
- **B** — all four are **inflows** (3.8.A.3)
- **C** — **depreciation is not a cash flow at all**; it is a non-cash expense
- **D** — the **sale** of an asset is an inflow, not an outflow

> **Depreciation is the most-tested non-cash item in this topic.**

---

## Trap patterns to watch

| # | Trap type | Where it appears |
|---|-----------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Credit sales counted as inflow** — only collected payments count | Q1 |
| 2 | **Depreciation treated as an outflow** — it is non-cash | Q2, Q4 |
| 3 | **Profit and cash assumed to agree** | Q2 |
| 4 | **Any single outflow blamed** for negative cash flow | Q2 |
| 5 | **Principal repayment called an expense** — it is an outflow, not an expense | Q2, Your turn |
| 6 | **"Raise a loan" always the answer** — match the remedy to the diagnosis | Q3 |
| 7 | **Interest earned vs. interest paid** — opposite directions | Q4 |
| 8 | **Selling vs. buying an asset** — opposite directions | Q4 |

## Wrap-up — 3.8 The Cash Flow Statement

**What we covered:**
- A **cash flow statement** shows how **cash inflows and outflows** change the **cash balance over a reporting period**
- Businesses watch cash to pay **recurring expenses (payroll, rent)**, **repay lenders**, and **handle the unforeseen**
- **Inflows**: customer payments, interest or dividends **earned**, **proceeds from selling assets**, **new loans / capital infusions**
- **Outflows**: payments to **employees and suppliers**, **interest paid**, **taxes paid**, **asset purchases**, **debt repayment**, **dividends**
- Cash flow may be **positive or negative**
- Stakeholders use it to judge the ability to pay **employees, suppliers, creditors, and shareholders**
- **Negative cash flow can force shutdown or bankruptcy even when net income is positive**
- Remedies: **raise more funds**, **collect receivables faster**, **get better terms from suppliers and lenders**
