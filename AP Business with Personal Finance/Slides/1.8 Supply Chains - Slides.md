# 1.8 Supply Chains
### AP Business with Personal Finance · Unit 1

Lesson deck — **concepts + practice**

---

| Distinguish **artisan** from **mass production** and explain the factors that determine the choice | LO 1.8.A |
| Describe the stages of a **supply chain** for a good and for a service | LO 1.8.B |

## Opening question

You know who owns the business (1.7), who does what, and how the departments work.

> **But where does the product *actually come from* — and how does it get to the customer?**

Sunday-night inventory count. The candy seller flips a chocolate bar over and realizes **he has no idea where it comes from.** Cocoa grew on a farm in another hemisphere. A factory melted, molded and wrapped it. A warehouse held it. A truck moved it. **Only then did his backpack enter the story.**

---

## Artisan or mass production

Before anything moves through a chain, somebody has to make it. Two poles:

| | Artisan | Mass production |
|---|---|---|
| Relies on | **skilled labor** + close attention to detail | **technology, assembly lines, machinery** |
| Output | **smaller** quantities | **far larger** quantities |
| At the park | The **cookie parent** — hours of skilled piping per batch, two dozen cookies, premium price | The **chocolate bars** — machines poured thousands of identical bars in the same time |

> **Quality can come from either pole.** Artisan does not mean better, mass-produced does not mean worse — classify by **how the product is made.**

---

## Three inputs determine the choice

| Input | The rule | The cookie parent's read |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Customer priorities** | Buyers who prize **customization** lean artisan; buyers who prize **low price** lean mass production | Parents at the park pay extra for birthday-worthy cookies |
| **2. Core competencies** (1.5) | A business built on hand skill should not pretend to be a factory | Her skill is decoration, not factory logistics |
| **3. Competitive landscape** (1.2) | Entering a market of cheap mass-produced rivals with a mid-priced product satisfies nobody | No rival at the park can copy her piping |

> **All three pointed the same direction.** Artisan was right not because artisan is "better," but because it matched her customers, her skills and her market.

## The supply chain for a good

A **supply chain** connects everyone involved at **each stage of producing and distributing a product** — from raw materials to the customer.

| Stage | What happens | The chocolate bar |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Raw materials & component parts** | Inputs are acquired | Cocoa farm, sugar, dairy, wrapper maker |
| 2. **Manufacturing facility** | Workers and equipment make finished goods | The factory melts, molds and wraps |
| 3. **Storage facility (warehouse)** | Finished goods wait | Pallets in a regional warehouse |
| 4. **Distribution center / retail store** | Goods are put in front of customers | Truck to the supermarket shelf |
| 5. **Customer** | The last link in the chain | The candy seller, and his backpack |

---

## Case clip — Ford 1913 (1.8.B.2)

**12 hours per car, then 93 minutes on a moving line; $850 became $260.** Ford is 1.8.A and 1.8.B in one story: the **mass-production** pole, and a chain built for **cost-cutting through scale** — fixed costs spread over more cars.

- **1950s Toyota** — just-in-time: no inventory, so **one late part stops the line**
- **1962 Walmart** — warehouses become **distribution centers**: days to hours
- **1990s globalization** — four continents per product: **length is risk**
- **2000s Amazon** — distribution sold as a **service**; **2020s COVID** — no backup, chains break

## Local, regional, or global — and the risk of length

| Chain | At the park | Failure points | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Local** | The cookie parent — flour from the neighborhood bakery, baked at home, sold 500 meters away | Few — one supplier, a short drive | Lower |
| **Regional** | The snack box — chips from a regional distributor, trucked across the metro area | Moderate — multiple suppliers, regional trucking | Medium |
| **Global** | The chocolate bar — cocoa from West Africa, factory in Europe, sold at a US school | Many — overseas suppliers, shipping, customs, currency | Higher |

> **The longer the chain, the more links can break.** Risk is the factor that hides, and it hides in the **length** of the chain itself: every additional stage adds a link that can fail.

## Warehouse vs. distribution center

| | Warehouse | Distribution center |
|---|---|---|
| Primary function | **Storage** | **Stores and moves onward** |
| Goods leave… | When someone comes to collect | On their way to stores and customers |
| At the park | The supermarket's back room: pallets sit until a shelf needs restocking | A regional distributor takes pallets from the factory and **forwards** them to stores |

> **A warehouse waits; a distribution center relays.** When the exam offers both terms, sort by **what happens to the goods next.**

---

## Supplier vs. distributor

| | Supplier | Distributor |
|---|---|---|
| What it moves | Materials or goods from one business to another | **Finished products** from manufacturers to retail stores |
| Sits where | Early in the chain — the cocoa farm feeding the factory | Between manufacturing and retail — the trucks and logistics company |

> **A supplier feeds production; a distributor feeds the retail shelf.** The factory cannot make bars without a supplier; the supermarket cannot stock them without a distributor. Sort by **where they sit in the chain.**

---

## The supply chain for a service

A service business acquires the **employees, the resources and the delivery systems** needed to provide the service — that list *is* its chain.

| Example | People | Resources | Delivery system |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tutoring company (virtual) | Tutors | Lesson materials | Video platform |
| Lawn-care crew (in-person) | Trained workers | Mowers, trimmers | App that routes the trucks |
| **The snack box** | Seller + cousin | Coolers, ice, snacks | Group-chat order + halftime handoff |

> **No factory, no warehouse, no truck** — hiring, equipping and delivering **is** the chain. Name all three parts.

**The service supply chain (1.8.B.3).** The platform makes nothing; it sells the delivery itself.

| Employees | Resources | Delivery system |
|---|---|---|

## Five supplier factors — and the backup plan

Every link in a chain is a choice, weighed on **five factors: cost, quality, efficiency, convenience** — and **risk**, the one that **hides** (disasters, political instability, shortages, production errors, supplier reputation).

| Option | Cost | Quality | Efficiency | Convenience | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Supermarket** | Pricier | Same bars | Always stocked | Down the street | **Never misses** |
| **Wholesale parent** | Cheaper (bulk) | Same bars | Larger quantities | Needs a ride | Van **broke down twice** |

He **splits the order:** the wholesaler carries the bulk, a Friday-night supermarket run **backstops the risk.**

> **A supplier answer names all five factors, prices in the risk, and includes a backup plan.** And remember: **buying inputs is not outsourcing** — every producer buys raw materials.

---

## Worked example — turn the hidden risk into arithmetic

Same case, now with numbers. **Assumption:** 200 kg of wax a month, 300 candles at $12 profit each.

| | Overseas supplier | Local supplier |
|---|---|---|
| Price per kg | **$1.76** (20% below local) | $2.20 |
| Shipments on time | 4 of 6 | 6 of 6 |
| One accident (it happened) | **−$2,700** missed sales, **−$600** scrapped, +$300 freight | would not happen |
| **The honest comparison** | saves **$88/month**, carries a **$3,600** accident | costs **$88/month** more |

> **Split the order — 60% overseas, 40% local.** The extra 40% costs about **$35 a month**, roughly **1% of one accident:** that $35 is **the price of the backup plan.**

## The sentence that banks every point

✍️ **Say it out loud with the pork numbers, then again with the HiSilicon one.** That is the whole of 1.8.B.4.

> She chooses the cheaper supplier on **cost**, but the risk of a named disruption (production error / shortage / political instability) means shipments **arrive late** and **costs rise**, which threatens the **competitive advantage** and profit — so she **splits the order** and keeps a **local backup.**

## Strategy shapes the chain

A business's **competitive advantage strategy decides what kind of chain it builds.**

| Strategy | Chain design | At the park | Advantage it creates |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low prices** | Mass production and cost-cutting | Bulk buying from the wholesale parent | Prices rivals struggle to **match** |
| **High quality** | High-quality resources and methods | Premium wax for the cookie parent | Goods rivals struggle to **imitate** |
| **Barriers to entry** | Exclusive or restrictive agreements | The only-vendor permit for Saturday | Rivals left hunting for **supply** |

> **Name the strategy, then show the mechanism:** a cheaper input, a better input, or a locked-up input. **The chain itself becomes the advantage.**

---

## Case clip — Zara (1.8.C.2)

**A new design reaches a store shelf in 2 weeks; the industry average is 6 months.** That one number decided every link: design kept in-house in Spain, fabric bought in advance and undyed so the colour is chosen later, **own factories instead of cheaper Asia**, one central facility at Arteixo through which every garment in the world passes and ships within hours.

- **Sacrificed:** cost. **Bought:** efficiency and control — the five factors ranked by strategy
- **A relay, not a storage room:** the warehouse-vs-distribution-center distinction made real
- **The chain is the barrier:** a dress is copied in days, a 2-week chain is not

## Worked example — food delivery: three strategies

| Strategy | What the chain is built to do | What it sacrifices | Advantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low prices** | Many orders on one courier: batching spreads the courier's hour across more orders | The delivery window — "your order waits for the next stop" | Prices rivals struggle to **match** |
| **High quality** | One courier, one order: dedicated couriers, temperature-controlled gear | Cost per order — several times higher | A service rivals struggle to **imitate** |
| **Barriers to entry** | **Exclusive agreements** with merchants: list only on us, or lose the placement | Merchant goodwill, plus legal scrutiny (1.3) | Rivals left hunting for **supply** |

> 💡 **Same people, same resources, same delivery system — the strategy decides what to buy and what to give up:** which input the chain **locks up**, **upgrades**, or **refuses to pay for**.

## Scaling

**Scaling:** a higher-capacity chain, where **revenue outruns cost** because **fixed costs spread.**

| | Before scaling | After scaling |
|---|---|---|
| Units | 20,000 | 40,000 |
| Revenue | $80,000 | $160,000 (**+100%**) |
| Total costs | $60,000 | $96,000 (**+60%**) |
| **Profit** | **$20,000** | **$64,000** |

> **Revenue grew 100%, costs 60% — that gap is the entire point of scaling.** The oven and permits stay fixed, so doubling the batches pays for them twice.

---

## Three question shapes

| Shape | What it asks | How to answer |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Order the stages** | Raw materials to customer | Warehouse comes **after manufacturing, before distribution** |
| **2. Choose a supplier** | Weigh the options | Weigh **all five factors**, with **risk priced in**, and name a **backup plan** |
| **3. Match strategy to chain** | Which design fits | Low price to cost-cutting; quality to better inputs; barriers to exclusive agreements |

> **Name the shape first, and the question mostly solves itself.**

# Practice — 6 questions

**4 on one stimulus (Copper Kettle Candles)** · **2 standalone**

Read to yourself, pause, **commit to an answer** — then advance.

---

## Stimulus: Copper Kettle Candles

**Nadia Farouk** owns **Copper Kettle Candles**, pouring small batches by hand with premium wax and charging approximately **twice what machine-made candles cost.** Customers praise the quality and the custom scents.

Nadia buys wax from an **overseas supplier** at a price **20% below local** — but **2 of the last 6 shipments arrived weeks late**, and one contained **the wrong blend.** A boutique chain offers to **feature Copper Kettle in 12 stores** if Nadia signs an **exclusive retail rights agreement.**

> **Watch for three decision points:** production approach, supplier choice, and the exclusive agreement.

---

## Q1 — Classify production approach

Nadia's method of pouring small batches by hand with premium wax is best described as which?

- A. Mass production, because she sells in multiple locations
- B. Artisan production, because she relies on skilled labor and attention to detail in smaller quantities
- C. Outsourcing, because she buys wax from an overseas supplier
- D. A service supply chain, because she provides custom scents

---

## Q1 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Artisan production relies on **skilled labor** and close attention to detail, producing **smaller quantities.** Nadia pours by hand, uses premium materials and makes small batches — every marker points to artisan.

**Trap: C** — **buying inputs is not outsourcing.** Every producer buys raw materials: Nadia makes the candles herself and buys wax the way a baker buys flour.

**Trap: A** — selling in multiple locations does not change the **production method.** She could sell in 100 stores and still pour every candle by hand.

---

## Q2 — Supplier decision

Nadia's overseas wax supplier offers a price 20% below local, but 2 of 6 recent shipments arrived late and one had the wrong blend. Which analysis **best** supports a sound supplier decision?

- A. Choose the overseas supplier because lower cost always increases profit
- B. Choose the local supplier because reliability is the only factor that matters
- C. Weigh the lower cost against the risks — late deliveries, wrong shipments, damage to reputation — and consider a backup plan
- D. Choose the overseas supplier because customers will never know about delivery problems

---

## Q2 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** The five supplier factors are cost, quality, efficiency, convenience and **risk.** The overseas supplier wins on cost but loses on reliability and risk, so a sound decision **weighs all five factors** and includes a **backup plan.**

**Trap: A** — **"always" is an absolute.** Lower cost does not always raise profit when late deliveries cause stockouts and lost customers.

**Trap: B** — reliability is one factor, not the **only** factor. "Only" is as much of a trap as "always."

---

## Q3 — Exclusive retail agreement

The boutique chain offers to feature Copper Kettle in 12 stores if Nadia signs an exclusive retail rights agreement. This agreement would most directly create competitive advantage through which mechanism?

- A. Barriers to entry — the agreement locks up retail shelf space, keeping competitors out of those stores
- B. Cost leadership — selling in 12 stores reduces per-unit production costs
- C. Economies of scale — more stores means Nadia can switch to mass production
- D. Guaranteed long-term profits from the partnership

---

## Q3 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** An exclusive retail rights agreement is a **barrier to entry** — it locks up shelf space so competitors cannot access those 12 stores. That is one of the three strategy-to-chain mechanisms the exam tests.

**Trap: D** — **"guaranteed" is an absolute.** The deal shifts the odds; the chain could drop her line, close stores or renegotiate.

**Trap: C** — 12 stores do not mean Nadia **should** become a factory. The agreement gives her **shelf space**, not production capacity.

---

## Q4 — Order the supply chain

Which best identifies the order of the supply chain for a mass-produced good, from start to finish?

- A. Manufacturing, raw materials, retail store, warehouse, customer
- B. Raw materials, warehouse, manufacturing, customer, distribution center
- C. Distribution center, raw materials, manufacturing, warehouse, customer
- D. Raw materials, manufacturing, warehouse, distribution center or retail store, customer

---

## Q4 — Answer: **D**

**Why:** The chain runs in one direction: **raw materials to manufacturing to warehouse to distribution/retail to customer.**

**Trap: B** fails twice — it puts the warehouse **before** manufacturing (a warehouse stores **finished** goods), and it puts a distribution center **after the customer.**

---

## Q5 — Scaling table

**BatchBox Snacks scaled up last year. Which conclusion is best supported?**

| | Before scaling | After scaling |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $180,000 | $450,000 |
| Total costs | $120,000 | $240,000 |

- A. Scaling failed, because total costs rose by $120,000
- B. Scaling succeeded: revenue grew 150% against cost growth of 100%
- C. Scaling guarantees higher profit every year
- D. The business should return to its old volume

---

## Q5 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Compute both growth rates — **revenue** $180,000 to $450,000 is **+150%**, **costs** $120,000 to $240,000 is **+100%**. Subtract to get profit: **$60,000 to $210,000.** Revenue growth **outran** cost growth: that is the scaling mechanism.

**Trap: A** reads one number and stops — costs rise when output rises; the question is whether revenue rose **faster.**

**Trap: C** turns one good year into a permanent promise: the **"guarantees" alarm** rings here as everywhere.

---

## Q6 — Service supply chain

An **online tutoring company** hires tutors, develops lesson materials, and delivers sessions through a video platform. Which best describes this company's supply chain?

- A. Raw materials flowing to a manufacturing facility for assembly
- B. A warehouse storing finished lessons until customers order them
- C. Acquiring tutors, learning resources and a virtual delivery platform to provide the service
- D. Services cannot have supply chains

---

## Q6 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** A service supply chain acquires the **employees, the resources and the delivery systems** needed to provide the service — tutors, materials and a video platform are those three parts.

**Trap: B** borrows the goods chain: warehouses store **physical products**, and a tutoring session is **delivered live.**

**Trap: D** is the misconception this topic exists to correct — **services absolutely run supply chains**, built from people, resources and delivery systems instead of trucks and pallets.

---

## Wrap-up

- **Artisan** (skilled, small) vs. **mass production** (technology, large) — the choice follows **customer priorities, core competencies, competitive landscape**
- **Good's chain:** raw materials → manufacturing → warehouse → distribution/retail → customer
- **Service chain:** people + resources + delivery system — **no factory in sight**
- **A warehouse stores; a distribution center stores and forwards. A supplier feeds production; a distributor feeds the retail shelf**
- **Five supplier factors:** cost, quality, efficiency, convenience, **risk** (the one that hides) — **plus a backup plan**
- **Strategy shapes the chain:** low price → cost-cutting, quality → better inputs, barriers → exclusive agreements
- **Scaling:** revenue growth outpaces cost growth because **fixed costs spread across more units**

> **Get the chain wrong and the product never arrives. Get it right and the chain is the moat.**
