# 1.8 Supply Chains
### AP Business with Personal Finance · Unit 1

Lesson deck — **concepts + practice**

---

## Today's goals

| By the end of this lesson you can… | CED reference |
|---|---|
| Distinguish **artisan** from **mass production** and explain the factors that determine the choice | LO 1.8.A |
| Describe the stages of a **supply chain** for a good and for a service | LO 1.8.B |
| Explain how a business's **competitive advantage strategy** shapes its supply chain | LO 1.8.C |

---

## Opening question

You know who owns the business (1.7), who does what, and how the departments work.

> **But where does the product *actually come from* — and how does it get to the customer?**

Sunday-night inventory count. The candy seller flips a chocolate bar over and suddenly realizes **he has no idea where it comes from.** Cocoa grew on a farm in another hemisphere. A factory melted, molded, and wrapped it. A warehouse held it. A truck moved it.

**Only then did his backpack enter the story.**

---

## Artisan or mass production — definition

Before anything moves through a chain, somebody has to make it. Two poles:

| | Artisan | Mass production |
|---|---|---|
| Relies on | **Skilled labor** + close attention to detail | **Technology, assembly lines, machinery** |
| Output | **Smaller** quantities | **Far larger** quantities |

> **Quality can come from either pole.** Artisan does not mean better; mass-produced does not mean worse.

---

## Artisan or mass production — in the examples

| | Artisan | Mass production |
|---|---|---|
| At the park | The **cookie parent** — hours of skilled piping per batch, two dozen cookies, premium price | The **chocolate bars** — machines poured thousands of identical bars in the same time |
| What drives it | Hand skill, attention to detail, small batches | Technology, assembly lines, sheer volume |

> The cookie parent and the chocolate bar sit at **opposite poles** of the same question: how do you make the product?

---

## Three inputs determine the choice — definition

**Which pole a business chooses depends on three inputs:**

1. **Customer priorities** (first) — buyers who prize **customization** lean toward artisan; buyers who prize **low price** lean toward mass production
2. **Core competencies** (second, from 1.5) — a business built on handcraft skill should not suddenly pretend to be a factory
3. **Competitive landscape** (third, from 1.2) — entering a market of cheap mass-produced rivals with a mid-priced product satisfies nobody

---

## Three inputs determine the choice — in the examples

The cookie parent read all three correctly:

| Input | Her read |
|---|---|
| **Customer priorities** | Parents at the park pay extra for birthday-worthy cookies — customization wins |
| **Core competencies** (1.5) | Her skill is decoration, not factory logistics — stay artisan |
| **Competitive landscape** (1.2) | No rival at the park can copy her piping — she owns the niche |

> **All three pointed the same direction.** That's why artisan was the right call — not because artisan is "better," but because it matched her customers, her skills, and her market.

---

## The supply chain for a good — definition

A **supply chain** connects all of the individuals and businesses involved at **each stage of producing and distributing a product** — from acquiring raw materials to final customer delivery.

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. **Raw materials & component parts** | Cocoa, sugar, milk, wrappers |
| 2. **Manufacturing facility** | Workers + equipment turn materials into finished goods |
| 3. **Storage facility (warehouse)** | Finished goods wait |
| 4. **Distribution center / retail store** | Goods are put in front of customers |
| 5. **Customer** | The last link in the chain |

---

## The supply chain for a good — in the examples

The chocolate bar's journey — from a farm on another continent to the candy seller's backpack:

| Stage | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Raw materials** | Cocoa farm, sugar plantation, dairy, wrapper manufacturer |
| **Manufacturing** | Factory melted, molded, wrapped — thousands of identical bars |
| **Warehouse** | Finished bars waited on pallets in a regional storage facility |
| **Distribution / retail** | Truck delivered to the supermarket shelf |
| **Customer** | The candy seller bought it, dropped it in his backpack |

> A chain can be **local, regional, or global.** The chocolate bar's chain is global by necessity — cocoa will not grow near the school.

---

## Local, regional, or global — in the examples

| Chain type | At the park | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Local** | The cookie parent — flour from the neighborhood bakery, baked at home, sold 500 meters away | Everything happens within one community |
| **Regional** | The snack box — chips from a regional distributor, delivered by truck across the metro area | Multiple cities, same country |
| **Global** | The chocolate bar — cocoa from West Africa, factory in Europe, sold at a school in the US | Multiple continents, many links |

> **The longer the chain, the more links can break.** A local chain has few failure points; a global chain has many — and every one of them is a risk.

---

## Chain length and risk

> **The longer the chain, the more links can break.**

| Chain length | Failure points | Risk level |
|---|---|---|
| **Local** | Few — a single supplier, a short drive | Lower |
| **Regional** | Moderate — multiple suppliers, regional trucking | Medium |
| **Global** | Many — overseas suppliers, shipping, customs, currency | Higher |

> **Risk is the factor that hides** — and it hides in the **length** of the chain itself. Every additional stage adds a link that can fail.

---

## Two facilities to keep separate — definition

| | Warehouse | Distribution center |
|---|---|---|
| Primary function | **Storage** | **Stores and moves onward** |
| Goods leave… | When someone comes to collect | On their way to stores and customers |

> **A warehouse waits. A distribution center relays.**

---

## Two facilities to keep separate — in the examples

| Facility | At the park |
|---|---|
| **Warehouse** | The supermarket stores pallets of chocolate bars in its back room — they sit until a shelf needs restocking |
| **Distribution center** | A regional distributor receives pallets from the factory and **forwards** them to multiple supermarket locations across the city |

> The supermarket **stores.** The distributor **stores and forwards.** Keep them separate.

---

## Two intermediaries to keep separate — definition

| | Supplier | Distributor |
|---|---|---|
| What it moves | Materials/goods from one business to another | **Finished products** from manufacturers to retail stores |
| Sits where | Early in the chain | Between manufacturing and retail |

> A **supplier** feeds the production process. A **distributor** feeds the retail shelf.

---

## Two intermediaries to keep separate — in the examples

| Intermediary | In the chocolate bar chain |
|---|---|
| **Supplier** | The cocoa farm — supplies raw materials to the factory |
| **Distributor** | The logistics company — moves finished bars from the factory warehouse to supermarket shelves |

> The factory cannot make chocolate bars without a **supplier** (cocoa). The supermarket cannot stock them without a **distributor** (trucks + logistics). Each sits at a different stage.

---

## The supply chain for a service — definition

Services run chains too — and the **parts list differs.** A service business acquires the **employees, the resources, and the delivery systems** needed to provide the service.

| Example | People | Resources | Delivery system |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tutoring company (virtual) | Tutors | Lesson materials | Video platform |
| Lawn-care crew (in-person) | Trained workers | Mowers + trimmers | Scheduling app that routes trucks |

> When a scenario describes **hiring, equipping, and delivering — with no factory in sight** — it is a **service supply chain.**

---

## The supply chain for a service — in the examples

The snack box itself is a service supply chain — two workers, coolers, and a group-chat order form:

| Part | The snack box |
|---|---|
| **People** | The candy seller + his cousin |
| **Resources** | Coolers, ice, snack inventory, a cash box |
| **Delivery system** | The group-chat order form + the halftime handoff route |

> No factory. No warehouse. No truck. **People + resources + delivery system** — that is the service chain.

---

## Five supplier factors — definition

Every link in a chain is a choice. Businesses weigh **five factors** when choosing suppliers:

| Factor | Visible? |
|---|---|
| **Cost** | On the price sheet |
| **Quality** | On the price sheet / specs |
| **Efficiency** | On the delivery schedule |
| **Convenience** | On the delivery schedule |
| **Risk** | **Hides** |

> **Risk is the factor that hides.** Natural disasters, political instability, resource shortages, production errors, and supplier reputation can all delay delivery or raise costs.

---

## Five supplier factors — in the examples

The candy seller has two sourcing options — each trades off different factors:

| Option | Cost | Quality | Efficiency | Convenience | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Supermarket** | Pricier | Same bars | Always stocked | Down the street | **Never misses** |
| **Wholesale parent** | Cheaper (bulk) | Same bars | Larger quantities | Needs a ride | Van **broke down twice** |

> Cheap + unreliable vs. pricier + certain — and a Saturday with an **empty cooler earns nothing.**

**He splits the order:** the wholesaler carries the bulk, and a supermarket run on Friday night **backstops the risk.**

---

## Supplier decisions — the exam habit

> **Every one of the five factors, plus a backup plan, belongs in a supplier answer.**

The exam loves to offer a **cheap option with hidden risk** and ask you to choose. The strong answer:

1. Names **all five factors** — not just cost
2. Prices in the **risk** — the factor that hides
3. Mentions a **backup plan**

> **Buying inputs is not outsourcing.** Every producer buys raw materials. The question is always **which** supplier, **why**, and **what happens if they fail.**

---

## Strategy shapes the chain — definition

> A business's **competitive advantage strategy decides what kind of chain it builds.**

| Strategy | Chain design | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Low prices** | Mass production + cost-cutting (plus or minus scaling) | Cheaper resources, more efficient processes |
| **High quality** | High-quality resources + methods | Better inputs, artisan or mass |
| **Barriers to entry** | Exclusive/restrictive agreements | Lock up suppliers or shelf space |

> **The chain itself becomes the source of competitive advantage.**

---

## Strategy shapes the chain — in the examples

| Strategy | At the park | Chain choice |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost-tuned** | The candy seller buying in bulk from the wholesale parent | Cheaper input, pass savings to buyers |
| **Quality-tuned** | The cookie parent using premium wax for decorating | Better input, commands higher price |
| **Exclusivity-tuned** | The only-vendor permit at the park on Saturdays | Locks out rivals from the same location |

> **Name the strategy first, then show how a particular chain choice — a cheaper input, a better input, or a locked-up input — serves exactly that strategy.**

---

## The chain becomes the advantage

Read the three strategies together:

| Chain type | Advantage it creates |
|---|---|
| **Cost-tuned** | Can support prices rivals struggle to **match** |
| **Quality-tuned** | Can produce goods rivals struggle to **imitate** |
| **Exclusivity-tuned** | Can leave rivals hunting for **suppliers and shelf space** |

> **On the exam, be specific about the mechanism:** name which strategy the business runs, then show how a particular chain choice serves exactly that strategy.

---

## Scaling — definition

Some cost-focused businesses go further and **scale:** build higher-capacity chains so that **revenue increases outrun cost increases.**

**The mechanism:** the second batch reuses the same oven, permits, and order system — the **fixed costs get spread across twice the units** while every unit still sells at full price.

| | Before scaling | After scaling |
|---|---|---|
| Units | 20,000 | 40,000 |
| Revenue | $80,000 | $160,000 (+100%) |
| Total costs | $60,000 | $96,000 (+60%) |
| **Profit** | **$20,000** | **$64,000** |

> Revenue grew **100%**; costs grew only **60%**. That gap is the entire point of scaling.

---

## Scaling — in the examples

If the snack box **doubled batches** for the same Saturday:

| What changes | What stays fixed |
|---|---|
| Inventory cost doubles | The oven (same one, second batch) |
| Prep time roughly doubles | The permits (already paid) |
| | The order system (same group chat) |

> **Fixed costs (oven, permits, order system) spread across twice the units.** Each unit still sells at full price — so revenue grows faster than costs.

> **That is the scaling mechanism the exam tests.** Know it as a formula: fixed costs spread, revenue outpaces costs, profit accelerates.

---

## Three question shapes

Chain questions arrive in three shapes:

| Shape | What it asks | How to answer |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Order the stages** | Raw materials to customer | Warehouse comes **after manufacturing, before distribution** |
| **2. Choose a supplier** | Weigh the options | Weigh **all five factors**, with **risk priced in** |
| **3. Match strategy to chain** | Which design fits | Low price to cost-cutting; quality to better inputs; barriers to exclusive agreements |

> **Name the shape first, and the question mostly solves itself.**

---

## Exam points to remember

| Concept | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Production** | Artisan (skilled, small) vs. mass production (tech, large) — chosen by customer priorities, competencies, landscape |
| **Good's chain** | Raw materials to manufacturing to warehouse to distribution/retail to customer |
| **Service chain** | People + resources + delivery system (no factory) |
| **Supplier factors** | Cost, quality, efficiency, convenience, **risk** (the one that hides) + backup plan |
| **Strategy to chain** | Low price to cost-cutting; quality to better inputs; barriers to exclusive agreements |
| **Scaling** | Revenue growth outpaces cost growth because fixed costs spread across more units |

---

## Two facilities — exam distinction

> **A warehouse stores. A distribution center stores and forwards.**

The exam will offer both terms in the same question. Sort by **what happens to the goods next:**

- Goods sit until collected = **warehouse**
- Goods move onward to stores and customers = **distribution center**

---

## Two intermediaries — exam distinction

> **A supplier feeds production. A distributor feeds retail.**

Sort by **where they sit in the chain:**

- Early in the chain, materials between businesses = **supplier**
- Between manufacturing and retail, finished products = **distributor**

---

## Service vs. goods chains — exam habit

| Signal in the scenario | It is… |
|---|---|
| Factory, raw materials, warehouse, truck | **Goods** supply chain |
| Hiring people, acquiring resources, building a delivery system — no factory | **Service** supply chain |
| "Services cannot have supply chains" | **Trap answer** — services absolutely run supply chains |

> **The answer to a service supply chain question names three parts: people, resources, and a delivery system.**

# Case study — 百年供应链进化史

**小Lin说** · 3.6M views · [B站 link](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1gAHseeEDT/)

A 100-year history of how supply chains evolved — from Ford's first assembly line to today's global, disruption-prone networks.

> *Watch the video before class. Each milestone maps to an AP 1.8 concept.*

---

## The timeline — six turning points

| Year | Milestone | 1.8 concept |
|---|---|---|
| **1913** | Ford's assembly line — 12 hrs to 93 min per car | **Mass production + scaling** |
| **1950s** | Toyota's just-in-time — no excess inventory | **Supplier reliability + efficiency** |
| **1962** | Walmart's distribution centers | **Warehouse to distribution center** |
| **1990s** | Globalization — chains stretch across oceans | **Global supply chain + risk** |
| **2000s** | Amazon + e-commerce — same-day delivery | **Service supply chain** |
| **2020s** | COVID + chip shortage — chains break | **Risk — the factor that hides** |

---

## Ford (1913) — mass production and scaling

**Ford's assembly line** turned artisan car-making into mass production.

- Before: one skilled team built an entire car — **12 hours per vehicle**
- After: each worker performed one task on a moving line — **93 minutes per car**
- Price dropped from **$850 to $260**

> **Customer priority shifted** from customization (artisan) to **low price** (mass production). The chain was built entirely around **cost-cutting through scale.**

**AP connection:** the three inputs (1.8.A) — Ford read customer priorities, matched its competencies to factory production, and exploited a landscape where no rival could match the price.

---

## Toyota (1950s) — just-in-time

**Toyota's JIT system** synchronized suppliers so precisely that a late part shut down the whole factory.

- Not bigger — **tighter**
- Supplier selection prioritized **reliability over cost**
- The factory held almost no inventory — parts arrived exactly when needed

> **Which of the five supplier factors** did Toyota prioritize? **Efficiency and reliability** — and when that factor failed, the entire line stopped.

**AP connection:** risk (the factor that hides) plus the need for a **backup plan.** A single late shipment could halt production for a day.

---

## Walmart (1962) — distribution centers

**Walmart** turned warehouses into distribution centers.

- A warehouse **stores.** A distribution center **stores and forwards.**
- This one change cut delivery from **days to hours**
- Created competitive advantage through **faster delivery at lower cost**

> **The warehouse-to-distribution-center distinction** is not just vocabulary — it is a **strategic choice** that reshapes the entire chain.

**AP connection:** two facilities (1.8.B) — the exam tests whether you can tell them apart. Walmart's advantage came from choosing the right one.

---

## Globalization (1990s) — chains stretch

Supply chains went **global** — raw materials from one country, manufacturing in another, assembly in a third, sale in a fourth.

- **Cost advantage:** labor and materials cheaper overseas
- **New risk:** one disruption halfway around the world could stop your entire chain
- The longer the chain, the **more links can break**

> **The risk was hiding in the length of the chain itself.**

**AP connection:** global supply chains are longer and more complex — each stage adds a link that can fail. The five supplier factors apply globally, with **risk amplified by distance.**

---

## Amazon (2000s) — the service supply chain

**Amazon** turned distribution into a service — and then sold the service itself.

- Fulfillment centers, delivery fleets, same-day logistics
- AWS: the infrastructure **became the product**
- The supply chain was no longer about **goods** — it was about **people, resources, and a delivery system**

> **The service supply chain** — tutors, lawn-care crews, and now Amazon — runs on the same three parts: people, resources, delivery system.

**AP connection:** service supply chains (1.8.B) — when the scenario describes hiring, equipping, and delivering with no factory, name the three parts.

---

## COVID (2020s) — risk materializes

**COVID-19** exposed every hidden risk at once:

- Single-source suppliers in one country = **no backup**
- Chip shortages halted car production worldwide
- Shipping containers sat in the wrong ports
- The risk had been hiding **for decades**

> **Every supplier decision should include a backup plan.** The factor that hides eventually shows up — and when it does, chains without a backup stop.

**AP connection:** risk is the factor that hides (1.8.C). COVID was the exam question nobody wanted: "What happens when the risk actually materializes?"

---

## Case — discussion questions

1. Ford scaled production and dropped the price. **Explain the scaling mechanism** — why did revenue growth outpace cost growth?

2. Toyota chose suppliers based on reliability, not just cost. **Which of the five supplier factors** did Toyota prioritize, and what happens when that factor fails?

3. Walmart turned warehouses into distribution centers. **What is the difference**, and how did it create competitive advantage?

4. COVID exposed single-source dependencies. **How is this an example of "the factor that hides"** — and what should businesses have done differently?

# Case study — Zara's supply chain

**ZARA 案例分析** · 8.5k views · [B站 link](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1qS4y1L73c/)

How Zara became the world's #1 fast fashion brand — not through cheaper prices, but through a **supply chain built for speed.** A new design goes from sketch to store shelf in **2 weeks** — the industry average is **6 months.**

> *Watch the video before class. The case maps every 1.8 concept to a real chain decision.*

---

## Zara — the strategy: speed

Zara's competitive advantage is **speed** — getting new designs to stores faster than any rival.

- **2 weeks** from design to store shelf (industry: 6 months)
- **Small batches** — if a style doesn't sell, it's gone in weeks
- **Frequent refresh** — stores receive new inventory **twice a week**

> **The strategy decided the chain.** Every link — design, manufacturing, distribution, retail — was built to serve one goal: **faster than everyone else.**

---

## Zara — the chain: every stage built for speed

| Stage | What Zara does differently | AP 1.8 concept |
|---|---|---|
| **Design** | In-house design team in Spain — no outsourcing | Core competency (1.5) kept in-house |
| **Raw materials** | Fabric purchased in advance (undyed) — color decided later | Pre-positioned materials cut lead time |
| **Manufacturing** | **Own factories** in Spain + nearby countries — not outsourced to Asia | Supplier control for speed, not cost |
| **Distribution** | One **central distribution center** in Spain — all products flow through it | Warehouse vs. distribution center (1.8.B) |
| **Retail** | 6,000+ stores worldwide, inventory refreshed **twice a week** | Short chain = less risk |

---

## Zara — the distribution center is the advantage

Zara built one massive **central distribution center** in Arteixo, Spain.

- Every garment — whether destined for Tokyo, New York, or São Paulo — passes through **this one facility**
- A warehouse **stores.** Zara's center **stores and forwards** — garments arrive and ship within **hours**
- The entire chain is designed so that a design sketch on Monday can be **on a store shelf by Friday**

> **This is the warehouse vs. distribution center distinction made real.** Zara doesn't store inventory — it **moves** inventory. The center is a relay, not a warehouse.

---

## Zara — supplier selection: speed over cost

Zara's supplier choices break the "cheapest wins" rule:

| Factor | Zara's choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost** | **Higher** — own factories in Spain cost more than outsourcing to Asia | Accepted as the price of speed |
| **Quality** | **High** — in-house control over every step | Reputation depends on it |
| **Efficiency** | **Maximum** — 2-week turnaround is the #1 priority | The whole chain exists for this |
| **Convenience** | **Centralized** — one distribution center for the world | Simplifies coordination |
| **Risk** | **Lower** — short chain, fewer borders, fewer links | Less distance = fewer things can break |

> **Zara traded cost for speed.** The five supplier factors don't all point the same direction — a business must **choose which factor to prioritize** based on its strategy.

---

## Zara — barriers to entry

Zara's supply chain is **nearly impossible to copy:**

- **Own factories** — competitors outsource and lose control
- **One central distribution center** — requires massive investment
- **2-week turnaround** — built over decades, not overnight
- **Small-batch, high-refresh model** — requires a completely different chain than traditional fashion

> **The supply chain itself is the barrier to entry.** Rivals can copy a dress design in days — but they can't copy the **chain that delivers it in 2 weeks.**

**AP connection:** this is the **exclusivity-tuned** chain strategy (EK 1.8.C.3) — not through a legal agreement, but through a chain so specialized that competitors **can't replicate it.**

---

## Zara — AP 1.8 connections

| 1.8 concept | Zara example |
|---|---|
| **Strategy → chain design** | Speed strategy → own factories + centralized distribution + short chain |
| **Supply chain stages** | Design → raw materials → manufacturing (own) → distribution center → stores → customer |
| **Warehouse vs. distribution center** | One central facility that **stores and forwards** — not a warehouse |
| **Supplier selection** | Traded cost for speed + efficiency + quality — five factors, different priority |
| **Barriers to entry** | The chain itself is the barrier — rivals can't replicate 2-week turnaround |
| **Risk** | Short chain + own factories = fewer links that can break vs. global outsourcing |

---

## Zara — discussion questions

1. Zara's strategy is **speed**, not low price. How did this strategy **shape every stage** of its supply chain? (Connect to EK 1.8.C.1, 1.8.C.2)

2. Zara built **own factories in Spain** instead of outsourcing to cheaper countries. Which of the five supplier factors did Zara **sacrifice**, and which did it **prioritize**? Why?

3. Zara's central distribution center in Arteixo handles **every garment for the entire world.** Explain the difference between a warehouse and a distribution center — and why Zara's facility is the latter.

4. Zara's supply chain is described as a **barrier to entry.** How is this different from a legal barrier (like an exclusive agreement)? What makes it **harder to copy**?

---

# Practice — 6 questions

**4 on one stimulus (Copper Kettle Candles)** · **2 standalone**

Read to yourself, pause, **commit to an answer** — then advance.

---

## Stimulus: Copper Kettle Candles

**Nadia Farouk** owns **Copper Kettle Candles**, pouring small batches by hand with premium wax and charging approximately **twice what machine-made candles cost.** Customers praise the quality and custom scents.

Nadia buys wax from an **overseas supplier** at a price **20% below local** — but **2 of the last 6 shipments arrived weeks late**, and one contained **the wrong blend.**

A boutique chain offers a deal: **feature Copper Kettle in 12 stores** if Nadia signs an **exclusive retail rights agreement.**

> **Watch for three decision points:** production approach, supplier choice, and the exclusive agreement.

---

## Q1 — Classify production approach

Nadia's method of pouring small batches by hand with premium wax is best described as which?

- A. Mass production, because she sells in multiple locations
- B. Artisan production, because she relies on skilled labor and attention to detail in smaller quantities
- C. Outsourcing, because she buys wax from an overseas supplier
- D. A service supply chain, because she provides custom scents

<!--
Pause. Ask: does she use machinery and assembly lines to produce large quantities, or skilled hands and careful attention in small batches?
-->

---

## Q1 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Artisan production relies on **skilled labor** and close attention to detail, producing **smaller quantities.** Nadia pours by hand, uses premium materials, and makes small batches — every marker points to artisan.

**Trap: C** — **buying inputs is not outsourcing.** Every producer buys raw materials. Nadia makes the candles herself; she buys wax the same way a baker buys flour.

**Trap: A** — selling in multiple locations does not change the **production method.** She could sell in 100 stores and still be artisan if she pours every candle by hand.

> **Classify by how the product is made, not by how many places it is sold.**

---

## Q2 — Supplier decision

Nadia's overseas wax supplier offers a price 20% below local, but 2 of 6 recent shipments arrived late and one had the wrong blend. Which analysis **best** supports a sound supplier decision?

- A. Choose the overseas supplier because lower cost always increases profit
- B. Choose the local supplier because reliability is the only factor that matters
- C. Weigh the lower cost against the risks — including late deliveries, wrong shipments, and potential damage to reputation — and consider a backup plan
- D. Choose the overseas supplier because customers will never know about delivery problems

---

## Q2 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** The five supplier factors are cost, quality, efficiency, convenience, and **risk.** The overseas supplier wins on cost but loses on reliability and risk (late deliveries, wrong blend). A sound decision **weighs all five factors** and includes a **backup plan.**

**Trap: A** — **"always" is an absolute.** Lower cost does not always increase profit if late deliveries cause stockouts and lost customers.

**Trap: B** — reliability is one factor, not the **only** factor. The question says "only" — that is as much of a trap as "always."

> **Weigh all five factors, with risk priced in. Name the backup plan.**

## Q3 — Exclusive retail agreement

The boutique chain offers to feature Copper Kettle in 12 stores if Nadia signs an exclusive retail rights agreement. This agreement would most directly create competitive advantage through which mechanism?

- A. Barriers to entry — the exclusive agreement locks up retail shelf space, preventing competitors from accessing those stores
- B. Cost leadership — selling in 12 stores reduces per-unit production costs
- C. Economies of scale — more stores means Nadia can switch to mass production
- D. Guaranteed long-term profits from the partnership

---

## Q3 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** An exclusive retail rights agreement is a **barrier to entry** — it locks up shelf space so competitors cannot access those 12 stores. That is one of the three strategy-to-chain mechanisms the exam tests.

**Trap: D** — **"guaranteed" is an absolute.** The deal shifts the odds in Nadia's favor; it guarantees nothing. The boutique chain could drop her line, close stores, or renegotiate.

**Trap: C** — selling in 12 stores does not automatically mean Nadia **should** switch to mass production. She is an artisan producer; the agreement gives her **shelf space**, not a factory.

> **Exclusive agreements create barriers to entry. They do not guarantee profits.**

## Q4 — Order the supply chain

Which best identifies the order of the supply chain for a mass-produced good, from start to finish?

- A. Manufacturing, raw materials, retail store, warehouse, customer
- B. Raw materials, warehouse, manufacturing, customer, distribution center
- C. Distribution center, raw materials, manufacturing, warehouse, customer
- D. Raw materials, manufacturing, warehouse, distribution center or retail store, customer

---

## Q4 — Answer: **D**

The chain runs in one direction:

> **Raw materials to manufacturing to warehouse to distribution/retail to customer**

**Trap: B** fails twice — it puts the warehouse **before** manufacturing (the warehouse stores **finished** goods), and it ends with a distribution center **after the customer.**

> **Warehouse comes after manufacturing and before distribution. Never after the customer.**

## Q5 — Scaling table

**BatchBox Snacks doubled its production scale last year:**

| | Before scaling | After scaling |
|---|---|---|
| Units | 20,000 | 40,000 |
| Revenue | $80,000 | $160,000 |
| Total costs | $60,000 | $96,000 |
| **Profit** | **$20,000** | **$64,000** |

Which conclusion is **best supported** by the table?

- A. Scaling failed, because total costs increased by $36,000
- B. Scaling succeeded, because revenue grew 100% while costs grew only 60%, raising profit from $20,000 to $64,000
- C. Scaling guarantees higher profits every year going forward
- D. The business should return to its original scale

---

## Q5 — Answer: **B**

Compute both growth rates:

- **Revenue:** $80,000 to $160,000 = **+100%**
- **Costs:** $60,000 to $96,000 = **+60%**
- **Profit:** $20,000 to **$64,000** confirms it

Revenue growth **outran** cost growth. That is the scaling mechanism.

**Trap: A** reads one number and stops — costs rose (as they do when production doubles); the question is whether revenue rose **faster.**

**Trap: C** converts one good year into a permanent promise — the **"guarantees" alarm** rings here as everywhere.

> **Read both growth rates. Compare them. One number alone is never enough.**

## Q6 — Service supply chain

An **online tutoring company** hires tutors, develops lesson materials, and delivers sessions through a video platform. Which best describes this company's supply chain?

- A. Raw materials flowing to a manufacturing facility for assembly
- B. A warehouse storing finished lessons until customers order them
- C. Acquiring tutors, learning resources, and a virtual delivery platform to provide the service
- D. Services cannot have supply chains

---

## Q6 — Answer: **C**

A service supply chain acquires the **employees, the resources, and the delivery systems** needed to provide the service — tutors, materials, and a video platform are those three parts.

**Trap: B** borrows the goods chain — warehouses store **physical products**; a tutoring session is **delivered live.**

**Trap: D** is the misconception this topic exists to correct — **services absolutely run supply chains**, built from people, resources, and delivery systems instead of trucks and pallets.

> **When the scenario names hiring, equipping, and delivering — with no factory — it is a service supply chain. The answer names three parts.**

## Wrap-up

- **Artisan** (skilled, small) vs. **mass production** (tech, large) — chosen by customer priorities, competencies, and competitive landscape
- **Good's chain:** raw materials to manufacturing to warehouse to distribution/retail to customer
- **Service chain:** people + resources + delivery system (no factory)
- **Five supplier factors:** cost, quality, efficiency, convenience, **risk** (the one that hides) — plus a backup plan
- **Strategy shapes the chain:** low price to cost-cutting; quality to better inputs; barriers to exclusive agreements
- **Scaling:** revenue growth outpaces cost growth because fixed costs spread across more units
- **The chain itself is where competitive advantage lives**
