# 1.8 Supply Chains
### AP Business with Personal Finance · Unit 1

Lesson deck — **concepts + practice**

---

## Today's goals

| By the end of this lesson you can… | CED reference |
|---|---|
| Distinguish **artisan** from **mass production** and explain the factors that determine the choice | LO 1.8.A |
| Describe the stages of a **supply chain** for a good and for a service | LO 1.8.B |
| Explain how a business's **competitive advantage strategy** shapes its supply chain | LO 1.8.C |

---

## Opening question

You know who owns the business (1.7), who does what, and how the departments work.

> **But where does the product *actually come from* — and how does it get to the customer?**

Sunday-night inventory count. The candy seller flips a chocolate bar over and realizes **he has no idea where it comes from.** Cocoa grew on a farm in another hemisphere. A factory melted, molded and wrapped it. A warehouse held it. A truck moved it. **Only then did his backpack enter the story.**

---

## Artisan or mass production

Before anything moves through a chain, somebody has to make it. Two poles:

| | Artisan | Mass production |
|---|---|---|
| Relies on | **skilled labor** + close attention to detail | **technology, assembly lines, machinery** |
| Output | **smaller** quantities | **far larger** quantities |
| At the park | The **cookie parent** — hours of skilled piping per batch, two dozen cookies, premium price | The **chocolate bars** — machines poured thousands of identical bars in the same time |

> **Quality can come from either pole.** Artisan does not mean better, mass-produced does not mean worse — classify by **how the product is made.**

---

## Three inputs determine the choice

| Input | The rule | The cookie parent's read |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Customer priorities** | Buyers who prize **customization** lean artisan; buyers who prize **low price** lean mass production | Parents at the park pay extra for birthday-worthy cookies |
| **2. Core competencies** (1.5) | A business built on hand skill should not pretend to be a factory | Her skill is decoration, not factory logistics |
| **3. Competitive landscape** (1.2) | Entering a market of cheap mass-produced rivals with a mid-priced product satisfies nobody | No rival at the park can copy her piping |

> **All three pointed the same direction.** Artisan was right not because artisan is "better," but because it matched her customers, her skills and her market.

## The supply chain for a good

A **supply chain** connects all of the individuals and businesses involved at **each stage of producing and distributing a product** — from acquiring raw materials to final customer delivery.

| Stage | What happens | The chocolate bar |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Raw materials & component parts** | Inputs are acquired | Cocoa farm, sugar plantation, dairy, wrapper maker |
| 2. **Manufacturing facility** | Workers and equipment make finished goods | Factory melted, molded and wrapped thousands of bars |
| 3. **Storage facility (warehouse)** | Finished goods wait | Pallets in a regional storage facility |
| 4. **Distribution center / retail store** | Goods are put in front of customers | Truck to the supermarket shelf |
| 5. **Customer** | The last link in the chain | The candy seller, and his backpack |

---

## Local, regional, or global — and the risk of length

| Chain | At the park | Failure points | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Local** | The cookie parent — flour from the neighborhood bakery, baked at home, sold 500 meters away | Few — one supplier, a short drive | Lower |
| **Regional** | The snack box — chips from a regional distributor, trucked across the metro area | Moderate — multiple suppliers, regional trucking | Medium |
| **Global** | The chocolate bar — cocoa from West Africa, factory in Europe, sold at a US school | Many — overseas suppliers, shipping, customs, currency | Higher |

> **The longer the chain, the more links can break.** Risk is the factor that hides, and it hides in the **length** of the chain itself: every additional stage adds a link that can fail.

---

## Case clips — one link breaks, and the bill arrives later

| 视频 | 链接 | 对应本节的哪个点 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The canal reopened, the world did not: the claims land on the shipowner** | [BV1fv41187MK](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1fv41187MK/)（5:28 · 393K views） | **Risk turning into cost.** One ship, one waterway, six days — and no bypass anywhere in the chain. Ships queued at both ends, ports downstream received several weeks of arrivals at once, and the money arguments started only after the traffic moved again (general average: everyone in the chain shares the loss). **The point is the timing — the chain reopened first, the bill arrived afterwards.** |

> 💡 **The line to land on:** a long chain does not necessarily break more often — it breaks **further away**, so the damage comes back as an invoice weeks later. Ask: which link was closest to the customer, and **which link paid?**

---

## Two facilities to keep separate: warehouse vs. distribution center

| | Warehouse | Distribution center |
|---|---|---|
| Primary function | **Storage** | **Stores and moves onward** |
| Goods leave… | When someone comes to collect | On their way to stores and customers |
| At the park | The supermarket's back room: pallets of bars sit until a shelf needs restocking | A regional distributor receives pallets from the factory and **forwards** them to stores across the city |

> **A warehouse waits; a distribution center relays.** When the exam offers both terms in one question, sort by **what happens to the goods next** — sitting until collected is a warehouse, moving onward is a distribution center.

---

## Two intermediaries to keep separate: supplier vs. distributor

| | Supplier | Distributor |
|---|---|---|
| What it moves | Materials or goods from one business to another | **Finished products** from manufacturers to retail stores |
| Sits where | Early in the chain — the cocoa farm feeding the factory | Between manufacturing and retail — the trucks and logistics company |

> **A supplier feeds production; a distributor feeds the retail shelf.** The factory cannot make bars without a supplier; the supermarket cannot stock them without a distributor. Sort by **where they sit in the chain.**

---

## The supply chain for a service

Services run chains too — and the **parts list differs.** A service business acquires the **employees, the resources and the delivery systems** needed to provide the service.

| Example | People | Resources | Delivery system |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tutoring company (virtual) | Tutors | Lesson materials | Video platform |
| Lawn-care crew (in-person) | Trained workers | Mowers and trimmers | Scheduling app that routes trucks |
| **The snack box** | The candy seller + his cousin | Coolers, ice, snack inventory, cash box | Group-chat order form + the halftime handoff route |

> **No factory, no warehouse, no truck** — hiring, equipping and delivering is a **service supply chain**, and the answer names all three parts: people, resources, delivery system.

---

## Worked example — food delivery: three parts, three strategies

**Part 1 — the service supply chain (1.8.B.3).** The platform makes nothing; it sells the delivery itself.

| Employees | Resources | Delivery system |
|---|---|---|
| Couriers, kitchen staff, dispatch and support teams | E-bikes, insulated bags, store kitchens, apps and servers | Dispatch batching, the promised delivery window, the courier app's accept–pick up–deliver flow |

> Delivered **in person** at the door. The same three parts appear in a **virtual** service — tutors, lesson materials, a video platform.

**Part 2 — the same three parts, built three different ways (1.8.C).**

| Strategy | What the chain is built to do | What it sacrifices | Advantage it creates |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low prices** | Put many orders on one courier — batching and order density. This is **scaling**: the courier's hour and the station's fixed costs spread across more orders, so revenue grows faster than cost | The individual delivery window — "your order waits for the next stop" | Prices rivals struggle to **match** |
| **High quality** | One courier, one order: dedicated couriers, temperature-controlled gear, no batching | Cost per order — it runs several times higher | A service rivals struggle to **imitate** |
| **Barriers to entry** | **Exclusive or restrictive agreements** with merchants: list only on us, or lose the placement | Merchant goodwill, and it invites legal scrutiny (1.3 legal factor) | Rivals left hunting for **supply** |

> 💡 **All three chains use the same people, resources and delivery system — the strategy decides what to buy and what to give up.** The exam never asks which strategy is best; it asks which input the chain **locks up**, **upgrades**, or **refuses to pay for**.

---

## Five supplier factors — and the backup plan

Every link in a chain is a choice. Businesses weigh **five factors** when choosing suppliers: **cost, quality, efficiency, convenience** — and **risk**, the one that **hides** (natural disasters, political instability, resource shortages, production errors, supplier reputation).

| Option | Cost | Quality | Efficiency | Convenience | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Supermarket** | Pricier | Same bars | Always stocked | Down the street | **Never misses** |
| **Wholesale parent** | Cheaper (bulk) | Same bars | Larger quantities | Needs a ride | Van **broke down twice** |

He **splits the order**: the wholesaler carries the bulk, and a Friday-night supermarket run **backstops the risk** — an empty cooler on Saturday earns nothing.

> **A supplier answer names all five factors, prices in the risk, and includes a backup plan.** The exam loves to offer a cheap option with hidden risk. And remember: **buying inputs is not outsourcing** — every producer buys raw materials.

---

## Strategy shapes the chain

A business's **competitive advantage strategy decides what kind of chain it builds.**

| Strategy | Chain design | At the park | Advantage it creates |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low prices** | Mass production and cost-cutting, plus or minus scaling | Buying in bulk from the wholesale parent | Prices rivals struggle to **match** |
| **High quality** | High-quality resources and methods | Premium wax for the cookie parent's decorating | Goods rivals struggle to **imitate** |
| **Barriers to entry** | Exclusive or restrictive agreements | The only-vendor permit for Saturday at the park | Rivals left hunting for **suppliers and shelf space** |

> **Name the strategy first, then show the mechanism:** which chain choice — a cheaper input, a better input, or a locked-up input — serves exactly that strategy. **The chain itself becomes the advantage.**

---

## Scaling

Some cost-focused businesses go further and **scale:** build higher-capacity chains so that **revenue increases outrun cost increases.** The mechanism is that **fixed costs get spread across more units** while every unit still sells at full price.

| | Before scaling | After scaling |
|---|---|---|
| Units | 20,000 | 40,000 |
| Revenue | $80,000 | $160,000 (**+100%**) |
| Total costs | $60,000 | $96,000 (**+60%**) |
| **Profit** | **$20,000** | **$64,000** |

Doubling the snack box's batches doubles inventory cost and prep time — but the **oven, the permits and the order system stay fixed**, so they are paid for twice over.

> **Revenue grew 100%; costs grew 60%. That gap is the entire point of scaling** — know it as a formula: fixed costs spread, revenue outpaces costs, profit accelerates.

---

## Three question shapes

| Shape | What it asks | How to answer |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Order the stages** | Raw materials to customer | Warehouse comes **after manufacturing, before distribution** |
| **2. Choose a supplier** | Weigh the options | Weigh **all five factors**, with **risk priced in**, and name a **backup plan** |
| **3. Match strategy to chain** | Which design fits | Low price to cost-cutting; quality to better inputs; barriers to exclusive agreements |

> **Name the shape first, and the question mostly solves itself.**

# Case study — a century of supply chains

🎬 [From Ford's assembly line to a global chain that can break](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1gAHseeEDT)（Xiao Lin Shuo · 3.6M views）

**Six turning points, six ideas from this lesson.** Watch the video before class.

---

## The timeline — six turning points

| Year | Milestone | 1.8 concept |
|---|---|---|
| **1913** | Ford's assembly line — 12 hours to 93 minutes per car | **Mass production + scaling** |
| **1950s** | Toyota's just-in-time — no excess inventory | **Supplier reliability + efficiency** |
| **1962** | Walmart's distribution centers | **Warehouse to distribution center** |
| **1990s** | Globalization — chains stretch across oceans | **Global supply chain + risk** |
| **2000s** | Amazon and e-commerce — same-day delivery | **Service supply chain** |
| **2020s** | COVID and the chip shortage — chains break | **Risk, the factor that hides** |

---

## Ford (1913) — mass production and scaling

**Ford's assembly line** turned artisan car-making into mass production.

- Before: one skilled team built an entire car — **12 hours per vehicle**
- After: each worker performed one task on a moving line — **93 minutes per car**
- Price dropped from **$850 to $260**

> **Customer priority shifted** from customization to **low price**, and the chain was built entirely around **cost-cutting through scale.**

---

## Toyota (1950s) — just-in-time

**Toyota's JIT system** synchronized suppliers so precisely that a late part shut the whole factory down.

- Not bigger — **tighter**
- Supplier selection prioritized **reliability over cost**
- The factory held almost no inventory; parts arrived exactly when needed

> **Which supplier factor did Toyota bet on?** Efficiency and reliability — and when that one failed, the line stopped. A single late shipment cost a day of production, which is exactly what a **backup plan** is for.

---

## Walmart (1962) — distribution centers

**Walmart** turned warehouses into distribution centers.

- A warehouse **stores**; a distribution center **stores and forwards**
- That one change cut delivery from **days to hours**
- Advantage: **faster delivery at lower cost**

> **The warehouse-to-distribution-center distinction is not vocabulary — it is a strategic choice** that reshapes the whole chain.

---

## Globalization (1990s) — chains stretch

Supply chains went **global** — raw materials from one country, manufacturing in another, assembly in a third, sale in a fourth.

- **Cost advantage:** labor and materials cheaper overseas
- **New risk:** one disruption halfway around the world could stop your entire chain

> **The risk was hiding in the length of the chain itself.** The five supplier factors apply globally, with **risk amplified by distance.**

---

## Amazon (2000s) — the service supply chain

**Amazon** turned distribution into a service — and then sold the service itself.

- Fulfillment centers, delivery fleets, same-day logistics
- AWS: the infrastructure **became the product**

> A chain of **people, resources and a delivery system** — the three parts of a service supply chain, the same three as a tutoring company or a lawn-care crew.

---

## COVID (2020s) — risk materializes

**COVID-19** exposed every hidden risk at once:

- Single-source suppliers in one country = **no backup**
- Chip shortages halted car production worldwide
- Shipping containers sat in the wrong ports

> **The factor that hides eventually shows up** — and chains without a backup stop. COVID was the exam question nobody wanted: *what happens when the risk actually materializes?*

---

## Case — discussion questions

1. Ford scaled production and dropped the price. **Explain the scaling mechanism** — why did revenue growth outpace cost growth?
2. Toyota chose suppliers on reliability, not just cost. **Which of the five factors** did it prioritize, and what happens when that factor fails?
3. Walmart turned warehouses into distribution centers. **What is the difference**, and how did it create competitive advantage?
4. COVID exposed single-source dependencies. **How is that "the factor that hides"** — and what should businesses have done differently?

---

## Case — connecting to the candy seller

Every milestone maps onto the candy seller's Saturday:

| Milestone | The candy seller's version |
|---|---|
| **Ford** (mass production) | The chocolate bars — machines poured thousands |
| **Toyota** (reliability) | Supermarket vs. wholesale parent — certainty vs. cost |
| **Walmart** (distribution centers) | The truck that delivers bars to the supermarket shelf |
| **Globalization** (global chain) | Cocoa from another hemisphere — the chain is global |
| **Amazon** (service chain) | The snack box itself — people, coolers, order form |
| **COVID** (risk materializes) | The wholesale parent's van breaking down — twice |

> **The video tells the hundred-year version; the candy seller lives the Saturday version.** Same concepts, different scale.

# Case study — Zara's supply chain

🎬 [How Zara became the world's number one fast-fashion brand](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1qS4y1L73c)（8.5k views）

**Speed, not low price:** a new design reaches a store shelf in **2 weeks** — the industry average is **6 months.**

---

## Zara — the strategy: speed

Zara's competitive advantage is **getting new designs to stores faster than any rival.**

- **2 weeks** from design to store shelf (industry: 6 months)
- **Small batches** — a style that doesn't sell is gone within weeks
- Stores receive fresh inventory **twice a week**

> **The strategy decided the chain.** Every link — design, manufacturing, distribution, retail — was built to serve one goal: faster than everyone else.

---

## Zara — every stage built for speed

| Stage | What Zara does differently | Concept |
|---|---|---|
| **Design** | In-house team in Spain, not outsourced | Core competency (1.5) kept in-house |
| **Raw materials** | Fabric bought in advance, undyed; colour decided later | Pre-positioned inputs cut lead time |
| **Manufacturing** | **Own factories** in Spain and nearby, not outsourced to Asia | Supplier control for speed, not cost |
| **Distribution** | One **central distribution center** — everything flows through it | Warehouse vs. distribution center |
| **Retail** | 6,000+ stores, refreshed **twice a week** | Short chain = fewer links to break |

---

## Zara — the distribution center is the advantage

Zara built one massive **central distribution center** in Arteixo, Spain.

- Every garment — for Tokyo, New York or São Paulo — passes through **this one facility**
- Garments arrive and ship within **hours**: the center is a **relay, not a storage room**
- A design sketch on Monday can be **on a store shelf by Friday**

> **This is the warehouse vs. distribution center distinction made real.** Zara does not store inventory, it **moves** inventory.

---

## Zara — supplier selection: speed over cost

Zara's supplier choices break the "cheapest wins" rule:

| Factor | Zara's choice |
|---|---|
| **Cost** | **Higher** — own factories in Spain cost more than outsourcing to Asia, accepted as the price of speed |
| **Quality** | **High** — in-house control over every step |
| **Efficiency** | **Maximum** — the 2-week turnaround is the first priority |
| **Convenience** | **Centralized** — one distribution center for the world |
| **Risk** | **Lower** — a short chain crosses fewer borders and has fewer links |

> **Zara traded cost for speed.** The five factors do not all point the same way, so a business must **choose which one to rank first — based on its strategy.**

---

## Zara — barriers to entry

Zara's supply chain is **nearly impossible to copy:**

- **Own factories** — rivals outsource and lose control
- **One central distribution center** — requires massive investment
- **2-week turnaround** — built over decades, not overnight
- **Small-batch, high-refresh model** — needs a different chain, not a faster one

> **The supply chain itself is the barrier to entry.** Rivals can copy a dress design in days; they cannot copy the **chain that delivers it in two weeks** — an exclusivity-style advantage built without any legal agreement.

---

## Zara — discussion questions

1. Zara's strategy is **speed**, not low price. How did that strategy **shape every stage** of the chain?
2. Zara built **its own factories in Spain** instead of outsourcing to cheaper countries. Which supplier factor did it **sacrifice**, which did it **prioritize**, and why?
3. Every garment in the world passes through Arteixo. Explain the difference between a **warehouse and a distribution center**, and why Zara's facility is the latter.
4. Zara's chain is called a **barrier to entry**. How is that different from a legal barrier such as an exclusive agreement — and what makes it harder to copy?

# Practice — 6 questions

**4 on one stimulus (Copper Kettle Candles)** · **2 standalone**

Read to yourself, pause, **commit to an answer** — then advance.

---

## Stimulus: Copper Kettle Candles

**Nadia Farouk** owns **Copper Kettle Candles**, pouring small batches by hand with premium wax and charging approximately **twice what machine-made candles cost.** Customers praise the quality and the custom scents.

Nadia buys wax from an **overseas supplier** at a price **20% below local** — but **2 of the last 6 shipments arrived weeks late**, and one contained **the wrong blend.** A boutique chain offers to **feature Copper Kettle in 12 stores** if Nadia signs an **exclusive retail rights agreement.**

> **Watch for three decision points:** production approach, supplier choice, and the exclusive agreement.

---

## Q1 — Classify production approach

Nadia's method of pouring small batches by hand with premium wax is best described as which?

- A. Mass production, because she sells in multiple locations
- B. Artisan production, because she relies on skilled labor and attention to detail in smaller quantities
- C. Outsourcing, because she buys wax from an overseas supplier
- D. A service supply chain, because she provides custom scents

---

## Q1 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Artisan production relies on **skilled labor** and close attention to detail, producing **smaller quantities.** Nadia pours by hand, uses premium materials and makes small batches — every marker points to artisan.

**Trap: C** — **buying inputs is not outsourcing.** Every producer buys raw materials: Nadia makes the candles herself and buys wax the way a baker buys flour.

**Trap: A** — selling in multiple locations does not change the **production method.** She could sell in 100 stores and still pour every candle by hand.

---

## Q2 — Supplier decision

Nadia's overseas wax supplier offers a price 20% below local, but 2 of 6 recent shipments arrived late and one had the wrong blend. Which analysis **best** supports a sound supplier decision?

- A. Choose the overseas supplier because lower cost always increases profit
- B. Choose the local supplier because reliability is the only factor that matters
- C. Weigh the lower cost against the risks — late deliveries, wrong shipments, damage to reputation — and consider a backup plan
- D. Choose the overseas supplier because customers will never know about delivery problems

---

## Q2 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** The five supplier factors are cost, quality, efficiency, convenience and **risk.** The overseas supplier wins on cost but loses on reliability and risk, so a sound decision **weighs all five factors** and includes a **backup plan.**

**Trap: A** — **"always" is an absolute.** Lower cost does not always raise profit when late deliveries cause stockouts and lost customers.

**Trap: B** — reliability is one factor, not the **only** factor. "Only" is as much of a trap as "always."

---

## Q3 — Exclusive retail agreement

The boutique chain offers to feature Copper Kettle in 12 stores if Nadia signs an exclusive retail rights agreement. This agreement would most directly create competitive advantage through which mechanism?

- A. Barriers to entry — the agreement locks up retail shelf space, keeping competitors out of those stores
- B. Cost leadership — selling in 12 stores reduces per-unit production costs
- C. Economies of scale — more stores means Nadia can switch to mass production
- D. Guaranteed long-term profits from the partnership

---

## Q3 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** An exclusive retail rights agreement is a **barrier to entry** — it locks up shelf space so competitors cannot access those 12 stores. That is one of the three strategy-to-chain mechanisms the exam tests.

**Trap: D** — **"guaranteed" is an absolute.** The deal shifts the odds; the chain could drop her line, close stores or renegotiate.

**Trap: C** — 12 stores do not mean Nadia **should** become a factory. The agreement gives her **shelf space**, not production capacity.

---

## Q4 — Order the supply chain

Which best identifies the order of the supply chain for a mass-produced good, from start to finish?

- A. Manufacturing, raw materials, retail store, warehouse, customer
- B. Raw materials, warehouse, manufacturing, customer, distribution center
- C. Distribution center, raw materials, manufacturing, warehouse, customer
- D. Raw materials, manufacturing, warehouse, distribution center or retail store, customer

---

## Q4 — Answer: **D**

**Why:** The chain runs in one direction: **raw materials to manufacturing to warehouse to distribution/retail to customer.**

**Trap: B** fails twice — it puts the warehouse **before** manufacturing (a warehouse stores **finished** goods), and it puts a distribution center **after the customer.**

---

## Q5 — Scaling table

**BatchBox Snacks scaled up last year:**

| | Before scaling | After scaling |
|---|---|---|
| Units | 12,000 | 30,000 |
| Revenue | $180,000 | $450,000 |
| Total costs | $120,000 | $240,000 |
| **Profit** | **$60,000** | **$210,000** |

Which conclusion is **best supported** by the table?

- A. Scaling failed, because total costs increased by $120,000
- B. Scaling succeeded, because revenue grew 150% against cost growth of 100%
- C. Scaling guarantees higher profit every year going forward
- D. The business should return to 12,000 units

---

## Q5 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Compute both growth rates — **revenue** $180,000 to $450,000 is **+150%**, **costs** $120,000 to $240,000 is **+100%**, and profit $60,000 to $210,000 confirms it. Revenue growth **outran** cost growth: that is the scaling mechanism.

**Trap: A** reads one number and stops — costs rise when output rises; the question is whether revenue rose **faster.**

**Trap: C** turns one good year into a permanent promise: the **"guarantees" alarm** rings here as everywhere.

---

## Q6 — Service supply chain

An **online tutoring company** hires tutors, develops lesson materials, and delivers sessions through a video platform. Which best describes this company's supply chain?

- A. Raw materials flowing to a manufacturing facility for assembly
- B. A warehouse storing finished lessons until customers order them
- C. Acquiring tutors, learning resources and a virtual delivery platform to provide the service
- D. Services cannot have supply chains

---

## Q6 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** A service supply chain acquires the **employees, the resources and the delivery systems** needed to provide the service — tutors, materials and a video platform are those three parts.

**Trap: B** borrows the goods chain: warehouses store **physical products**, and a tutoring session is **delivered live.**

**Trap: D** is the misconception this topic exists to correct — **services absolutely run supply chains**, built from people, resources and delivery systems instead of trucks and pallets.

---

## Wrap-up

- **Artisan** (skilled, small) vs. **mass production** (technology, large) — the choice follows **customer priorities, core competencies, competitive landscape**
- **Good's chain:** raw materials to manufacturing to warehouse to distribution/retail to customer
- **Service chain:** people + resources + delivery system — **no factory in sight**
- **A warehouse stores; a distribution center stores and forwards. A supplier feeds production; a distributor feeds the retail shelf**
- **Five supplier factors:** cost, quality, efficiency, convenience, **risk** (the one that hides) — **plus a backup plan**
- **Strategy shapes the chain:** low price to cost-cutting, quality to better inputs, barriers to exclusive agreements — and **the chain itself becomes the advantage**
- **Scaling:** revenue growth outpaces cost growth because **fixed costs spread across more units**

> **Get the chain wrong and the product never arrives. Get it right and the chain is the moat.**
