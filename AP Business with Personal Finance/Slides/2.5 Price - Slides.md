# Part 1: Pricing Strategies
### LO 2.5.A — Six ways to set a price

---

## Why does price matter?

Price is the **only element of the marketing mix that generates revenue**.

- Product costs money to develop
- Place costs money to distribute
- Promotion costs money to advertise
- **Price is where money comes back in**

> **Set the price too high → customers walk away.**
> **Set the price too low → you leave money on the table — or lose it.**

---

## The six pricing strategies

| # | Strategy | Core Idea |
|---|----------|----------|
| 1 | Value-Based | Price = what the customer believes it's worth |
| 2 | Competitive | Price = what competitors charge ± a little |
| 3 | Cost-Based | Price = cost + markup |
| 4 | Penetration | Price = low to grab market share, then rise |
| 5 | Dynamic | Price = shifts in real time with demand |
| 6 | Loss-Leader | Price = below cost on one item to sell others |

> **No strategy is "best" — the right one depends on the product, the market, and the goal.**

---

## Strategy 1 — Value-Based Pricing

**How it works:** Set the price based on what the customer is **willing to pay** — not what it costs you to make.

| Factor | Question |
|--------|---------|
| Customer perception | What do they think this product is worth? |
| Unique benefits | What can they get here that they can't get elsewhere? |
| Brand strength | Do they trust this brand enough to pay more? |

**Example:** A Starbucks coffee costs ~$0.50 to make but sells for $5+. Customers pay for the experience, the brand, and the convenience.

> **Value-based pricing works when the product offers something unique that competitors cannot easily copy.**

---

## Strategy 2 — Competitive Pricing

**How it works:** Set the price by looking at what **competitors charge** and positioning accordingly — slightly above, slightly below, or matching.

**Example:** Gas stations on the same street often charge within $0.02 of each other.

> **Competitive pricing works when many similar products exist and customers compare prices before buying.**

---

## Strategy 3 — Cost-Based Pricing

**How it works:** Calculate the **total cost per unit** (materials, labor, overhead), then add a **markup** for profit.

$$\text{Price} = \text{Cost per unit} + \text{Desired profit (markup)}$$

**Example:** A T-shirt costs $8 to produce. The business wants $4 profit per unit. Price = $12.

> **Cost-based pricing is simple and predictable — but it ignores what the customer is willing to pay.**

---

## Strategy 4 — Penetration Pricing

**How it works:** Set a **low initial price** to attract customers quickly and gain market share, then **gradually raise** the price once the customer base is established.

**The logic:**
1. Low price → many customers try the product
2. Many customers → economies of scale → lower costs
3. Lower costs + loyal customers → gradually raise price
4. Large market share → barrier to new competitors

> **Penetration pricing sacrifices short-term profit for long-term market dominance.**

---

## Xiaomi — The penetration pricing story

In 2011, Xiaomi entered the smartphone market with the **Mi 1** at **1,999 RMB (~$310)**.

Competitors with similar specs (Samsung, HTC) charged **4,000–5,000 RMB**.

**What happened:**
- Xiaomi sold **high-spec phones at budget prices**
- The original plan was 1,499 RMB, but manufacturing costs exceeded expectations
- Xiaomi sold at a **near-loss** or **below cost** on early models
- Built a massive customer base in China and emerging markets

> **Xiaomi's value proposition: "Flagship specs, half the price."**

---

## Xiaomi — From penetration to premium

| Year | Model | Price (RMB) | Stage |
|------|-------|-------------|-------|
| 2011 | Mi 1 | 1,999 | Penetration — entering the market |
| 2014 | Mi 4 | 1,999 | Holding price — building loyalty |
| 2019 | Mi 9 | 2,999 | Gradual increase — trust established |
| 2020 | Mi 10 | 3,999 | Premium push — value-based pricing |
| 2024 | Mi 15 | 4,499 | Premium positioning — brand equity earned |
| 2024 | SU7 Ultra | 814,900 | Full premium — luxury car segment |

> **Over 13 years, Xiaomi went from "cheapest flagship" to "premium brand" — the penetration pricing playbook in action.**

---

## Xiaomi — The 5% rule

Xiaomi made a public commitment: **hardware net profit margin will never exceed 5%**.

This is written into the company's **articles of association** — a legal document.

**Why?**
- Reinforces the "value for money" brand promise
- Customers trust that Xiaomi is not overcharging
- Profits come from **internet services** (72.3% gross margin) and **ecosystem products**

> **Xiaomi's pricing power grew not from charging more on hardware — but from building an ecosystem customers don't want to leave.**

## Strategy 5 — Dynamic Pricing

**How it works:** Adjust the price in **real time** based on current demand, supply, time, or other conditions.

**Examples:**
- **Uber/Lyft:** Prices surge during rush hour or bad weather
- **Airlines:** Same seat costs $200 in January, $800 in July
- **Hotels:** Weekend rates ≠ weekday rates
- **Amazon:** Prices on some products change multiple times per day

> **Dynamic pricing works when demand fluctuates and customers accept that prices change.**

---

## Strategy 6 — Loss-Leader Pricing

**How it works:** Sell a **specific product below cost** to draw customers in — expecting they will also buy **profitable items**.

**The math:**
- Lose $1 on Product A (the "leader")
- Customer also buys Products B, C, D (profitable)
- Net profit from the basket > loss on Product A

**Examples:**
- Grocery stores selling milk below cost
- Printers sold cheaply, ink cartridges sold at high margins
- Video game consoles sold at a loss, games sold at profit

> **Loss-leader pricing works when the business sells many products and can afford a loss on one.**

---

## Costco — The loss-leader master

Costco is the world's master of loss-leader pricing:

| Product | Costco Price | What It Really Is |
|---------|-------------|------------------|
| Rotisserie chicken | **$4.99** | Costco loses **$30–40 million/year** on chicken — on purpose |
| Hot dog + drink | **$1.50** | Same price since **1985** — over 40 years without a raise |
| Kirkland Signature products | **At or near cost** | Costco caps markup at **14%** (competitors: 25–50%) |

> **The chicken is not a product — it is an advertisement for the $65 membership.**

---

## Costco — The membership model

Costco's real product is the **membership card**, not the goods on the shelf.

| Metric | Number |
|--------|--------|
| Annual membership fee | $65 (US) |
| Cardholders worldwide | 135+ million |
| Annual membership revenue | **$4.8 billion** |
| Membership renewal rate | **90%+** |
| Gross margin on goods | **~12%** (vs. Walmart's ~24%) |

**The math:**
- Costco makes almost **zero profit** on selling products
- **100% of profit** comes from membership fees
- Loss leaders (chicken, hot dog) keep customers renewing

> **Costco doesn't make money when you buy. Costco makes money when you come back.**

---

## Costco — The 14% rule

Costco has a company rule: **no product can be marked up more than 14%**.

- Most retailers mark up **25–50%**
- Costco's cap means customers **always get a deal**
- This builds **trust** — customers know Costco is not gouging them
- Kirkland Signature (Costco's private label) has a slightly higher cap of **15%**

> **The 14% cap is Costco's version of Xiaomi's 5% rule — a public pricing commitment that builds customer trust.**

| 5 | Price is the only marketing mix element that **generates revenue** |

# Part 2: Pricing Power
### LO 2.5.B — Who can raise prices and who cannot?

---

## What is pricing power?

**Pricing power** = the ability to raise prices **without losing customers** to competitors.

Some businesses can raise prices and customers stay:
- **Apple** raises iPhone prices every year — customers line up
- **Dyson** charges 2–3x competitors — customers pay

Other businesses raise prices and customers leave:
- A gas station raises prices by $0.10 — drivers go next door
- A generic cereal raises price — shoppers switch brands

> **Pricing power is determined by how customers respond — not by what the business wants.**

---

## What creates pricing power?

| Factor | More Pricing Power | Less Pricing Power |
|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Brand identity | Strong, trusted brand | Weak or unknown brand |
| Product uniqueness | No close substitutes | Many similar alternatives |
| Switching costs | Hard for customers to leave | Easy to switch |
| Customer loyalty | Built on trust and quality | Purely price-sensitive |
| Demand type | Inelastic (customers keep buying) | Elastic (customers leave when price rises) |

> **The more unique your product and the more loyal your customers, the more pricing power you have.**

---

## Price elasticity of demand

**Price elasticity** measures how **sensitive** customers are to price changes.

| Type | What Happens | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Elastic** demand | Small price increase → big drop in sales | Generic bottled water — switch brands easily |
| **Inelastic** demand | Price increase → sales barely change | Insulin — patients have no alternative |

> **Businesses with elastic demand have LESS pricing power.**
> **Businesses with inelastic demand have MORE pricing power.**

**Note:** Calculating elasticity is **excluded** from the AP Exam, but you need to understand the concept.

---

## Xiaomi — Pricing power evolution

| Phase | Pricing Power | Why |
|-------|--------------|-----|
| 2011–2015 | **Low** | Customers were price-sensitive; if Xiaomi raised prices, they would switch to other budget brands |
| 2016–2019 | **Growing** | Ecosystem lock-in (phones + wearables + smart home); switching costs rising |
| 2020–2024 | **Strong** | Brand premium established; Mi 10 at 3,999 RMB sold well; customers trust the brand |
| 2024 (SU7 Ultra) | **Very strong** | 814,900 RMB for a car — customers pay for the Xiaomi ecosystem + brand |

> **Xiaomi's pricing power grew as the ecosystem expanded — leaving Xiaomi means leaving the whole ecosystem.**

---

## Costco — Pricing power through loyalty

Costco's pricing power comes from a different source than Xiaomi's:

| Factor | How Costco Creates It |
|--------|----------------------|
| Membership lock-in | You paid $65 — you want to "use" your membership |
| Trust | 14% cap means customers know they're getting a fair deal |
| Exclusive products | Kirkland Signature only available at Costco |
| Bulk buying | Once you buy in bulk, you plan your shopping around Costco |
| Renewal rate | **90%+** — customers choose to come back every year |

> **Costco's pricing power is not about charging more — it's about keeping customers from leaving.**

---

## Your turn — Pricing power check

> A local bakery sells artisan bread for $8/loaf. A new supermarket opens nearby and sells bread for $3/loaf. The bakery's sales drop by only 10%.

**Does the bakery have pricing power? What factors might explain why most customers stayed?**

---

## Your turn — Pricing power check answer

**Yes — the bakery has pricing power.** Despite a $5 price difference, 90% of customers stayed.

**Possible explanations:**
- **Brand loyalty** — customers trust the bakery's quality
- **Product uniqueness** — artisan bread is not the same as supermarket bread (no close substitute)
- **Switching costs** — customers value the experience, the relationship, the routine
- **Inelastic demand** — for these customers, artisan bread is not easily replaced

> **Pricing power = customers choose you even when cheaper alternatives exist.**

---

## 🔑 Exam points to remember — Pricing Power

| # | Point |
|---|-------|
| 1 | **Pricing power** = ability to raise prices without losing customers |
| 2 | Strong brand + unique product + high switching costs = **more** pricing power |
| 3 | **Elastic** demand = customers are price-sensitive = **less** pricing power |
| 4 | **Inelastic** demand = customers keep buying despite price increase = **more** pricing power |
| 5 | Pricing power is determined by **customer behavior**, not by what the business wants |

# Part 3: Legal Constraints
### LO 2.5.C — What businesses cannot do with prices

---

## Three illegal pricing practices

| Practice | What It Is | Why It's Illegal |
|----------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Price Fixing** | Competitors secretly agree to set the same price | Eliminates real competition; customers pay artificially high prices |
| **Price Gouging** | Raising prices to unfair levels during a crisis | Exploits people in emergencies (e.g., $50 for water during a hurricane) |
| **Price Discrimination** | Charging different prices to different groups for the same product | Illegal when based on race, nationality, sex, or other protected status |

> **These laws exist to protect customers and ensure fair competition.**

---

## Price fixing — Collusion

**What it is:** Two or more competitors **secretly agree** to set prices at a certain level — usually higher than the market would naturally set.

**Example:** Three airlines agree to charge $500 for the same route. Without collusion, competition would drive the price down to $350.

**Why it's harmful:**
- Customers pay more than they should
- Competition is an illusion — the "market" is rigged
- New competitors cannot enter because the price is artificially controlled

> **Price fixing is illegal in the US, EU, China, and most countries. It is a criminal offense.**

---

## Price gouging — Crisis exploitation

**What it is:** Raising prices to **unfair levels** during a crisis or emergency when customers have no alternative.

**Examples:**
- Selling bottled water for $50 during a hurricane
- Charging $200 for a box of masks during a pandemic
- Raising hotel prices 500% during a natural disaster

**Legal status:**
- Illegal in **many US states** during declared emergencies
- Illegal in **many countries** including China
- Laws vary by jurisdiction — some states have no specific price gouging law

> **Price gouging exploits desperation — the customer has no choice and no alternative.**

---

## Price discrimination — Different prices for different groups

**What it is:** Charging **different prices to different customer segments** for the **same product**.

**When it IS legal:**
- Student discounts (age-based, not protected status)
- Senior citizen discounts
- Bulk pricing (buy more, pay less per unit)
- Geographic pricing (different prices in different markets)

**When it is ILLEGAL:**
- Charging more based on **race, nationality, sex, or other protected status**
- Example: Charging higher prices in neighborhoods based on the ethnicity of residents

> **Not all price discrimination is illegal — only when it targets protected characteristics.**

---

## Your turn — Legal check

> During a severe winter storm, a hardware store raises the price of generators from $300 to $1,200. The store owner says: "I'm just responding to high demand."

**Is this price gouging, dynamic pricing, or both? What makes the difference?**

---

## Your turn — Legal check answer

**This is most likely price gouging.**

The key difference:
- **Dynamic pricing** adjusts based on normal market conditions (Uber surge pricing on a Friday night)
- **Price gouging** exploits a **crisis** when customers have **no alternative** and **no time to shop around**

In this scenario:
- A winter storm is a crisis/emergency
- Customers need generators to stay safe — no alternative exists
- Raising the price 4x exploits desperation

> **The line between dynamic pricing and price gouging is context: normal demand fluctuation vs. crisis exploitation.**

---

## 🔑 Exam points to remember — Legal Constraints

| # | Point |
|---|-------|
| 1 | **Price fixing** = competitors collude to set prices = illegal everywhere |
| 2 | **Price gouging** = crisis exploitation = illegal in many jurisdictions |
| 3 | **Price discrimination** = different prices for different groups = illegal only when based on protected status |
| 4 | Student/senior discounts and bulk pricing are **legal** forms of price discrimination |
| 5 | The line between **dynamic pricing** and **price gouging** is whether the situation is a normal market or a crisis |

## Practice — Stimulus

**Peak & Trail** is a mid-range outdoor gear company that sells hiking boots.

| Year | Price per pair | Pairs sold | Revenue |
|------|---------------|------------|---------|
| 1 | $85 | 12,000 | $1,020,000 |
| 2 | $95 | 11,200 | $1,064,000 |
| 3 | $110 | 9,000 | $990,000 |

In Year 4, a competitor launches a similar boot at $75.

Peak & Trail's marketing team debates three options:
- **Option A:** Drop the price to $70 to match the competitor
- **Option B:** Keep the price at $110 and invest in brand advertising
- **Option C:** Launch a premium line at $150 with advanced materials

---

## Practice — Q1

> In Year 2, Peak & Trail raised the price from $85 to $95 and lost 800 customers, but revenue still increased. What does this suggest about the demand for Peak & Trail boots at that price range?

> A. Demand was perfectly elastic — any price increase causes customers to leave
>
> B. Demand was relatively inelastic — the price increase did not cause enough lost sales to reduce revenue
>
> C. Demand was perfectly inelastic — the price increase had no effect on sales
>
> D. Demand was elastic — the company lost revenue from the price increase

---

## Practice — Q1 answer

**Answer: B.**

Price rose from $85 to $95 (+12%). Sales dropped from 12,000 to 11,200 (−6.7%). Revenue rose from $1,020,000 to $1,064,000 (+4.3%). The percentage drop in sales was smaller than the percentage increase in price, so demand was relatively inelastic in this range.

**Traps:**
- **A** — "Perfectly elastic" means ANY price increase drives ALL customers away; 800 out of 12,000 stayed
- **C** — "Perfectly inelastic" means sales would not change at all; 800 customers left
- **D** — Revenue increased, not decreased; elastic demand would have reduced revenue

> **If revenue goes UP when price goes UP, demand is inelastic in that range.**

---

## Practice — Q2

> In Year 3, Peak & Trail raised the price from $95 to $110 and revenue dropped from $1,064,000 to $990,000. What changed?

> A. Demand became more inelastic at higher prices
>
> B. Demand became more elastic at higher prices — more customers were sensitive to the higher price
>
> C. The company should have lowered the price instead
>
> D. Revenue dropped because of external factors, not the price change

---

## Practice — Q2 answer

**Answer: B.**

At $95, demand was inelastic (price up, revenue up). At $110, demand became elastic (price up, revenue down). The price increase from $95 to $110 crossed a threshold where enough customers stopped buying to reduce total revenue.

**Traps:**
- **A** — The opposite happened: at higher prices, more customers left, making demand MORE elastic
- **C** — This is a strategy recommendation, not an explanation of what happened
- **D** — The question provides no evidence of external factors; the price change is the most direct cause

> **Demand elasticity is not fixed — it can shift from inelastic to elastic as the price rises.**

---

## Practice — Q3

> A competitor launches a similar boot at $75. If Peak & Trail chooses **Option A** (drop price to $70), which pricing strategy is this?

> A. Value-based pricing
>
> B. Penetration pricing
>
> C. Competitive pricing
>
> D. Loss-leader pricing

---

## Practice — Q3 answer

**Answer: C.**

Option A sets the price **relative to the competitor** ($70 vs. $75) — this is competitive pricing. The price is not based on costs (cost-based), customer perception (value-based), or an initial low price designed to rise later (penetration). It is a direct response to a competitor's price.

**Traps:**
- **B** — Penetration pricing is a **strategy for entering a market** with a low price that will later increase; Peak & Trail is already in the market
- **D** — Loss-leader means selling below cost; there is no evidence the boot costs more than $70 to produce
- **A** — Value-based pricing would focus on what customers believe the boot is worth, not what the competitor charges

> **"Match the competitor" = competitive pricing. "Low price to enter and raise later" = penetration pricing.**

---

## Practice — Q4

> If Peak & Trail chooses **Option C** (premium line at $150), which pricing strategy is this most aligned with?

> A. Penetration pricing — attracting new customers with a low price
>
> B. Dynamic pricing — adjusting based on demand
>
> C. Value-based pricing — charging based on what customers believe premium materials are worth
>
> D. Cost-based pricing — simply adding a markup to the higher material costs

---

## Practice — Q4 answer

**Answer: C.**

A premium line at $150 with "advanced materials" targets customers who perceive higher value in quality, durability, and performance. The price is set based on what these customers are willing to pay — not on the cost of materials or on competitor prices.

**Traps:**
- **A** — $150 is not a "low initial price"; it is the opposite of penetration
- **B** — There is no indication the price will change based on real-time demand
- **D** — Cost-based pricing would calculate materials + markup; "advanced materials" suggests the price reflects perceived value, not just added cost

> **"Premium product + what customers are willing to pay" = value-based pricing.**

---

## Practice — Q5

> A sports equipment retailer approaches Peak & Trail and says: "Sell us your Year 2 boot at $60 per pair. We will only sell it in our stores, not online." Peak & Trail agrees, and the retailer sells it at $95 — the same price Peak & Trail charges on its own website.

> What type of legal pricing constraint is **most relevant** to this arrangement?

> A. Price fixing
>
> B. Price gouging
>
> C. Price discrimination
>
> D. No legal constraint — this is a normal wholesale arrangement

---

## Practice — Q5 answer

**Answer: D.**

This is a normal wholesale/retail arrangement. Peak & Trail sells at wholesale ($60) and the retailer sets its own retail price ($95). There is no agreement between competitors (price fixing), no crisis exploitation (price gouging), and no different pricing to different customer groups based on protected status (price discrimination).

**Traps:**
- **A** — Price fixing requires an agreement between **competitors**; Peak & Trail and the retailer are not competitors — they are in a supply chain relationship
- **B** — Price gouging requires a crisis or emergency; this is a routine business transaction
- **C** — The retailer charges the same price ($95) to all its customers; there is no discrimination between customer groups

> **Wholesale pricing with retailer markup is standard business practice — not a pricing law violation.**

---

## Practice — Q6

> Peak & Trail decides to charge $110 for boots sold in its US stores but only $85 for the same boots sold in Southeast Asian markets. Is this price discrimination, and is it legal?

> A. It is price discrimination and it is illegal because it charges different prices for the same product
>
> B. It is not price discrimination because different markets have different costs
>
> C. It is price discrimination, but it is legal because geographic pricing is not based on a protected status
>
> D. It is price gouging because the US price is higher than necessary

---

## Practice — Q6 answer

**Answer: C.**

This is a form of price discrimination (different prices for the same product), but it is **legal**. Geographic pricing is a common business practice. Price discrimination is only illegal when based on **protected characteristics** (race, nationality, sex, etc.) — not when based on market conditions, purchasing power, or geography.

**Traps:**
- **A** — "Price discrimination" and "illegal" are not the same thing; many forms of discrimination are legal
- **B** — Even if costs are the same, geographic pricing is still a form of discrimination — but a legal one
- **D** — Price gouging requires a crisis; this is a routine market-based pricing decision

> **Price discrimination ≠ illegal. It is only illegal when targeting protected status.**

---

## Trap patterns to watch

| # | Trap type | Where it appears |
|---|-----------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Revenue up + price up = inelastic**; revenue down + price up = elastic | Q1, Q2 |
| 2 | **Elasticity changes with price level** — inelastic at low prices, elastic at high prices | Q2 |
| 3 | **"Match competitor" = competitive**, not penetration (penetration = enter market then raise) | Q3 |

| 6 | **Geographic pricing is legal** price discrimination — only protected-status discrimination is illegal | Q6 |
