# 1.6 Business Ethics
### AP Business with Personal Finance · Unit 1

Lesson deck — **concepts + practice**

---

## Opening question

You know what the business is *for* (1.5) — vision, mission, values.

> **But what happens when the profitable move and the right move point in different directions?**

That's today's question.

---

## Today's goals

By the end of this lesson you can:

1. Explain how and why businesses **encourage ethical behavior** — codes of conduct, training, repercussions, and modeling
2. Explain how business leaders **respond to ethical dilemmas** — stakeholder analysis and the greatest-benefit / least-harm framework

**Running story continues:** the snack box has a new helper — and the helper suggests **hiding the fact that one bag of chips is past its sell-by date** to avoid a loss on the weekend's revenue.

## What is unethical behavior?

**Unethical behavior** = actions that violate moral or professional standards.

The CED names **three forms:**

| Form | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Falsifying or concealing information** | Lying on a label, hiding a known flaw, not disclosing a defect |
| **Misusing company property** | Using business resources for personal gain without authorization |
| **Causing harm** to employees or customers | Ignoring safety standards, selling a product known to be dangerous |

## Business ethics vs. core values (1.5)

| | **Core values** (1.5) | **Business ethics** (1.6) |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | The **beliefs a business chooses** for itself | Standards of **right and wrong that apply to everyone** |
| **Who sets it** | The business itself — internal, self-declared | Moral + professional standards — external |
| **Can it vary?** | Yes — every business picks its own | No — it is a **floor** beneath every business |
| **The question** | "Did we act like who we say we are?" | "Did this violate a moral or professional standard?" |
| **When it fails** | **Decoration** (1.5) — abandoned at the first real cost | **Unethical behavior** — one of the CED's three forms |

> **Core values are a promise a business makes to itself; business ethics is a line it cannot cross no matter what it promises.**
> - Leaving honesty off its value list does **not** make concealing a defect ethical
> - Abandoning "creativity" fails a value but breaks no ethic
> - The bridge into 1.6: values get codified into a **code of conduct**, and the vision becomes **Lens 2** below

---

## Concealing information — and who does it

> The helper's suggestion — **hide the expired chips** — is **concealing information.** The bag is small; the customer is still paying for something the seller knows is false.

Unethical behavior **can occur at all levels** of a business — owner, manager, or entry-level employee. The **source** never changes the classification.

- A manager hiding a safety report is the **same form** as a cashier hiding an expired bag
- The **behavior** is what the question asks you to name — not the job title of the person doing it

## Incentive structures — the hidden driver

**Incentive** = a reward that encourages a particular action.

The common driver behind unethical behavior is an incentive that rewards **output without checking how the output was produced.**

> *If the snack box paid a bonus for every Saturday with zero unsold inventory, what would the helper learn to do with the last few bags?*

The incentive rewards **zero leftover** and never asks **how** — so the helper learns to hide expiring bags instead of reporting them.

## How businesses encourage ethical behavior

**Four tools** the exam tests — each works differently:

| Tool | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Code of conduct** | A written document outlining expected behavior and standards |
| **Training** | Teaching employees to follow ethical guidelines |
| **Internal repercussions** | Consequences imposed **after** a transgression (warnings, suspension, termination) |
| **Modeling ethical behavior** | Leaders **demonstrate** the standard themselves — employees learn by watching |

> **Sort them by when they act:** codes and training arrive **before** any behavior; repercussions arrive **after**; modeling runs **continuously.**

🎬 **案例视频** — 胖东来成功秘诀：做完一家良心企业能做的全部（3:48）· https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1Xx4y1B7Em
> *四件工具一起用：明文标准 + 员工培训 + 严格执行 + 老板以身作则。*

---

## Acting before the behavior: code and training

#### Code of conduct

- A written document defining the business's ethical expectations — clear boundaries employees can reference
- Usually covers conflicts of interest, confidentiality, customer treatment
- Only as strong as its **enforcement**: 安然 wrote a 64-page code and still fell

#### Training

- Moves ethics from a poster on the wall to **practice scenarios**
- Helps employees recognize a dilemma **before** they act
- Must be **ongoing**, not one-time

> *The snack box writes on the cooler lid: "Check every date. If it's past, pull it — no exceptions."* That's a one-line code of conduct; Marcus's monthly fifteen-minute session on rush-order defects is the training.

---

## Acting after the behavior: internal repercussions

**Internal repercussion** = a consequence imposed **after** an ethical transgression.

- Can range from a written warning to termination
- Exists to **discourage repeat behavior**
- Must be **consistent and proportional** to be credible

> **Direction test:** a repercussion pushes **backward** — it arrives after the behavior and deters the next occurrence.

> **"Internal" means the business does the punishing** — warning, suspension, termination. Luckin Coffee's SEC settlement and delisting were **external** consequences, which is a different CED idea.

---

## Acting continuously: modeling

**Modeling** = a leader **demonstrates** ethical behavior in front of employees, so the standard is learned by **observation, not instruction.**

- Works because employees watch what leaders **do**, not just what they **say**
- Most powerful when it **costs** the leader something (time, money, short-term revenue)

> *Marcus reprints the flawed order at his own expense, explains why to both employees, and reads them the message he sent the customer.* That's modeling — the value survived contact with a real cost.

> 🎬 **案例视频** — 于东来：用真品换真心，员工愿给他打一辈子工（7:54）· https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV14x4y1e7jo
> *老板怎么做，员工就怎么学——价值观是被「看」会的，不是被「讲」会的。*

---

## Incentives vs. repercussions — the direction test

**The pair the exam loves to swap:**

| | **Incentive** | **Repercussion** |
|---|---|---|
| Arrives… | **Before** the behavior | **After** the behavior |
| Direction | **Pulls toward** an action | **Pushes back** against an action |
| Example | Bonus for on-schedule completion | Written warning for concealing a defect |

> **Sort by direction, not by topic.** A bonus that rewards speed without checking disclosure is an **incentive** leaning on employees to cut corners — it is not a repercussion, and the incentive didn't say "cheat": it said "produce results," and nobody checked the method.

# Case study — 康美药业 (Kangmei)

**When the punishment doesn't fit the crime, the incentive stays.**

🎬 [A股造假第一案 · 探寻"药王"康美18年股市飙升奇迹](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1bP4y1b7NH)（中国商业史37 · 冲浪普拉斯）

---

## Kangmei — 虚增 300 亿，罚 60 万

**Kangmei Pharmaceutical (康美药业)** — a blue-chip darling once worth **~¥140 billion**. Across the 2016–2018 statements it inflated its reported cash and hid **¥11.6 billion** of diverted related-party funds: still the largest financial fraud in A-share history.

The 2019 penalty was the old Securities Law's **statutory maximum: ¥600,000** — a reward thousands of times the fine, which the internet called **罚酒三杯** (a token cup of wine). The cost arrived later, on four other lines:

| Where | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Law** | 2020: the Securities Law raised the cap for this offense to **¥10 million** |
| **Civil** | First-ever securities collective lawsuit: **¥2.459 billion** to 50,000+ investors — executives *and the auditor* jointly liable |
| **Criminal** | Chairman Ma Xingtian sentenced to **12 years** |
| **Market** | Value fell from **¥140 billion** to **~¥22 billion** — 85% destroyed |

---

## Kangmei — why the repercussion failed

> **A repercussion only works when it is consistent and proportional to the act it punishes.**

- **Proportional?** A ¥600,000 fine against a ¥30-billion fraud is not, so it did not discourage the behavior
- **Credible?** When the expected penalty is a rounding error next to the gain, executives calculate — and cheat
- **Deterrent?** The behavior changed only once the punishment **grew to match the crime**

> **When repercussions fail, the incentive structure quietly wins — and the cost just moves to a different line on the statement.**

---

## Why ethics matter — the two chains

Ethical practice **attracts customers and employees** and contributes to **brand loyalty.** The chain runs both ways:

> **Ethical behavior** → trust from customers and employees → loyalty → **higher revenue, lower costs**

> **Unethical behavior**, or a weak response to it → lost trust → customers leave, employees disengage → **lower revenue, higher costs**

> **A business's response to an ethical lapse shapes customer relationships, employee relationships and public perception — all of which land on the revenue and cost lines.**

> *大众作弊曝光后信任与销量双跌；胖东来反过来把良心做成了护城河。*

## The reverse chain in real time — 星宇股份

**Xingyu (星宇股份, 601799)** — China's auto-lamp leader, supplying VW, Mercedes and BMW. In August 2026, one month after **440 new graduates** started work, HR offered a forced choice: "resign voluntarily" with half a month's pay, or move to the assembly line at operator wages. **107 resigned.**

| Chain stage | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Unethical behavior** | Batch "persuaded resignations" — the labor bureau called the process crude; the harm landed on **employees**, the CED's third form |
| **Trust collapses** | Its own HK IPO filing disclosed **~¥122 million** of social-insurance underpayment; outsourcing hours rose 3.5× in three years |
| **Customers act** | **VW China opened a supply-chain investigation**; no court had ruled — graduates sent evidence straight to the OEMs' compliance channels |
| **Revenue and costs** | Value reportedly fell **~¥3 billion in a week**, the A+H listing is in question, and the "lamp leader" label now repels the engineers it needs |

> **Reputation needs no law — and in B2B, the customer's compliance department is the police.** *(Links to 1.8: supply-chain ethics audits. Ongoing case: some items are media reports, not confirmed findings.)*

## What is an ethical dilemma?

**Ethical dilemma** = a situation where a **core value** (transparency, fairness, empathy) conflicts with **another core value** or with **a business goal or practice.**

- Both sides have a **legitimate claim** — that's what makes it a dilemma
- The question is always **"What should I do?"** — not "Did someone do something wrong?"

> *The snack box:* **honesty** (tell the family the chips are past date) versus the **business goal** (sell everything, maximize Saturday revenue). Both claims are legitimate, so there is **no cost-free answer.**

> 🎬 **案例视频** — 携程大数据杀熟：越忠实越被宰？（3:09）· https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1YXGP6iErd
> *合规定价 vs 公平透明——两边都有正当理由，这才叫 dilemma。*

---

## Dilemma vs. unethical behavior — the sorting rule

| | Ethical dilemma | Unethical behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Trade-offs | **Both sides** have legitimate claims | **One side** is clearly wrong |
| The question | "What should I do?" | "Did someone do something wrong?" |
| Example | Honesty vs. revenue goal | Concealing a known defect |

> **If one side is clearly wrong, it is unethical behavior. If both sides have legitimate claims, it is a dilemma.** Sort by that test, not by how uncomfortable the situation feels.

> **Exam habit:** classify the **specific action** described. The dilemma existed *before* the helper spoke; the suggestion to **hide the defect** is already unethical behavior, not a dilemma.

🎬 **案例视频** — 瑞幸咖啡事件来龙去脉（2:46）· https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1LQ4y1M7AQ
> *伪造 22 亿营收不是 dilemma：只有一边是对的，另一边干脆是错的。*

## Stakeholders — who has a stake?

**Stakeholder** = any individual or group affected by a business's decisions.

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal** | **Direct involvement** in operations, decisions and outcomes | Owners, managers, employees |
| **External** | Not employed or directly involved, but holds a **vested interest** | Customers, government agencies, community members |

> **The sorting rule is direct involvement — importance and seniority play no part.**
> - An employee who sweeps the floor daily is **internal**, alongside the owners
> - The most loyal weekly customer is still **external**: they can buy or leave, but they do not run the business
> - The government sets the rules and stays **external**

---

## Responding to an ethical dilemma — two lenses

#### Lens 1 — stakeholder arithmetic

- Weigh the **benefits and costs** of each option **for every stakeholder group**, internal and external
- Choose the option with the **greatest total benefit** or the **least total harm** — a net calculation, not the loudest voice

#### Lens 2 — vision and goals

- Choose the option most **consistent** with the business's **vision and/or goals**
- If the vision already contains the value in question, the answer may be **built in**

> *The snack box's vision (1.5): "Game days where no family scrambles."* Selling expired chips scrambles trust — Lens 2 settles the dilemma before the math starts.

---

## Which lens? The question stem decides

- The stem says **"consider the stakeholders"** → **Lens 1** (stakeholder arithmetic)
- The stem says **"consistent with the vision"** → **Lens 2**
- The stem gives **both** → both matter, but the vision is the tiebreaker

> **When Lens 1 and Lens 2 point the same way, the case is strong. When they conflict, the prompt signals which lens to lead with** — so don't guess the priority, read the question.

> **Wrong lens = wrong answer**, even when the reasoning is sound.

---

## Stakeholder arithmetic — worked example

A furniture maker considers switching to a cheaper overseas supplier whose delays would reach customers.

| Option | Internal stakeholders | External stakeholders | **Net** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Keep current** | −$90,000 (higher input prices) | +$70,000 (reliable deliveries) | **−$20,000** |
| **Switch** | +$90,000 (savings) | −$150,000 (late/canceled orders) | **−$60,000** |

Both options hurt — so the method picks the **least total harm** → keep current.

> **Always sum across every stakeholder column.** An option that pays insiders by costing outsiders more is exactly what the arithmetic is built to catch.

# Case study — Ctrip (携程)

**When a business has the data to charge loyal customers more — should it?**

🎬 [在携程面前，互联网大厂都是弟中弟](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV12drSBpEnv)（6 min · 十万个品牌故事 · 短版入门）

🎬 [【苏星河】互联网真的会杀熟？外卖软件的秘密…](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1HWQiBPEH1)（13 min · 苏星河 · 157万播放 · 跨平台完整版）

---

## Ctrip — the dilemma, and how 杀熟 works

**Ctrip (携程)** is China's largest online travel agency — hotels, flights, train tickets, packages — with over half the market.

**大数据杀熟** reads each user's booking frequency, device, search history and price sensitivity to find their **maximum willingness to pay**, then charges **loyal, frequent users more** than new ones for the **same room, the same night.**

> A user who books 20 trips a year sees **¥500**; a first-time user sees **¥380** for the identical room. The algorithm knows the loyal user is **less likely to switch**, so it charges more.

> **Is this smart pricing — or exploiting trust?** That's the dilemma. Short term the shareholders smile; long term the loyal users leave.

> 🎬 **案例视频** — 大数据「杀熟」太可怕！坑你没商量（4:48）· https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV19E411r7MD
> *算法算的不是成本，是你有多不容易换平台。*

---

## Ctrip — the two sides

| **Core value: fairness + transparency** | **Business goal: profit maximization** |
|---|---|
| Loyal customers should be **rewarded**, not penalized | Dynamic pricing is standard in airlines and hotels |
| Users don't know they're seeing a different price — **no transparency** | Analytics lets the company match price to willingness-to-pay |
| Exploiting trust **destroys brand loyalty** over time | Higher revenue per user → more profit → more investment |

- **Legally**, Ctrip may price different users differently; **ethically**, it penalizes loyalty without telling anyone
- **Neither side is frivolous** — that is the exam's threshold for a genuine dilemma

---

## Ctrip — the stakeholder arithmetic

| | Stakeholder | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal** | Ctrip / investors | **+** Higher revenue per loyal user |
| **External** | Loyal customers | **−** Pay more for the same product; feel deceived |
| **External** | New customers | **+** Pay less, subsidized by loyal users |
| **External** | Competitors (Meituan, Fliggy) | **+** Betrayed loyal users switch to them |

> **Short term:** internal stakeholders gain, no immediate cost. **Long term:** trust erodes and the revenue gain is temporary.

When users **discovered** the practice — screenshots went viral on Weibo — the betrayed regulars **switched.** The profit was bought with the company's most valuable asset: **repeat customers.**

---

## Ctrip — the resolution question

**Pair up. 2 minutes.** If you were Ctrip's CEO, which approach would you take?

1. **Keep 大数据杀熟** — it's legal, it maximizes short-term revenue, and most users never find out
2. **Stop the practice** — reward loyalty with **lower** prices, sacrifice short-term revenue for long-term trust
3. **Transparent dynamic pricing** — show all users the same base price, but offer **loyalty discounts** openly

> **Lens 1:** which option produces the greatest total benefit or least total harm across those four groups? **Lens 2:** what would a company that says *"make travel easier"* do?

---

## Answer-choice traps

#### The absolutes trap

Watch for **"always," "never," "guarantee"** in answer choices:

- *"Reliable suppliers **always** increase long-run profits"* → the right decision reached by a **broken reason**, and "always" is the tell
- *"A sincere apology **guarantees** customers will return"* → a good response **improves the odds and promises nothing**

#### The legal-factor trap

Answer choices that **borrow from Topic 1.3 (PESTEL):**

- *"Profitability is unaffected unless a law was broken"* → legal consequences are one risk; **reputational consequences arise whether or not any law is involved**

> 🎬 **案例视频** — 强生婴儿爽身粉致癌危机（3:32）· https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV18DizBEE5S
> *"合规"从来不等于"没事"——强生最终赔付数十亿美元。*

---

## The classification tells — exam habit

| Signal in the scenario | Points to |
|---|---|
| Someone **lies, hides, takes** something | **Unethical behavior** |
| A value conflicts with another value or a business goal | **Ethical dilemma** |
| A reward offered **before** behavior | **Incentive** |
| A consequence imposed **after** behavior | **Repercussion** |
| A leader **acts** (absorbs a cost, demonstrates the standard) | **Modeling** |
| "Direct involvement" in operations | **Internal** stakeholder |
| "Vested interest" but not employed or involved | **External** stakeholder |

> **Every question on this topic is a sorting question.** Find the signal, name the concept, then answer with that word.

# Practice — 6 questions

**3 on one stimulus (Osei Printworks)** · **3 standalone**

Read → pause → **commit to an answer** → then reveal.

---

## Stimulus: Osei Printworks

**Marcus Osei** owns **Osei Printworks**, a small print shop with two employees. Posted **core values:** quality and honesty. A **rush order of 500 event programs** comes back with a **printing flaw visible only under bright light.** **Reprinting costs $300 and misses the customer's deadline.** A **new employee suggests shipping the programs without mentioning the flaw.** Marcus is also drafting the shop's first **code of conduct** and is considering a **bonus that pays employees for each rush order completed on schedule.**

> **Watch for the artifacts:** a concealment suggestion, an incentive (the bonus), and a potential modeling moment.

---

## Q1 — Classify the employee's suggestion

The employee's suggestion to ship the programs without mentioning the flaw is best described as which?

- A. Concealing information from a customer, a form of unethical behavior
- B. Misusing company property
- C. An ethical dilemma between two core values
- D. A legal factor affecting the print market

---

## Q1 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** Shipping a product with a known flaw and saying nothing means the customer pays believing something the seller knows is false — **concealing information** is a named form of unethical behavior.

**Trap: C** — Marcus *does* face a genuine dilemma (honesty vs. deadline), but **the question asks about the employee's specific suggestion** — and that suggestion is **silence for gain**, which is simply unethical behavior.

---

## Q2 — Classify the on-schedule bonus

The proposed bonus for each rush order completed on schedule is best described as which?

- A. A code of conduct provision
- B. An internal repercussion for unethical behavior
- C. An incentive structure that could influence employees toward cutting corners
- D. Modeling ethical behavior for the staff

---

## Q2 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** An incentive is a reward that encourages a particular action. A bonus tied **only to on-schedule completion** rewards speed and pays the same **whether the flaw is disclosed or hidden** — so it quietly leans on employees to ship and stay silent.

**Trap: B** — **points the concept backward.** A repercussion is a consequence imposed **after** unethical behavior; the bonus arrives **before** any behavior and rewards output.

---

## Q3 — Marcus reprints + explains + shows the note

Marcus **reprints the order at the shop's expense**, tells both employees why, and shows them the note he sent the customer. This action best represents which?

- A. An external repercussion
- B. Modeling ethical behavior
- C. Outsourcing an ethical decision
- D. A conflict of interest

---

## Q3 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Modeling means leaders **demonstrate** the ethical behavior themselves, so employees learn the standard by **watching it** — Marcus performed the standard in front of his staff **at real cost.**

**Trap: A** — a repercussion is a consequence aimed at someone who behaved unethically, and **nobody here is being punished.** Marcus **absorbed a cost to demonstrate a value** — that's modeling.

---

## Q4 — Stakeholder classification

Which best identifies a **correct** classification of stakeholders in a business?

- A. Customers are internal; owners are external
- B. Managers are external; community members are internal
- C. Owners are internal; employees are external
- D. Employees are internal; government agencies are external

---

## Q4 — Answer: **D**

**Why:** Internal = **direct involvement** in operations/decisions/outcomes (owners, managers, employees). External = **vested interest without** being employed or directly involved (customers, government agencies, community members).

**Trap: C** — gets owners right and then **exports the employees** to external.

---

## Q5 — Which decision does the table support?

A furniture maker must decide whether to switch to a cheaper overseas supplier whose delivery delays would reach customers. Estimated effects:

| Option | Internal stakeholders | External stakeholders |
|---|---|---|
| **Keep current** | −$90,000 (higher input prices) | +$70,000 (reliable deliveries) |
| **Switch** | +$90,000 (savings) | −$150,000 (late/canceled orders) |

Using the approach of **greatest total benefit or least total harm across all stakeholders**, which decision does the table support?

- A. Switching — it produces a $90,000 benefit for internal stakeholders
- B. Switching — customer losses are not costs to the company
- C. Keeping — its net cost of $20,000 is smaller than the $60,000 net cost of switching
- D. Keeping — reliable suppliers **always** increase long-run profits

---

## Q5 — Answer: **C**

Work each option across **all** stakeholders:

- **Keep:** +$70,000 − $90,000 = **net cost of $20,000**
- **Switch:** +$90,000 − $150,000 = **net cost of $60,000**

Both options hurt — so the method picks the **least total harm** → $20,000 is the smaller loss.

**Trap: A** — totals one column and stops. The method exists **precisely to count every stakeholder group.**

**Trap: D** — reaches the right decision with a **broken reason** — and **"always" is the tell.** No ethical choice carries a promised profit outcome.

---

## Q6 — How a response to a lapse hits profitability

Which best explains how a business's **response to an ethical lapse** can affect its **profitability**?

- A. Profitability is unaffected unless a law was broken
- B. The response shapes customer relationships, employee relationships, and public perception, which in turn affect revenues and costs
- C. A sincere apology **guarantees** customers will return
- D. Ethical responses matter only for large corporations with public brands

---

## Q6 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** The chain runs **response → relationships and perception → profitability.** Customers reward trust with loyalty and punish deception by leaving; employees weigh employers the same way; both movements land on the **revenue and cost** lines.

**Trap: A** — borrows **legal-factor thinking from Topic 1.3.** Reputational consequences can arise **whether or not any law is involved.**

**Trap: C** — fails on **"guarantees."** A good response improves the odds and promises nothing.

---

## Wrap-up

- **Unethical behavior** = falsifying or concealing information, misusing company property, causing harm — at any level of the business
- **Four tools:** code of conduct, training, internal repercussions, modeling — sort by **when** they act
- **Incentives** pull toward (before) and **repercussions** push back (after) — sort by **direction**
- **Ethical dilemma** = value vs. value or value vs. goal; if one side is clearly wrong it is **unethical behavior** instead
- **Internal** stakeholders are directly involved; **external** ones hold only a vested interest
- **Respond** with Lens 1 (sum every stakeholder group) or Lens 2 (consistency with the vision) — the question stem decides
- **Absolutes** ("always / guarantee") and "unless a law was broken" are the two traps — reputational harm needs no law
