## The line Theo refused

Saturday at the flea market. A father circles a **$95 commuter bike** for the third time without reaching for his wallet.

The vendor two racks over leans in and offers Theo the oldest trick in used sales:

> *"Tell him three other people are coming to see it this afternoon."*

Nobody else is coming — and Theo knows it.

---

## What Theo does instead

He flips up the **orange tag** zip-tied to the handlebars and reads the man all **21 points** of the inspection (brakes to bottom bracket), then taps the **30-day tune-up promise** printed at the bottom.

**The father buys the bike.**

> This topic explains **why** he said yes — and why the honest version **outsold** the lie.

---

## LO 2.2.A — Why consumers buy

### EK 2.2.A.1

Consumers buy goods and services to **meet basic needs and wants.**

The father *needs* his kid transported to school and *wants* that on a bike the kid will actually ride.

Most needs have **rival goods** circling them — multiple products that can meet the same need.

---

## Four tiebreakers

When rivals exist, **four things break the tie:**

| Tiebreaker | How it pulls the father |
|---|---|
| **Price** | a comparable new bike costs about **twice** Theo's price |
| **Differentiated features** | the signed inspection tag + the 30-day tune-up promise — no factory-fresh rival carries these |
| **Availability** | his bike rolls home **today**; an online order ships next week |
| **Advertising** | the listing that brought him to the stall — putting the product in front of the right person |

---

## Rational vs. habitual decisions

### EK 2.2.A.2

How carefully a consumer chooses depends on **what a mistake would cost.**

- **Consequential purchases** (a home, a car, a first bike for a kid riding to school in traffic): consumers use a **rational decision-making process** — list the alternatives, compare them systematically, pick the best fit
- **Small routine purchases** (the coffee on the way to work, the same gum at the same register): run on **habit** with no comparison at all

---

## Worked example — true cost comparison

A parent spends three evenings on listings and finds an **untagged $75 rival** to Theo's **$95** commuter.

The rival comes with **no inspection**, and a shop charges about **$25** to check the brakes.

$$\$75 + \$25 = \$100$$

The tagged bike **wins by $5** — even though the sticker price pointed the other way.

> Three evenings of searching surfaced that reversal — a **sane trade for a bike and an absurd one for gum.**

---

## Match depth to stakes

| Purchase type | Process | Cost of the process |
|---|---|---|
| **Consequential** (bike, car, home) | Rational — compare alternatives systematically | Mostly **time** spent gathering information |
| **Routine** (coffee, gum) | Habitual — no comparison | Almost **zero** |

> **Match the depth of the process to the size of the decision.**

---

## Why rational matters — the search cost

The rational process "charges" for the search **mostly in time spent gathering information.**

Theo's buyer spent **three evenings** comparing listings to find the $75 rival. That time investment makes sense for a **$95 bike** — but would be absurd for a **$1.50 pack of gum.**

> **The search cost must be proportionate to the stakes.** A rational process for every purchase would paralyze the buyer.

---

## Four families of influence

### EK 2.2.A.3–2.2.A.6

Four families of factors shape buying decisions:

| Family | Where it lives |
|---|---|
| **Personal** | **who** the buyer is |
| **Psychological** | **what** the buyer thinks |
| **Social & cultural** | **who surrounds** the buyer |
| **Situational** | **where and when** the buying happens |

---

## Personal factors

### EK 2.2.A.3

Facts of the buyer's own life:

**Age, sex, education, occupation, income level, budget, lifestyle.**

They set what a consumer needs, wants, and can afford.

> Renee's profile card (from 2.1) is a stack of personal factors: 41 years old, a $90 budget, a middle schooler who needs to reach campus by bell time. Her **income sets the ceiling**; her **kid's age sets the frame size.**

---

## Personal factors — examples at Theo's stall

| Factor | How it applies |
|---|---|
| **Age** | Renee is 41 — old enough to have a middle schooler |
| **Income / budget** | $90 ceiling — sets what she can spend |
| **Occupation** | works near the school — needs the bike by Saturday |
| **Lifestyle** | drives everywhere but wants her kid to ride |

---

## Psychological factors

### EK 2.2.A.4

Inside the buyer's head:

**Values and beliefs, perceptions, learning, individual motivations.**

| Factor | Meaning | Theo's stall |
|---|---|---|
| **Values** | what matters most | Renee values **safety over speed** |
| **Perception** | how a product reads **before the facts arrive** | used bikes read as **risky** — the orange tag corrects that |
| **Learning** | associations from **prior experience** | a parent whose last secondhand bike snapped a chain arrives carrying an association Theo must outweigh |
| **Motivation** | the **push** behind the purchase | hers is a **Saturday deadline** |

---

## Social and cultural factors

### EK 2.2.A.5

From other people:

**Peers, family, social status, cultural norms, media.**

They define what feels acceptable, desirable, or appropriate to buy.

> When three families at school pickup mention the same stall, buying there becomes the **normal** move. At the school bike rack, a clean rebuilt commuter carries a little **status** of its own. The pickup photos Theo posts (with permission) are **media** doing the same work at scale.

---

## Situational influences

### EK 2.2.A.6

Temporary conditions around the moment of purchase:

**Store design (noise, lighting, organization), timing, product availability.**

> A flea market is loud and cluttered, so Theo's rack **fights the setting**: three tiers in a straight line, tags facing out, a clear lane for test rides. A sunny back-to-school Saturday encourages buying; a rainy one discourages it — **the bikes are identical both days.**

---

## Situational — the exam warning

> **Situational influences are temporary conditions around the purchase — not features of the product.**

The candle shop plays quiet music and keeps the lighting warm → **situational** (surroundings change for 20 minutes).

The candle is made of soy wax → **not situational** (that's a product feature).

> **If it changes when the shopper walks out the door, it's situational. If it travels home with the product, it isn't.**

---

## Exam signal — the four-family question

The exam will describe a scenario and ask: **which family of influence?**

**Method:**
1. **Read the scenario** — find the factor acting on the buyer
2. **Ask the sorting question** — census trait? contents of mind? other people? temporary surroundings?
3. **Eliminate** — if it's not a census trait, it's not personal; if other people aren't involved, it's not social

> **"Store design" and "lighting" always point to situational. "Values" and "beliefs" always point to psychological. Don't overthink it.**

## Four-family sorting key — exam reference

| Family | Where it lives | Sorting question |
|---|---|---|
| **Personal** | **who** the buyer is (age, income, budget, occupation, lifestyle) | Could a **census form** record it? |
| **Psychological** | **what** the buyer thinks (values, beliefs, perceptions, learning, motivation) | Does it describe **what a person cares about**? |

| **Situational** | **where and when** the buying happens (store design, timing, availability) | Is it **temporary** — does it change when the shopper leaves? |

> **The four-family sort lives on the real exam. Memorize the "where it lives" column.**

## LO 2.2.B — Purchasing patterns

### EK 2.2.B.1

A **purchasing pattern** = a consumer's typical routine for buying, described by:

| Dimension | What it measures |
|---|---|
| **Timing** | when the purchase happens |
| **Frequency** | how often |
| **Quantity** | how much per purchase |

Where a person lives/works, income level, and the patterns of family/friends all help set it.

---

## Purchasing patterns — Theo's ledger

| Dimension | What Theo's ledger shows |
|---|---|
| **Timing** | clustered in **late August** (back to school) |
| **Frequency** | every **few years per kid** (one bike per growth spurt) |
| **Quantity** | **one bike at a time** |

> Local families buy one bike at a time, every few years per kid, clustered in late August, from a stall a short drive from home.

---

## Purchasing patterns — the exam method

When the exam gives you a **data table** and asks about purchasing patterns:

1. **Check timing** — when do purchases happen? (same time each day? seasonal?)
2. **Check frequency** — how often? (daily? weekly? yearly?)
3. **Check quantity** — how much per purchase? (one item? bulk?)
4. **If all three are steady** → habitual pattern
5. **If any vary** → not habitual; possibly rational

> **A table that shows consistent timing + frequency + quantity = habitual. Don't add what the table doesn't show.**

---

## Laws bend patterns

### EK 2.2.B.2

Laws regulate **what** consumers can buy and **when/where** they can buy it:

- Many places require **helmets** for young riders → a kids' bike sale tends to pull a helmet sale along behind it
- **Consumer protection laws** help people avoid fraudulent or unsafe products → reselling a recalled children's product can break the law, so checking recall lists is the **first point** on Theo's 21-point inspection
- Legal factors also give rise to **substitutes** — when a product gets harder to obtain, consumers find another way to meet the need

---

## Laws create substitutes

When a law makes a product harder to obtain, **the need persists** and substitutes appear:

| Law | Substitutes that appeared |
|---|---|
| Cities restrict rental scooters | some riders **substitute bicycles** |
| Single-use plastic straw ban | paper and metal straws fill the gap |
| Helmet requirement for young riders | helmet sales rise alongside bike sales |

> **The law changes availability. The need stays the same. Businesses arrive to serve it with something else.**

---

## Technology rewrites patterns

### EK 2.2.B.3

Innovation keeps making new products accessible:

- The **internet** reaching homes in the **1990s** moved shopping, information, and entertainment online
- **Smartphones** spreading in the **2010s** put all three in every pocket
- **Trued Cycles only exists inside that shift** — Theo learned repairs from free videos, sells through a marketplace app, and watches parents check his price against new-bike prices on their phones

---

## Technology — both sides of the sale

> **Technology changes patterns on both sides of the sale.**

| Side | How technology changed it |
|---|---|
| **Seller reach** | Theo lists on a marketplace app → reaches buyers he'd never meet at the flea market |
| **Buyer information** | parents compare Theo's price to new-bike prices on their phones **while standing at his stall** |

> **Exam point:** a question about technology affecting "how consumers shop" and "how businesses reach customers" is testing **both sides** — don't name only one.

---

## Technology — Theo's stall

| Technology | Effect on Theo's business |
|---|---|
| **Marketplace app** | reaches buyers he'd never meet at the flea market |
| **Free repair videos** | taught him the skills — no trade school needed |
| **Smartphone price checks** | parents verify his price vs. new bikes **in real time** |
| **Messaging apps** | 30-day tune-up reminders sent with one tap |

> Every row is technology changing **seller reach**, **buyer information**, or **both.**

## Real-world case — Keep 运动 App

**Keep** — Chinese fitness app, 300M+ users, IPO in Hong Kong 2023.

Before Keep: fitness = gym membership ($50-100/month) + commute + fixed schedule
After Keep: fitness = free workouts at home, any time, any place

> **Technology changed both sides of the sale.**

---

## Keep — technology + Cialdini

**Technology rewrites patterns** (EK 2.2.B.3):
- Smartphone penetration → fitness accessible to millions who never went to gyms
- Online fitness users in China: ~100M (2015) → ~400M (2023)

**Cialdini principles in the app:**

| Principle | How Keep uses it |
|---|---|
| **Consistency** | "自律给我自由" (Self-discipline gives me freedom) — appeals to self-image; streak counters reinforce identity |
| **Unity** | Training camps, running clubs, shared goals → "I'm part of the Keep community" |
| **Consensus** | "100万人已跟练" (1M people completed this workout) — social proof |
| **Reciprocity** | Free content → users feel value → more likely to subscribe |

---

## Keep — habitual vs rational

| Decision | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Daily workout on the app | **Habitual** | Same time, same routine, no comparison needed |
| Premium subscription ($19/month) | **Rational** | Compare with gym cost, other apps, features |
| Buying a yoga mat | **Rational** | Compare price, quality, reviews |
| Opening the app each morning | **Habitual** | Reminder + streak counter → automatic |

> The app is designed to make fitness **habitual** — reminders, streaks, daily plans. But the purchase of premium is **rational** — a consequential decision.

## LO 2.2.C — Seven principles of influence

### EK 2.2.C.1–8

Psychologist **Robert Cialdini** cataloged the principles of influence — psychological factors that lead people to comply with a request. Marketers build sales tactics on them.

> **Every principle has an honest application and a manipulative one.**

---

## The seven principles — table

| Principle | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Scarcity** | the rarer an item seems, the more people want it |
| **Authority** | people follow authority figures and credentials |
| **Consensus** (social proof) | people follow the norms of their group |
| **Liking** | people are more easily influenced by someone they like |
| **Reciprocity** | people feel obliged to give back after receiving value |
| **Consistency** | people repeat behavior that matches their self-image |
| **Unity** | when people feel part of a group, the group's influence grows |

---

## How to sort the seven principles

The exam gives you a scenario and asks **which principle.** Sort by the **mechanism**, not the keyword:

| Principle | Mechanism | Sorting question |
|---|---|---|
| **Scarcity** | perceived rarity | Is the item presented as **limited or one-of-a-kind**? |
| **Authority** | expert credentials | Does an **expert or credential** endorse it? |
| **Consensus** | many buyers' behavior | Do **many other people** already buy or approve? |
| **Liking** | rapport / relatability | Does the seller feel **friendly or relatable**? |
| **Reciprocity** | obligation from receiving | Did the buyer **receive something free** first? |
| **Consistency** | self-image match | Does the tactic appeal to **who the buyer believes they are**? |
| **Unity** | group membership | Does the buyer feel like **part of a group**? |

> **The word in the answer choice is unreliable. The engine decides.**

---

## Scarcity — Theo's honest version

**Scarcity** = the rarer an item seems, the more people want it.

| Honest version | Manipulative version |
|---|---|
| every used bike really **is** one of one — no invention needed | the vendor's *"three other buyers"* line is scarcity **faked** |

> True scarcity costs nothing when checked. Faked scarcity dies the moment a buyer asks *"who?"*

---

## Authority — Theo's honest version

**Authority** = people follow authority figures, expert spokespeople, and credentials.

| Honest version | Manipulative version |
|---|---|
| the **signed orange tag** — a documented 21-point inspection is a credential you can hold | a paid "expert" who isn't really an expert |

> The tag is a credential the buyer can **physically hold and read.**

---

## Consensus — Theo's honest version

**Consensus** (social proof) = people follow the norms of their group; promote positive feedback until a product feels widely liked.

| Honest version | Manipulative version |
|---|---|
| the **permission-based pickup photos** — a wall of local kids the next family can join | fake reviews |

> Two dozen photos of real local kids tell a browsing parent: **people like you already chose here.**

---

## Liking — Theo's honest version

**Liking** = people are more easily influenced by someone they like or relate to.

| Honest version | Manipulative version |
|---|---|
| Theo is a **local teenager** who remembers each kid and explains brake pads in plain language | manufactured false rapport |

> He doesn't pretend to be something he isn't — he **is** the neighbor kid who fixes bikes.

---

## Reciprocity — Theo's honest version

**Reciprocity** = people feel obliged to give back after receiving value (free trials, samples, gifts).

| Honest version | Manipulative version |
|---|---|
| he **airs up tires and lubes the chain** of whatever bike a browser rode in on — no charge | a "gift" that creates hidden obligation |

> The visitor leaves with a better bike than they arrived with — **whether or not they buy.**

---

## Consistency — Theo's honest version

**Consistency** = people repeat behavior that matches their **self-image**; appeal to identity.

| Honest version | Manipulative version |
|---|---|
| a listing that opens *"for parents who put safety first"* invites Renee to act like the person she believes she is | getting a small commitment, then exploiting it |

> The listing doesn't create a new identity — it **names the one Renee already has.**

---

## Unity — Theo's honest version

**Unity** = when people feel part of a group, the group's influence grows; build community, invite co-creation.

| Honest version | Manipulative version |
|---|---|
| each kid **picks the handlebar tape color**; a fall **group ride for "Trued Families"** turns customers into members | manufactured in-group vs. out-group pressure |

> Customization + community event = customers become **members.**

---

## Real-world case — 茶颜悦色 Tea Yan Yue Se

**Tea Yan Yue Se** — Chinese tea brand, 500+ stores, founded 2013 in Changsha.

For **7 years** available ONLY in Changsha — tourists literally planned trips around getting a cup.

> Every Cialdini principle, applied honestly — and each one **passes the face test.**

---

## 茶颜悦色 — Cialdini in action

| Principle | How 茶颜悦色 uses it | Face test |
|---|---|---|
| **Scarcity** | Only in Changsha; seasonal limited flavors rotate and disappear | ✅ "This flavor ends this season" — true |
| **Authority** | Founder writes product copy; each cup has classical poetry; "authentic Chinese tea culture" | ✅ Real cultural references, not fake credentials |
| **Consensus** | 1M+ Xiaohongshu posts; "Changsha travel must-do" | ✅ Real posts from real customers |
| **Liking** | Chinese-style cup design, playful product names, friendly staff | ✅ Genuine brand personality |
| **Reciprocity** | Buy 6 get 1 free stamp card; free festival gifts | ✅ Real value given |
| **Consistency** | "I'm a 茶颜 fan" identity drives repeat choice | ✅ Identity is self-chosen |
| **Unity** | Changsha locals feel ownership; online fan communities with millions | ✅ Real community, not manufactured |

> **Seven principles, all honest. That's why the brand survived 10+ years.**

---

## 茶颜悦色 — purchasing patterns

| Customer type | Timing | Frequency | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tourists** | During Changsha trip | 3-4 cups in 2-3 days | 1 cup per visit |
| **Locals** | After work, weekends | 1-2 times per week | 1-2 cups per visit |
| **Holiday spike** | Spring Festival, National Day | Daily during holiday | Higher (gifts for friends) |

> Same product, **different purchasing patterns** based on personal factors (where you live) and situational factors (when you visit).

## The Face Test

### EK 2.2.C.8

The exam creators will ask you to **judge** a tactic and **build** one.

> **A tactic is worth keeping when the principle still works after full disclosure.**

Theo's test: **say the tactic to the customer's face and see if it survives.**

---

## Face Test — why it works

The face test separates **honest persuasion** from **deception:**

| | Honest tactic | Manipulative tactic |
|---|---|---|
| **Disclosure** | survives when the buyer knows how it works | **collapses** when the buyer knows how it works |
| **Trust** | builds over time — returning shoppers come back | **erodes** — one caught lie undoes months of goodwill |
| **Principle** | works because the underlying psychology is **real** | works only because the buyer is **misinformed** |

> **If the principle needs secrecy to function, it's manipulation. If it works in the open, it's persuasion.**

---

## Face Test — three examples

| Tactic | Face test |
|---|---|
| "This tag lists everything I checked" | **survives** — the buyer can read every point |
| "Come back within 30 days and the tune-up is free" | **survives** — and reverses the risk that keeps used-bike buyers from saying yes |
| "Three other people are coming this afternoon" | **dies** the moment a buyer asks *"who?"* |

> One caught lie undoes the trust the signed checklist was built to earn — and **trust is what converts these buyers.**

---

3. **Run the face test** — can he say it to the buyer's face? Does the principle still work after full disclosure?

*Write your answer before moving on.*

## Your turn — sample answer

**Principle:** Reciprocity

**Tactic:** Theo keeps a pump and chain lube at the stall. Any visitor who rode a bike there gets the tires aired up and the chain lubed — **free, no purchase required.**

> **The obligation is real, the value is real, and the disclosure changes nothing.** That's a tactic that passes the face test.

---

## Exam points to remember

- **Needs/wants + rival goods** broken by four tiebreakers: price, features, availability, advertising
- **Rational vs. habitual:** match depth to stakes; add the **true cost of a cheaper rival** ($75 + $25 = $100)
- **Four families:** personal, psychological, social/cultural, situational
- **Situational = temporary conditions around the purchase, NOT product features**
- **Purchasing pattern** = timing, frequency, quantity
- **Laws** regulate what/when/where → create **substitutes**; the need persists
- **Technology reshapes both sides** — seller reach and buyer information
- **Seven Cialdini principles:** each has an **honest** and a **manipulative** application
- **Face test:** a tactic is worth keeping if the principle **still works after full disclosure**

## Stimulus: Cobalt Finch Ceramics

**Ivy Santos**, a high school senior, runs **Cobalt Finch Ceramics**, a stall selling handmade mugs at a monthly craft fair. **Three other stalls sell mugs**, so shoppers can meet the same want **four ways** and weigh price, glaze, and feel before choosing.

Behind her table Ivy pins a **corkboard of short notes from past buyers** describing their mugs in daily use (**two dozen** so far). At the front edge sits a **dish of small glazed clay magnets, free** to anyone who stops.

---

## Stimulus — continued

The vendor at the next stall urges her to tell shoppers **each design is the last one available** — including designs with **duplicates boxed under the table.**

Ivy declines the line, and letters a small card reading **"one of a kind"** for the three mugs whose copper glaze she cannot repeat.

*Watch for: consensus (the corkboard), reciprocity (the free magnets), and scarcity (the honest card vs. the faked line).*

---

## Q1 — The corkboard of buyer notes

The corkboard of buyer notes is best identified as a sales tactic built on which principle of influence?

- A. **Authority**, because the notes come from experienced mug owners
- B. **Liking**, because the notes make the stall feel friendly
- C. **Consensus**, because displayed customer feedback signals that many buyers like the product
- D. **Unity**, because reading the notes makes shoppers part of a group

<!--
Pause. Ask: what is the mechanism — expertise, rapport, many-buyers, or membership?
-->

---

## Q1 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** Consensus = individuals **follow the norms of their social group** → promoting positive customer feedback creates the sense that **most buyers like the product.** Two dozen notes from satisfied strangers tell a browsing shopper that **people like her already chose these mugs.**

**Traps:**
- **A** — the test is **expertise.** Authority persuades through **experts and credentials**; Ivy's note writers **claim nothing beyond owning a mug.**
- **D** — overreads **membership.** Unity works when customers feel **part of a group**; reading strangers' notes **confers no membership.**

> **Consensus = what many buyers did. Authority = what a credentialed expert says. Unity = makes the buyer a member.**

---

## Q2 — The dish of free magnets

Which best explains how the dish of free magnets can increase mug sales?

- A. Receiving an item of value makes shoppers feel **obligated to give something back**, often a purchase
- B. The magnets make shoppers **like Ivy**, and people buy from sellers they like
- C. The magnets appeal to shoppers' **self-image** as supporters of young artists
- D. The magnets make the mugs seem **rarer** than the rival stalls' mugs

---

## Q2 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** **Reciprocity** = individuals feel obligated to reciprocate when given something of value (why marketers offer free samples, trials, gifts). A shopper who pockets a free magnet carries a small felt debt to the stall — **buying a mug settles it.**

**Traps:**
- **B** names a real **side effect** but the **wrong engine**: liking persuades through **rapport/relatability**; a giveaway's tested mechanism is the **obligation** it plants.
- **C** describes **consistency** (people repeat behavior matching their self-image); a magnet in a dish makes **no identity claim.**

> **A free item persuades through the obligation it creates = reciprocity.**

---

## Q3 — Refuse the line, keep the cards

Which best explains Ivy's decision to refuse the vendor's line while keeping the one-of-a-kind cards?

- A. Scarcity persuades **only** when the seller invents the shortage, so the cards cannot motivate buyers
- B. Refusing the line means Ivy has **given up** the scarcity principle entirely
- C. Scarcity claims are **prohibited** at in-person markets
- D. A **true claim of rarity** applies the scarcity principle honestly, while the false line risks deception and lost trust

---

## Q3 — Answer: **D**

**Why:** Scarcity runs on **perceived rarity**; **real rarity produces that perception honestly** — so the card on an unrepeatable glaze keeps the persuasive pull **and** keeps the claim true. The false version invites the deception **consumer protection laws exist to catch**, and at a monthly fair with returning shoppers, **one duplicate spotted in a box undoes months of corkboard trust.**

**Traps:**
- **A** — the tell is the **absolute.** Nothing in the principle requires **invention**; a genuine shortage is the **cleanest** source of perceived rarity.
- **B** — fails on the stimulus: Ivy **still labels** the three unrepeatable mugs, so the principle stays in use **where it is true.**

> **A true rarity claim applies the same principle as a false one — and costs nothing when checked.**

## Q4 — Purchasing-pattern table

**Bluestem Coffee** (drive-through) pulls one member's loyalty-app records:

| | Week 1 | Weeks 2–4 |
|---|---|---|
| Visits | 5 | 5 each |
| Window | 7:10–7:25 am | **same** |
| Order | 1 medium coffee | **same** |
| Price | $4 each | **same** |

Which conclusion is supported by the table?

---

## Q4 — Answer choices

- A. The member **systematically evaluates** rival coffee options before each visit
- B. The purchases form a **habitual purchasing pattern** — consistent in timing, frequency, and quantity — totaling **$80** across the four weeks
- C. The member's **lifetime value** to Bluestem is **$80**
- D. The app **guarantees** the member will keep buying five coffees a week

---

## Q4 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** A purchasing pattern is a consumer's typical routine, read through **timing, frequency, quantity** — all three steady here:

$$5 \text{ visits} \times 4 \text{ weeks} = 20 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 20 \times \$4 = \$80$$

Small routine buys run on **habit**; a rational process of comparing alternatives fits **consequential** purchases.

**Traps:**
- **A** claims **deliberation** the table contradicts — a shopper comparing rivals would show **varied stops and varied orders.**
- **C** borrows last topic's vocabulary: LTV estimates total spending across the **whole** customer relationship; this table shows **one month.**

> **A conclusion the table cannot compute is never supported.**

## Q5 — The straw ordinance

A city ordinance bars restaurants from handing out single-use plastic straws. Within a year, local stores stock **paper and metal straws**, and reusable-straw sales climb. Which best explains the change in purchasing patterns?

- A. **Legal restrictions can give rise to substitute products** as consumers find new ways to meet an existing need
- B. The ordinance **eliminated the need** the product served, so demand disappeared
- C. The change is a **situational influence**, because store layouts shifted
- D. **Consumer protection laws require** every banned product to be replaced by a licensed alternative

---

## Q5 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** When a law makes a product **harder to obtain, the want it served survives** → consumers find **substitutes** and businesses arrive to sell them. (The straw leaves, the drink stays; paper and metal fill the gap.)

**Traps:**
- **B** breaks the chain at the **wrong link**: the ordinance changed **availability**, and the underlying want persisted **untouched.**
- **C** borrows a **neighboring factor family**: situational influences are **temporary** (noise, lighting, timing); an ordinance is a **legal factor** that **holds until repealed.**

> **When the law removes a product, the need remains, and substitutes appear to serve it.**

---

## Q6 — The candle shop

A candle shop plays **quiet music**, keeps the **lighting warm**, and sets **open testers** by the entrance so shoppers smell the candles immediately. Shoppers who came in to browse frequently leave with a purchase. Which best identifies the factor influencing these purchases?

- A. A **personal** factor (occupation and budget)
- B. A **social and cultural** factor (peers set the norm)
- C. A **situational influence** (store design creates temporary conditions that encourage buying)
- D. A **psychological** factor (the store changes each shopper's values and beliefs)

---

## Q6 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** Situational influences = **temporary conditions** that encourage/discourage buying, created by **store design** (noise, lighting, organization), timing, and product availability. The music, warmth, and testers work on the shopper **only while she stands inside them.**

**Trap: D** — the test is **what changed:** the store adjusted the shopper's **surroundings for twenty minutes**, and her **values and beliefs walked out the same as they walked in.**

> **If it changes when the shopper walks out the door, it's situational. If it travels home with the product, it isn't.**

## Wrap-up

- **Why consumers buy:** needs/wants + four tiebreakers (price, features, availability, advertising)
- **Rational vs. habitual:** match depth to stakes
- **Four families:** personal, psychological, social/cultural, situational (temporary, not product features)
- **Purchasing pattern** = timing + frequency + quantity; **laws create substitutes**; **technology reshapes both sides**
- **Seven Cialdini principles** — each has an honest and a manipulative version
- **Face test:** say the tactic to the customer's face — does it survive?
