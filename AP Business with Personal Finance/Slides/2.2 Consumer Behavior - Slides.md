## The line Theo refused

**Theo, 16** — Saturday at the flea market. A father circles a **$95 commuter bike** for the third time. The vendor two racks over offers the oldest trick in used sales:

> *"Tell him three other people are coming to see it this afternoon."*

Nobody else is coming. Theo flips up the **orange tag** instead — reads all **21 points** of the inspection, then taps the **30-day tune-up promise** printed at the bottom.

> **The father buys the bike.** Today: **why** he said yes — and why the honest version outsold the lie.

---

## Why consumers buy (2.2.A.1)

Consumers buy goods and services to **meet basic needs and wants** — and most needs have **rival goods** circling them: multiple products that can meet the same need.

| | The father at the stall |
|---|---|
| **Need** | get his kid to school |
| **Want** | a bike the kid will actually ride |
| **Rival goods** | department-store new bikes, online listings, the $75 untagged rival |

> **When rivals exist, something has to break the tie.**

---

## Four tiebreakers

| Tiebreaker | How it pulls the father |
|---|---|
| **Price** | a comparable new bike costs about **twice** Theo's price |
| **Differentiated features** | the signed inspection tag + the tune-up promise — no factory-fresh rival carries these |
| **Availability** | his bike rolls home **today**; an online order ships next week |
| **Advertising** | the listing that brought him to the stall — putting the product in front of the right person |

> **The exam asks which tiebreaker a scenario is using — name the mechanism, not the keyword.**

## Rational vs. habitual (2.2.A.2)

How carefully a consumer chooses depends on **what a mistake would cost:**

| Purchase type | Process | Cost of the process |
|---|---|---|
| **Consequential** (a home, a car, a first bike for a kid riding in traffic) | **Rational** — list the alternatives, compare systematically, pick the best fit | mostly **time** spent gathering information |
| **Routine** (the coffee on the way to work, the same gum at the same register) | **Habitual** — run on habit, no comparison at all | almost **zero** |

> **Match the depth of the process to the size of the decision.**

---

## Worked example — the true cost of the cheaper rival

A parent spends three evenings on listings and finds an **untagged $75 rival** to Theo's **$95** commuter. It comes with **no inspection** — and a shop charges about **$25** to check the brakes.

$75 + $25 = $100 — the tagged bike still wins: $100 − $95 = $5

> **The search cost must be proportionate to the stakes** — a sane trade for a bike, an absurd one for gum.

## Four families of influence (2.2.A.3–2.2.A.6)

The exam describes a scenario and asks: **which family?** Sort by where the factor lives:

| Family                | Where it lives                        | Sorting question                                       |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Personal**          | **who** the buyer is                  | Could a **census form** record it?                     |
| **Psychological**     | **what** the buyer thinks             | Does it describe **what the person cares about**?      |
| **Social & cultural** | **who surrounds** the buyer           | Does the influence come from **other people**?         |
| **Situational**       | **where and when** the buying happens | Is it **temporary** — gone when the shopper walks out? |

> **"Store design" → situational. "Values" → psychological. Don't overthink it.**

## Situational influences (2.2.A.6)

**Temporary conditions around the moment of purchase:** store design (noise, lighting, organization), timing, product availability.

- Theo's rack **fights the flea-market setting**: three tiers in a straight line, tags facing out, a clear lane for test rides
- A sunny back-to-school Saturday encourages buying; a rainy one discourages it — **the bikes are identical both days**

> **The candle shop's warm lighting → situational. Soy wax → a product feature.** If it changes when the shopper walks out the door, it's situational; if it travels home with the product, it isn't.

# 2.2.B — Purchasing Patterns

**Read the routine, then ask what bent it.**

---

## Purchasing patterns (2.2.B.1)

A **purchasing pattern** = a consumer's typical routine for buying, read through three dimensions:

| Dimension | What it measures | Theo's ledger |
|---|---|---|
| **Timing** | when the purchase happens | clustered in **late August** (back to school) |
| **Frequency** | how often | every **few years per kid** (one bike per growth spurt) |
| **Quantity** | how much per purchase | **one bike at a time** |

> **All three steady = a habitual pattern.** Where a person lives, income level, and the habits of family and friends all help set it.

---

## Laws bend patterns (2.2.B.2)

| Law | What it does to the pattern |
|---|---|
| **Helmet requirement** for young riders | a kids' bike sale pulls a helmet sale along behind it |
| **Consumer protection** laws | reselling a recalled product can break the law — the recall check is **point 1** on Theo's 21-point inspection |
| **Restrictions & bans** | harder to obtain → consumers substitute: rental-scooter limits push riders to **bicycles**; the straw ban fills the gap with **paper and metal** |

> **The law changes availability. The need stays the same. Businesses arrive to serve it with something else.**

---

## Technology rewrites patterns (2.2.B.3)

| Technology | Effect on Theo's business |
|---|---|
| **Marketplace app** | reaches buyers he'd never meet at the flea market |
| **Free repair videos** | taught him the trade — no school needed |
| **Smartphone price checks** | parents compare his price to new bikes **while standing at his stall** |
| **Messaging apps** | 30-day tune-up reminders, sent with one tap |

> **Technology changes both sides of the sale** — seller reach *and* buyer information. The internet (1990s) moved shopping online; smartphones (2010s) put it in every pocket. **Trued Cycles only exists inside that shift.**

---

## Real-world case — Keep

**Keep** — Chinese fitness app, 300M+ users, IPO in Hong Kong 2023. Online fitness in China: ~100M users (2015) → ~400M (2023). Before: gym membership + commute + fixed schedule. After: free workouts at home, any time.

| Decision | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Daily workout on the app | **Habitual** | same time each day, streak counters, no comparison |
| Premium subscription | **Rational** | compared with a $50–100/month gym and rival apps |

> **The app is engineered to make fitness habitual — the purchase is still rational.**

# 2.2.C — Seven Principles of Influence

**Every principle has an honest application and a manipulative one.**

---

## The seven principles (2.2.C.1)

**Robert Cialdini** cataloged the principles of influence — psychological factors that lead people to comply with a request. Marketers build sales tactics on them. Sort by the **mechanism**, not the keyword:

| Principle | Mechanism | Sorting question |
|---|---|---|
| **Scarcity** | perceived rarity | Is it presented as **limited or one-of-a-kind**? |
| **Authority** | expert credentials | Does an **expert or credential** endorse it? |
| **Consensus** | many buyers' behavior | Do **many others** already buy or approve? |
| **Liking** | rapport / relatability | Does the seller feel **friendly, relatable**? |
| **Reciprocity** | obligation from receiving | Did the buyer **receive something free** first? |
| **Consistency** | self-image match | Does it appeal to **who the buyer believes they are**? |
| **Unity** | group membership | Does the buyer feel **part of a group**? |

> **The word in the answer choice is unreliable. The engine decides.**

---

## The seven principles at Theo's stall

| Principle | Theo's honest version | The manipulative version |
|---|---|---|
| **Scarcity** | every used bike really **is** one of one | the *"three other buyers"* line — **faked** |
| **Authority** | the **signed orange tag** — a credential you can hold | a paid "expert" who isn't one |
| **Consensus** | real local kids' pickup photos, with permission | fake reviews |
| **Liking** | a local teen explaining brake pads plainly | manufactured false rapport |
| **Reciprocity** | free air + chain lube for any bike ridden in | a "gift" that creates hidden obligation |
| **Consistency** | a listing opening *"for parents who put safety first"* | a small commitment, then exploited |
| **Unity** | tape color picks; a **Trued Families** ride | manufactured in-group pressure |

> **True scarcity costs nothing when checked — faked scarcity dies the moment a buyer asks *"who?"***

---

## Real-world case — 茶颜悦色

Chinese tea brand, 500+ stores. For **7 years** available ONLY in Changsha — tourists planned trips around getting a cup.

| Principle | How it shows up | Face test |
|---|---|---|
| **Scarcity** | 7 years, one city; seasonal flavors rotate and disappear | ✅ "this flavor ends this season" — true |
| **Consensus** | 1M+ real Xiaohongshu posts; "Changsha travel must-do" | ✅ real posts from real customers |
| **Reciprocity** | buy-6-get-1 stamp card; free festival gifts | ✅ real value given |
| **Consistency** | "I'm a 茶颜 fan" drives the repeat choice | ✅ identity is self-chosen |

> **Seven principles, all honest — and every one of them survives full disclosure. That's the face test.**

---

## The face test (2.2.C.8)

The exam will ask you to **judge** a tactic and **build** one. Theo's test: **say the tactic to the customer's face and see if it survives.**

| Tactic | Face test |
|---|---|
| "This tag lists everything I checked" | **survives** — the buyer can read every point |
| "Come back within 30 days and the tune-up is free" | **survives** — it reverses the risk that keeps used-bike buyers from saying yes |
| "Three other people are coming this afternoon" | **dies** the moment a buyer asks *"who?"* |

> **If the principle needs secrecy to function, it's manipulation. If it works in the open, it's persuasion.**

---

## Stimulus: Cobalt Finch Ceramics

**Ivy Santos**, a high school senior, sells handmade mugs at a monthly craft fair — **three other stalls sell mugs**, so shoppers can meet the same want **four ways** and weigh price, glaze, and feel before choosing.

Behind her table: a **corkboard of short notes from past buyers** (two dozen so far). At the front edge: a **dish of small glazed clay magnets, free** to anyone who stops.

The next stall's vendor urges her to tell shoppers **each design is the last one available** — including designs with **duplicates boxed under the table.** Ivy declines the line — and letters **"one of a kind"** cards for the three mugs whose copper glaze she cannot repeat.

---

## Q1 — The corkboard of buyer notes

The corkboard of buyer notes is best identified as a sales tactic built on which principle of influence?

> A. **Authority**, because the notes come from experienced mug owners
> B. **Liking**, because the notes make the stall feel friendly
> C. **Consensus**, because displayed customer feedback signals that many buyers like the product
> D. **Unity**, because reading the notes makes shoppers part of a group

---

## Q1 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** consensus = individuals **follow the norms of their social group** — two dozen notes from satisfied strangers tell a browsing shopper that **people like her already chose these mugs.**

**Trap A** — the test is **expertise**; the note writers claim nothing beyond owning a mug. **Trap D** — reading strangers' notes **confers no membership.**

---

## Q2 — The dish of free magnets

Which best explains how the dish of free magnets can increase mug sales?

> A. Receiving an item of value makes shoppers feel **obligated to give something back**, often a purchase
> B. The magnets make shoppers **like Ivy**, and people buy from sellers they like
> C. The magnets appeal to shoppers' **self-image** as supporters of young artists
> D. The magnets make the mugs seem **rarer** than the rival stalls' mugs

---

## Q2 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** a free item persuades through the **obligation** it plants — a shopper who pockets a magnet carries a small felt debt to the stall, and **buying a mug settles it.**

**Trap B** — a real side effect, wrong engine: liking persuades through **rapport**, not giveaways. **Trap C** — a magnet in a dish makes **no identity claim** (that's consistency).

---

## Q3 — Refuse the line, keep the cards

Which best explains Ivy's decision to refuse the vendor's line while keeping the one-of-a-kind cards?

> A. Scarcity persuades **only** when the seller invents the shortage, so the cards cannot motivate buyers
> B. Refusing the line means Ivy has **given up** the scarcity principle entirely
> C. Scarcity claims are **prohibited** at in-person markets
> D. A **true claim of rarity** applies the scarcity principle honestly, while the false line risks deception and lost trust

---

## Q3 — Answer: **D**

**Why:** real rarity produces the perception **honestly** — the card keeps the persuasive pull **and** keeps the claim true. The faked line invites the deception **consumer protection laws exist to catch** — at a monthly fair with returning shoppers, one duplicate spotted in a box undoes months of corkboard trust.

**Trap A** — the absolute tell: nothing in the principle requires **invention**. **Trap B** — Ivy still labels the three unrepeatable mugs; the principle stays in use **where it is true.**

---

## Q4 — Read the table

**Bluestem Coffee** (drive-through) pulls one member's loyalty-app records:

| | Week 1 | Weeks 2–4 |
|---|---|---|
| Visits | 5 | 5 each |
| Window | 7:10–7:25 am | **same** |
| Order | 1 medium coffee | **same** |
| Price | $4 each | **same** |

> Which conclusion is supported?

---

## Q4 — Answer: **a habitual pattern — $80 across the four weeks**

**Why:** timing, frequency, and quantity are all steady — 5 visits × 4 weeks = 20 coffees × $4 = **$80**. Small routine buys run on **habit**; a rational comparison fits **consequential** purchases.

**Trap A** — comparing rivals would show **varied stops and varied orders.** **Trap C** — one month of records is not **lifetime value.** **Trap D** — the app **guarantees** nothing.

---

## Q5 — The straw ordinance

A city ordinance bars restaurants from handing out single-use plastic straws. Within a year, local stores stock **paper and metal straws**, and reusable-straw sales climb. Which best explains the change in purchasing patterns?

> A. **Legal restrictions can give rise to substitute products** as consumers find new ways to meet an existing need
> B. The ordinance **eliminated the need** the product served, so demand disappeared
> C. The change is a **situational influence**, because store layouts shifted
> D. **Consumer protection laws require** every banned product to be replaced by a licensed alternative

---

## Q5 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** when a law makes a product **harder to obtain, the want it served survives** — consumers find **substitutes** and businesses arrive to sell them. The straw leaves, the drink stays; paper and metal fill the gap.

**Trap B** — the ordinance changed **availability**, not the want. **Trap C** — an ordinance is a **legal factor** that holds until repealed, not a **temporary** situational condition.

---

## Q6 — The candle shop

A candle shop plays **quiet music**, keeps the **lighting warm**, and sets **open testers** by the entrance. Shoppers who came in to browse frequently leave with a purchase. Which best identifies the factor influencing these purchases?

> A. A **personal** factor (occupation and budget)
> B. A **social and cultural** factor (peers set the norm)
> C. A **situational influence** (store design creates temporary conditions that encourage buying)
> D. A **psychological** factor (the store changes each shopper's values and beliefs)

---

## Q6 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** store design, timing, and availability create **temporary conditions** — the music, warmth, and testers work on the shopper only **while she stands inside them.**

**Trap D** — the test is **what changed:** her surroundings for twenty minutes, not her values and beliefs — those walked out the same as they walked in.

---

## Wrap-up

- **Why consumers buy:** needs + rival goods, broken by four tiebreakers — price · features · availability · advertising
- **Rational vs. habitual:** match the depth of the process to the stakes
- **Four families:** personal · psychological · social/cultural · situational — *temporary, not product features*
- **Purchasing pattern** = timing + frequency + quantity; **laws create substitutes**; **technology reshapes both sides**
- **Seven Cialdini principles** — each has an honest and a manipulative application
- **Face test:** say the tactic to the customer's face — does it survive?

> **The honest version outsold the lie — that isn't luck, it's the psychology working as designed.**
