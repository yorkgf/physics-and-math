## Today's goals

By the end of this lesson you can:

1. Explain **why and how** marketers collect customer data
2. Use **market segmentation** to identify target customers and build a **customer profile**
3. Describe how **customer relationships** affect acquisition cost and lifetime value

**Running example all period:** Theo, 16, rebuilding bikes in his family's garage — **Trued Cycles.**

---

## Meet Theo — Trued Cycles

Theo is 16. He rebuilds neglected bikes in his family's garage.

- His first customer is **Ricky** (the candy seller from Unit 1)
- Ricky needs a bike to haul crates to his soccer-park snack box
- Theo sells him a **$60 used bike with a cargo rack**
- Ricky pays cash and asks for a **receipt**

Theo has no receipts — so he opens a **$2 spiral notebook** and writes one line: buy price, parts cost, hours, sale price.

> One request for paperwork created a **ledger** — and a ledger full of buyers is **customer data.**

---

## From receipt to ledger

Six weeks later, the notebook tells Theo something:

- Line 1: the $60 cargo bike (Ricky)
- Lines 2–7: **the same buyer six times** — a parent buying a safe first commuter bike for a middle schooler

> Six lines of ledger told Theo **who to serve, what to rebuild, and who his listings should speak to.**

That's the power of customer data — and today you learn to read it.

---

## What is marketing?

**Marketing** = all the activities a business undertakes to **identify customers' problems, needs, and wants**, and to **promote, sell, and deliver products** that meet them.

- Marketing ≠ advertising — advertising is **one piece** of marketing
- Theo studying local listings to see which bikes sell fast → **marketing**
- Pricing a finished bike, posting it, handing it over with the seat adjusted → **marketing**

> Marketing covers the **entire journey** from understanding the customer to delivering the product.

---

## What marketing is — in the examples

| Activity | Why it's marketing |
|---|---|
| Theo reads marketplace listings to see which bike styles sell fastest | **Identifying** what customers want |
| He writes a listing that says "21-point safety inspection" | **Promoting** to the right audience |
| He adjusts the seat height before handoff | **Delivering** a product that fits |

Every one of these is marketing — **none of them are advertising.**

---

## Why collect customer data?

Customer data feeds **three decisions** (EK 2.1.A.2):

1. Which **customers** the business should serve
2. Which **products** it should produce
3. **How** to market them effectively and profitably

Theo's notebook answered all three:
- **Who:** parents of middle schoolers (6 of 6 sales)
- **What:** safe commuter bikes ($95 range)
- **How:** listings that mention safety inspection + tune-up promise

---

## Why collect customer data — in the examples

**Riverbend Suds** — Dana Whitfield's small-batch soap business:

- Saturday farmers market stall + small online store
- Stall shoppers who join her **email list** get a free sample
- The online store **logs what each account browses and buys**
- A **checkout survey** asks age range, zip code, and what matters most

Reading the results → Dana sees **two clusters** and decides which one to serve next.

> Data answered: who buys, what they buy, and what they care about.

---

## Two kinds of customer data

| | Demographic | Psychographic |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | **Measurable qualities** describing a population | **Cognitive and behavioral factors** within a population |
| **Examples** | age, sex, race, ethnicity, income, location | interests, activities, values, lifestyles |
| **Sorting test** | Could a **census form** record it? → demographic | Does it describe **what a person cares about** or how they spend time? → psychographic |

> **Traits of the person = demographic · Contents of the mind = psychographic**

---

## Two kinds of data — in the examples

**Theo's notebook:**

| Data point | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Buyers in their late 30s–40s | Demographic | age — census records it |
| Budgets near $90 | Demographic | income — census records it |
| Homes near the Saturday flea market | Demographic | location — census records it |
| Value **safety over price** | Psychographic | values — what they care about |
| Want to **watch their kid ride** before paying | Psychographic | behavior — how they spend time |
| Respond to the **30-day tune-up promise** more than a discount | Psychographic | preferences — contents of the mind |

## Where data comes from — digital tools

| Tool | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Subscriber list** | Records who asked to hear from the business |
| **Online accounts** | Store each customer's history |
| **Click-tracking software** | Records what visitors tap/click |
| **Tracking apps** | Log where and when customers show up |
| **Social media monitoring** | Reading what people say publicly about a product |

> Theo runs none of these himself — the **marketplace** he lists on runs them and reports the data back.

---

## Where data comes from — traditional + purchased

**Traditional tools:**
- **Survey** = the same short questions put to many customers
- **Interview** = a longer conversation with one customer
- Theo's version: **two questions at every pickup** — *"How did you find this bike?"* and *"What almost stopped you from buying it?"*

**Purchased data:**
- Businesses **buy customer data** from other businesses
- Example: a national parts retailer pays a race organizer for its rider mailing list
- Theo buys nothing — but his notebook is exactly the kind of asset the market trades

---

## Where data comes from — in the examples

| Dana's tool | Type | What it captures |
|---|---|---|
| Email list (free sample for sign-up) | Digital — subscriber list | Who wants to hear from her |
| Online store software | Digital — online accounts + click-tracking | What each account browses and buys |
| Checkout survey | Traditional — survey | Age range, zip code, what matters most |
| Marketplace chat logs | Digital — social media monitoring | Questions every parent asks |

> One survey can collect **both demographic and psychographic data** — it depends on the **question asked**, never the tool.

## Real-world case — Luckin Coffee 瑞幸咖啡

**Luckin Coffee** — Chinese coffee chain, 9,000+ stores, competes with Starbucks with tech-driven affordable coffee.

**No cash registers.** Every order goes through the app or WeChat mini-program — capturing full behavioral data.

> Marketing ≠ advertising. Luckin's entire business runs on **data**, not ads.

---

## Luckin Coffee — data collection

| What Luckin collects | Tool | 2.1 concept |
|---|---|---|
| Order history, flavor preferences, frequency | App click-tracking + online accounts | Digital data tool |
| User tags (1,000+ per user: gender, ice/hot, social energy, scenario) | Customer Data Platform (CDP) | Demographic + psychographic |
| Location, visit patterns | Tracking app (LBS) | Digital data tool |
| Community feedback, complaints | WeChat groups + social media monitoring | Traditional + digital |

---

## Luckin Coffee — segmentation + CRM

**Target customer:** 50% aged 20-29, 60%+ office workers, 30%+ in Beijing/Shanghai/Guangzhou

**Relationship tactics:**
- "Invite a friend, both get free cup" → launch day: new users **doubled**, orders **+40%**
- WeChat community groups: **1.8 million** users, **35,000+** direct orders/day
- After joining community: monthly frequency **+30%**, weekly repurchase **+28%**
- Stores open 3+ months: repurchase rate **>80%**

> Every data point feeds the **who / what / how** decisions — and every relationship tactic pushes **CAC down** and **LTV up.**

## Market segmentation

**Market segmentation** = aggregating potential customers into groups — **market segments** — based on **shared demographic and psychographic characteristics** (EK 2.1.B.1)

- Helps businesses understand what each group **needs**
- Shows how products might **address** those needs

Theo's buyers sort into **three segments:**

| Segment | Buys | Cares about |
|---|---|---|
| Parents of younger kids | $60 bikes | mostly working brakes |
| **Parents of middle schoolers** | **$95 commuters** | safety, test ride, guarantee |
| Adult hobbyists | $150 road bikes | parts list, negotiate hardest |

---

## Market segmentation — in the examples

**Riverbend Suds — Dana's two clusters:**

| Segment | Demographic traits | Psychographic traits |
|---|---|---|
| Shoppers **over 50** | age 50+ | buy single unscented bars; rank **sensitive-skin ingredients** first |
| Shoppers in their **late 20s–30s** | age 25–39 | buy **gift sets**; follow shop on social media; rank **plastic-free packaging** first |

Dana decides her next product line will serve the **younger cluster.**

> Segments are groups. But Dana needs to go **one zoom level deeper.**

---

## Target customers

**Target customers** = the buyers **most likely to purchase** a specific product because their wants, needs, and preferences already point at it (EK 2.1.B.2)

Theo's notebook makes the choice: **6 of 6 sales** sit in the commuter-parent segment.

> That segment gets his rebuild hours, stall space, and listing wording.

**Why narrow the aim?**

Products, branding, pricing, and advertising designed for **one target population** usually work **better** and cost **less** than appeals to everyone.

---

## Target customers — in the examples

Two listings that cost the same to post:

**Broad:** *"Used bikes for sale, all sizes, good prices."*

**Targeted:** *"Inspected commuter bikes for middle schoolers, 21-point safety check on an orange tag, test rides Saturday, 30-day tune-up included."*

The second answers **Renee's exact worries in order** — and Renee is the one holding the money.

> Targeting **improves the odds** — it never **guarantees** the sale.

---

## Customer profile

**Customer profile** = a **fictional description** of a specific sample customer, combining demographic data, psychographic data, and that person's wants, needs, and preferences (EK 2.1.B.3)

Theo's customer profile — written on an index card:

> **Renee, 41.** Wants a safe first commuter bike for her middle schooler.
> Budget: $90. Deadline: Saturday.
> Values the test ride and the tune-up promise more than the price.

Renee is **fictional** — but every line on her card is backed by **six real buyers** in the notebook.

---

## Customer profile — in the examples

**Dana's customer profile:**

> **Marisol, 31.** Renter who hosts friends often.
> Follows zero-waste creators on social media.
> Buys gifts locally.
> Cares most about **plastic-free packaging.**

| Profile element | Type |
|---|---|
| Age 31, renter | Demographic |
| Hosts friends, follows zero-waste creators, buys locally | Psychographic |
| Plastic-free packaging | Psychographic (values) |

> **Segment = the group · Profile = one invented person who stands for it.**

Customers don't buy because they're thirsty — they buy for **social check-in, lifestyle display, and aesthetic experience.**

## Building customer relationships

A sale to Renee is worth more if the **relationship outlives it** (EK 2.1.C.1).

Three standard tactics:

| Tactic | What it does | Theo's version |
|---|---|---|
| **Personalized service** | Treats a customer as a known individual | 30-day free tune-up — greets the kid by name |
| **Rewards programs** | Gives repeat buyers a reason to return | $10 off a sibling's bike for any family in the notebook |
| **Customer feedback opportunities** | Asks buyers what to fix | Two questions at every pickup |

Social media and the internet make all three **cheaper to run.**

---

## Building relationships — in the examples

**Theo's 30-day tune-up:**
- Bike comes back within a month
- He trues the wheels free
- Greets the kid by name
- Every post on social media (with permission) reminds past buyers Trued Cycles is still open

**Theo's sibling discount:**
- $10 off for any family already in the notebook
- Satisfied customers **refer new customers**
- A referred buyer arrives at **zero marketing cost**

> Strong relationships push acquisition cost **down** and lifetime value **up.**

---

## Customer acquisition cost (CAC)

**CAC** = total marketing, advertising, and sales costs used to acquire customers **÷** number of customers acquired (EK 2.1.C.2)

**Theo's first month at the flea market:**

- Spends **$20** (stall fees + photocopied flyers)
- **4 buyers** come in through them

$$\text{CAC} = \frac{\$20}{4} = \$5 \text{ per customer}$$

> Satisfied customers **refer** new customers — and a referred buyer arrives at **zero marketing cost**, pushing CAC **down.**

---

## CAC — in the examples

**Cinder Peak Cocoa** ran two campaigns:

| | Spring | Fall |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising costs | $6,000 | $6,000 |
| Other marketing & sales costs | $2,000 | $3,000 |
| New customers acquired | 400 | 300 |

$$\text{Spring CAC} = \frac{6000 + 2000}{400} = \frac{8000}{400} = \$20$$

$$\text{Fall CAC} = \frac{6000 + 3000}{300} = \frac{9000}{300} = \$30$$

> CAC uses **ALL** acquisition costs — marketing **+** advertising **+** sales. Never just advertising alone.

---

## CAC — the trap

Fall's CAC ($30) is **higher** than Spring's ($20).

Does that mean Fall was worse?

**Not necessarily.** Watch what happens when we add lifetime value next.

> Never judge CAC **on its own** — always compare it to LTV.

---

## Lifetime value (LTV)

**LTV** = the estimated amount a customer will spend on the business's products **over time** (EK 2.1.C.3)

Track LTV in **margin** (the part you keep) so value and cost stay in the same units.

**Building Renee's LTV:**

| Step | Value |
|---|---|
| Margin on her first bike | $38 |
| Two paid tune-ups over the years ($12 margin each) | $24 |
| A sibling bike later (~$65), given a 40% chance → 0.40 × 65 | $26 |

$$\text{LTV (in margin)} = 38 + 24 + 26 = \$88$$

---

## LTV vs. CAC — the business case

$$\text{Renee: } \$88 \text{ lifetime value} - \$5 \text{ acquisition cost} = \$83 \text{ net}$$

That gap is the **business case** for the free tune-up, the sibling discount, and the pickup photos.

> Satisfied customers **widen the gap** through brand loyalty and repeat purchases.

---

## LTV — in the examples

Back to **Cinder Peak Cocoa:**

| | Spring | Fall |
|---|---|---|
| CAC | $20 | $30 |
| Average customer LTV | $60 | $90 |
| **LTV − CAC** | **$40** | **$60** |

Fall paid **$10 more** per customer to **gain $30 more** in value.

> A higher CAC can still be the **better buy** when LTV grows faster.

---

## Your turn — CAC calculation

> A small bakery spends **$1,200** on flyers, social media ads, and a tasting event. It acquires **80 new customers.**

What is the CAC?

$$\text{CAC} = \frac{\text{total marketing + advertising + sales costs}}{\text{customers acquired}}$$

## Data risks — privacy

Every tactic in this topic runs on collected data — and **collection creates risk** (EK 2.1.D.1).

**Theo's problem:** His notes app holds **19 customer phone numbers.** He never wrote down **why** he keeps them. Nothing on the form says **how long** a number stays or what else it might be used for.

> Collecting, compiling, and storing customer data can violate customers' **privacy** — especially when people are **unaware** the collection is happening or **uninformed** about how the data may be used.

---

## Data risks — breach, identity theft, fraud

Unsecured data adds a second layer of risk (EK 2.1.D.2):

| Risk | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Data breach** | Exposes stored information to outsiders |
| **Identity theft** | Uses stolen personal details to impersonate a person |
| **Fraud** | Uses deception (often powered by stolen data) to take money or property |

Theo's whole database is **one phone carried through a flea market every Saturday.** One stolen bag hands a stranger 19 families' names, numbers, and chat logs showing which kid rides which bike home.

---

## Data risks — in the examples

**The neighborhood gym** — advertises itself as a **trusted community space** but stores members' payment card numbers and check-in histories in an **unsecured spreadsheet.**

A breach could:
- **Drive members away** — customers who no longer feel safe leave
- **Violate the gym's stated values** — "trusted" becomes a broken promise
- **Damage its reputation** — the story reaches people who never joined

> The three business-side risks: **lost customers, violated values, damaged reputation.**

---

## Balancing data benefits and risks

Businesses must balance the **benefits** of using data against the **potential risks** (EK 2.1.D.3):

| Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|
| Better targeting | Privacy violations |
| Higher LTV | Data breaches |
| Lower CAC | Identity theft / fraud |
| Better products | Loss of customers |
| | Violation of core values |
| | Harm to reputation |

---

## Theo's fix — three steps

1. **Lock** the phone
2. **Delete** every number that has no purpose
3. **Add one line** to the order form stating what gets kept and why

> Lock → Delete → Disclose. Simple, but most small businesses skip all three.

---

## Exam points to remember

- **Marketing ≠ advertising** — it's identify + promote + sell + deliver; data feeds the who / what / how decisions
- **Demographic** (measurable, census-recordable) vs. **psychographic** (cares about / how they spend time) — use the **sorting test**
- **Segmentation → target customer → customer profile (Renee):** narrow the aim because targeted appeals work better and cost less
- **Three relationship tactics:** personalized service, rewards programs, customer feedback opportunities
- **CAC** = ALL acquisition costs ÷ customers acquired (referrals push it toward zero)
- **LTV** = estimated spending over time — track in margin to compare with cost
- **Data risks:** privacy violation, data breach, identity theft, fraud — weigh benefits against trust / core values / reputation
- **Fix pattern:** lock → delete → disclose

## Practice — Q1

> Which best describes the customer data the checkout survey collects?
>
> A. Only demographic data, because survey answers can be counted
>
> B. **Both** demographic data (age + zip code) **and** psychographic data (what matters most)
>
> C. Only psychographic data, because surveys record opinions

**Answer: B.**

## Practice — Q2

> Which best explains the advantage Dana gains by designing the next product line for the younger cluster?
>
> A. She can design branding, pricing, and advertising that appeal to **anyone who might ever buy** soap
>
> B. Serving one segment **guarantees** the new line outsells her current products
>
> C. She can stop collecting data from customers outside the chosen segment

## Practice — Q2 answer

**Answer: D.**

**Traps:**

- **B** — **"guarantees"** is the tell. Targeting **improves the odds** and promises nothing.

## Practice — Q4

> Which best explains how a rewards program for frequent buyers can increase a business's profits?
>
> A. It reduces the **cost of manufacturing** each unit sold
>
> B. It **guarantees** members will keep making repeat purchases
>
> C. It builds **brand loyalty and repeat purchasing**, which raises the **lifetime value** of each customer

## Practice — Q4 answer

**Answer: C.**

Relationship tactics (rewards, personalized service, feedback) **raise LTV** because loyal customers make repeat purchases. Satisfied customers also **refer friends**, pushing CAC **down.**

**Traps:**
- **B** — **"guarantees"** again. A rewards program **raises the probability** and promises nothing.
- **A** — **borrows from operations.** A punch card changes buying behavior and leaves manufacturing cost **untouched.**

> **Relationship tactics move LTV and CAC — never production cost.**

## Practice — Q5

**Cinder Peak Cocoa** ran one campaign in spring and one in fall:

| | Spring | Fall |
|---|---|---|

## Practice — Q5 answer

$$\text{Spring CAC} = \frac{6000+2000}{400} = \$20 \qquad \text{Fall CAC} = \frac{6000+3000}{300} = \$30$$

| | Spring | Fall |
|---|---|---|
| CAC | $20 | $30 |
| LTV | $60 | $90 |
| **LTV − CAC** | **$40** | **$60** |

**Answer: Fall produced more value per customer — LTV exceeds CAC by $60 vs. $40.**

- **"Spring CAC = $15"** — divides advertising alone (6000÷400). CAC uses **ALL** costs.

> **Compute CAC from all costs, then judge it against LTV — never on its own.**

> A neighborhood gym that advertises itself as a **trusted community space** stores members' payment card numbers and check-in histories in an **unsecured spreadsheet.** Which best explains the risk this creates **for the gym itself?**

> **The three business-side risks: lost customers · violated values · damaged reputation.**
