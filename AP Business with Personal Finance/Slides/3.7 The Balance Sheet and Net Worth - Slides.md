# Part 1: One date, one equation
### LO 3.7.A — What a balance sheet is

---

## The balance sheet and the accounting equation (3.7.A.1)

**3.7.A.1** A **balance sheet** is a financial statement that compares a business's **assets** to its **liabilities and owners' equity** — the **net worth of the business to its owners** — **at a specific point in time**.

According to the **balance sheet equation** (also called the **fundamental accounting equation**):

**Assets = Liabilities + Owners' Equity**

| | **Income statement** | **Balance sheet** |
|---|---|---|
| **Time** | A **period** (a year, a quarter) | **A specific point in time** |
| **Question** | "How much did we earn?" | "What are we worth on this date?" |
| **Metaphor** | A video | A photograph |

> **"At a specific point in time" — and the equation has a second name, the *fundamental accounting equation*. Both are testable.**

---

## Why it is called a *balance* sheet

**Every transaction touches two places**, so the two sides always agree.

| Transaction | Assets | Liabilities + Equity |
|---|---|---|
| Borrow $13,250 | Cash **+13,250** | Loan **+13,250** |
| Buy a $9,000 machine with cash | Equipment **+9,000**, cash **−9,000** | *(no change)* |
| Sell a $5 coffee for cash | Cash **+5** | Equity **+5** (profit) |
| Pay a $500 supplier invoice | Cash **−500** | Accounts payable **−500** |
| Receive a $1,000 payment from a customer on credit | Cash **+1,000**, AR **−1,000** | *(no change)* |

> **If your answer changes only one side, you are missing something. The equation is a checking mechanism, not just a formula.**

# Part 2: Assets, grouped by liquidity
### LO 3.7.A.2

---

## Assets are grouped by liquidity (3.7.A.2)

**3.7.A.2** The assets listed on a balance sheet include **every item of value owned by a business**, grouped **according to liquidity** into three groups:

| Group | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Current assets** | Highly liquid; used to fund **day-to-day operations** |
| **Long-term assets** | Less liquid; used to **run business operations** |
| **Intangible assets** | **Nonphysical**, but valuable |

**3.7.A.2.i — Liquidity** is the **ease with which an asset can be converted into cash**.

> **Liquidity is the organising principle of the asset side. Not "how big" or "how important" — how *fast it becomes cash*.**

---

## Current assets (3.7.A.2.ii)

**3.7.A.2.ii** **Current assets** are considered **highly liquid** and are used to fund the **day-to-day operations** of the business:

| Current asset | DK Coffee Lab | Liquidity rank |
|---|---|---|
| **Cash** | $22,000 | 1 — already cash |
| **Short-term investments** | $4,000 | 2 — sellable in days |
| **Accounts receivable** (money owed by customers) | $1,500 | 3 — collectible in weeks |
| **Inventory** | $6,000 | 4 — must be **sold**, then collected |
| **Total current assets** | **$33,500** | |

> **Accounts receivable is money owed *to* the business — an asset. Accounts payable is money owed *by* the business — a liability. The direction of the arrow decides the side of the statement.**

---

## Long-term assets (3.7.A.2.iii)

**3.7.A.2.iii** **Long-term assets** are considered **less liquid**. They include:

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| **Fixed assets** | Production plants, equipment, vehicles, buildings — **used to run business operations** |
| **Long-term investments** | Investments held beyond a year |

**DK Coffee Lab:** roaster and espresso machines $38,000 + café building $180,000 = **$218,000**

> **Fixed assets are not held for sale — they are held to operate. That is why they are not "current" no matter how valuable they are.**

---

## Intangible assets (3.7.A.2.iv)

**3.7.A.2.iv** **Intangible assets** — such as **patents, brand names, and trademarks** — are **not physical items** but **have value because they represent potential revenue**.

**DK Coffee Lab:** the "DK Coffee Lab" trademark and brand — **$12,000**

| Intangible | What it protects |
|---|---|
| **Patent** | An invention — the exclusive right to make or use it |
| **Brand name / trademark** | Identity customers recognise and pay for |
| **Copyright** | An original creative work |

> **The test is "does it represent potential revenue without being a thing you can touch?"** DK's trademark is why customers pay $5 instead of $3 — and that is exactly what the EK means by *value*.

---

## DK Coffee Lab's asset side

| Group | Items | Total |
|---|---|---|
| **Current** | Cash $22,000 · short-term investments $4,000 · AR $1,500 · inventory $6,000 | **$33,500** |
| **Long-term** | Equipment $38,000 · building $180,000 | **$218,000** |
| **Intangible** | Trademark and brand $12,000 | **$12,000** |
| **Total assets** | | **$263,500** |

> **Three groups, in that order — most liquid first. The ordering is itself a piece of knowledge: a balance sheet reads as a liquidity ladder from top to bottom.**

# Part 3: Claims on the assets
### LO 3.7.A.3 / 3.7.A.4 / 3.7.A.5

---

## Liabilities are grouped by due date (3.7.A.3)

**3.7.A.3** **Liabilities** represent **debts or obligations owed by a business**, grouped into **current and long-term liabilities**, **in order of when payment is due**.

**3.7.A.3.i — Current liabilities** must be paid **within one year**:

| Current liability | DK Coffee Lab |
|---|---|
| **Accounts payable** (money owed to suppliers) | $9,000 |
| **Short-term debt** | $0 |
| **Current payments on long-term debt** | $3,000 |
| **Accrued (unpaid) operating expenses** | $2,800 |
| **Total current liabilities** | **$14,800** |

> **Note the third item: part of a long-term loan is a *current* liability — the portion due this year. The same loan appears on both sides of the one-year line.**

---

## Long-term liabilities (3.7.A.3.ii)

**3.7.A.3.ii** **Long-term liabilities** — such as **mortgages, bank loans, and long-term bonds** — represent obligations to pay **beyond one year**.

**DK Coffee Lab:** the remaining $8,400 of the bank loan = **$8,400 long-term**

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Current liabilities | $14,800 |
| Long-term liabilities | $8,400 |
| **Total liabilities** | **$23,200** |

> **The one-year line splits the identical instrument. This is why a business can look safe one year and fragile the next without borrowing a cent more.**

---

## Owners' equity (3.7.A.4)

**3.7.A.4** **Owners' equity** — the **net worth of the business** — is the **difference between assets and liabilities**.

It is often comprised of:

| Component | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Stock** | Value of ownership shares issued |
| **Retained earnings** | The business's **cumulative profits not paid out as dividends** |

**DK Coffee Lab:** $263,500 − $23,200 = **$240,300**

> **Retained earnings is a cumulative total, not this year's profit.** Year 1's $35,100 net profit (3.6) is only *part* of it — earlier profits and dividends also shaped the number.

---

## The finished balance sheet

| **Assets** | | **Liabilities + Owners' Equity** | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | $22,000 | Accounts payable | $9,000 |
| Short-term investments | $4,000 | Accrued expenses | $2,800 |
| Accounts receivable | $1,500 | Current portion of long-term debt | $3,000 |
| Inventory | $6,000 | **Current liabilities** | **$14,800** |
| **Current assets** | **$33,500** | Bank loan (long-term) | $8,400 |
| Equipment | $38,000 | **Total liabilities** | **$23,200** |
| Building | $180,000 | Stock | $200,000 |
| **Long-term assets** | **$218,000** | Retained earnings | $40,300 |
| Trademark and brand | $12,000 | **Owners' equity** | **$240,300** |
| **Total assets** | **$263,500** | **Total liabilities + equity** | **$263,500** |

---

## Comparable data is part of the statement (3.7.A.5)

**3.7.A.5** A balance sheet **typically provides a snapshot of comparable data** — for example, the **end of a quarter** — **from a previous year or multiple years**, for **purposes of comparison**.

| | Dec 31, year 1 | Dec 31, year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Current assets | $33,500 | $41,000 |
| Current liabilities | $14,800 | $19,500 |
| **Working capital** | **$18,700** | **$21,500** |

> **Same logic as 3.6.A.1's "multiple periods": one number is a fact, two numbers are a trend. The exam often hands you two columns and asks what changed.**

# Part 4: Reading a balance sheet
### LO 3.7.B — Net worth, working capital, risk

---

## Who reads it (3.7.B.1)

**3.7.B.1** **Internal stakeholders** — business managers and owners — as well as **external stakeholders** — lenders and investors — use balance sheets to **evaluate the financial condition of a business at a point in time** (for example, the end of a year or quarter).

**Internal and external stakeholders read the same statement for different reasons:**

| Stakeholder | What they are checking |
|---|---|
| **Managers and owners** | Can we fund operations? Where is the strain? |
| **Lenders** | Is there enough equity cushion? Can they repay? |
| **Investors** | Is the business worth what we are paying for it? |

---

## Three questions the balance sheet answers (3.7.B.2)

**3.7.B.2** A balance sheet indicates:

| # | Indicates | How you check it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Whether the business has a **positive net worth** | Owners' equity > 0 (assets > liabilities) |
| 2 | Whether it has **sufficient working capital** to fund day-to-day operations | **Current assets ≥ current liabilities** |
| 3 | Whether its **debt level is comparable to similar businesses** | Compare with industry peers |

**DK Coffee Lab: working capital = $33,500 − $14,800 = $18,700 → positive and comfortable.**

> **Working capital is the CED's phrase for "current assets meet or exceed current liabilities." Learn it as a phrase — it appears verbatim in questions.**

---

## When current assets are not enough (3.7.B.3)

**3.7.B.3** Business owners may **shut down the business or pursue bankruptcy** if the business **cannot access sufficient current assets to fund operations**.

**Bankruptcy** is a **legal process** through which a business can:

- **liquidate assets**
- **eliminate or repay debts**
- **either shut down or reorganize** the business **under legal supervision**

> **Note: bankruptcy is not only "closing." Reorganization under legal supervision is a bankruptcy outcome — and the process is *legal*, not merely financial.**

# Part 5: Personal net worth
### LO 3.7.C — Households, lenders, retirement

---

## Net worth belongs to a household (3.7.C.1)

**3.7.C.1** **Personal net worth** is typically calculated for a **household**, which **may include more than one individual**.

It is determined by:

1. **adding all of the household's assets** — **savings and investments, property, and personal possessions**
2. **subtracting all liabilities**

**Net worth = household assets − household liabilities**

> **"Household," not "person" — a couple's net worth is one number, and so is a family's. And note "personal possessions" counts as an asset, not just financial accounts.**

---

## The Ruiz household net worth

**The Ruiz household** — two earners, one mortgage application:

| Assets | | Liabilities | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Savings and investments | $22,600 | Credit card balances | $3,400 |
| Retirement accounts | $41,000 | Car loan | $9,200 |
| Home (market value) | $265,000 | Mortgage | $198,000 |
| Car (market value) | $14,000 | Student loans | $24,000 |
| Personal possessions | $18,000 | | |
| **Total assets** | **$360,600** | **Total liabilities** | **$234,600** |

**Net worth = $360,600 − $234,600 = $126,000**

> **Two people, one number. Adding a spouse's accounts on both sides is the household concept in practice.**

---

## Why lenders ask for it (3.7.C.2)

**3.7.C.2** **Lenders may require information about a consumer's household net worth** as part of the **loan application process**.

**Why it matters:** net worth is the **cushion** behind the loan. A household with positive net worth can absorb a shock and still repay; one with negative net worth has nothing to fall back on.

> **This is the consumer mirror of 3.7.B.2's equity test: lenders check the same thing for a household that they check for a business.**

---

## Why retirement planning needs it (3.7.C.3)

**3.7.C.3** **Consumers and financial planners consider net worth** when determining whether individuals have **sufficient savings to retire**.

**Two households, same income:**

| | Household A | Household B |
|---|---|---|
| Assets | $620,000 (home $500,000, savings $120,000) | $620,000 (savings $560,000, home $60,000) |
| Liabilities | $410,000 (mortgage) | $10,000 |
| **Net worth** | **$210,000** | **$610,000** |

> **Same gross assets, three times the net worth — because retirement depends on what you *keep*, not what you *hold*. This is why 3.7.C.3 asks for net worth, not asset value.**

---

## 🔑 Exam points to remember — Balance sheet and net worth

| # | Point |
|---|-------|
| 1 | A **balance sheet** compares **assets** to **liabilities and owners' equity** — the **net worth of the business to its owners** — **at a specific point in time** |
| 2 | **Assets = Liabilities + Owners' Equity** — the **balance sheet equation**, also called the **fundamental accounting equation** |
| 3 | Assets are grouped **by liquidity** into **current, long-term, and intangible** |
| 4 | **Liquidity** = the ease with which an asset can be converted into **cash** |
| 5 | **Current assets** — cash, short-term investments, **accounts receivable**, inventory — are highly liquid and fund **day-to-day operations** |
| 6 | **Long-term assets** — **fixed assets** and long-term investments — are less liquid and **used to run operations** |
| 7 | **Intangible assets** — **patents, brand names, trademarks** — are nonphysical but valuable because they **represent potential revenue** |
| 8 | Liabilities are grouped as **current** (due **within one year**: AP, short-term debt, current payments on long-term debt, accrued operating expenses) and **long-term** (mortgages, bank loans, long-term bonds), **in order of when payment is due** |
| 9 | **Owners' equity** = **assets − liabilities** = net worth of the business; often **stock + retained earnings** (cumulative profits not paid as dividends) |
| 10 | A balance sheet typically shows **comparable data from previous years or quarters** for comparison |
| 11 | **Internal and external stakeholders** use it to evaluate financial condition **at a point in time** |
| 12 | It shows whether the business has **positive net worth**, **sufficient working capital** (**current assets ≥ current liabilities**), and a **debt level comparable to similar businesses** |
| 13 | Without sufficient current assets, owners may **shut down or pursue bankruptcy** — a **legal process** to liquidate assets, eliminate or repay debts, and **shut down or reorganize under legal supervision** |

| 15 | **Lenders may require household net worth** during a loan application |
| 16 | Consumers and **financial planners** use net worth to judge whether savings are **sufficient to retire** |

---

## Three exam tells — balance sheet

**Tell 1 — "Point in time or period?"** Balance sheet = a date ("as of December 31"). Income statement = a period ("for the year ended December 31"). This resolves a surprising number of questions instantly.

**Tell 2 — "Then or now?"** Current = within one year. Long-term = beyond one year. Intangible = nonphysical but revenue-generating. And remember that a single loan can be split across the one-year line.

**Tell 3 — "Assets or net worth?"** Lenders, investors, and retirement planners care about **what is left after liabilities**. Report net worth, not gross assets, unless gross assets are specifically asked for.

## Practice — Stimulus

**Cedar & Pine Furniture**, December 31:

| Assets | | Liabilities and equity | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | $18,000 | Accounts payable | $96,000 |
| Short-term investments | $5,000 | Accrued operating expenses | $22,000 |
| Accounts receivable | $42,000 | Current portion of long-term debt | $30,000 |
| Inventory | $88,000 | Mortgage (20 years remaining) | $260,000 |
| Equipment | $210,000 | Long-term bonds | $100,000 |
| Building | $340,000 | Stock | $120,000 |
| Brand name | $25,000 | Retained earnings | $100,000 |

---

## Practice — Q1

> What is Cedar & Pine's total assets?

> A. $703,000
> B. $728,000
> C. $153,000
> D. $593,000

---

## Practice — Q1 answer

**Answer: B.**

| Group | Items | Total |
|---|---|---|
| **Current** | 18,000 + 5,000 + 42,000 + 88,000 | $153,000 |
| **Long-term** | 210,000 + 340,000 | $550,000 |
| **Intangible** | brand name | $25,000 |
| **Total assets** | | **$728,000** |

**Traps:**
- **A** — dropped the $25,000 brand name because it is not physical; intangible assets are still assets (3.7.A.2.iv)
- **C** — that is current assets only
- **D** — long-term assets plus brand name, omitting current assets

> **Intangible still means asset. It has value because it represents potential revenue.**

---

## Practice — Q2

> What is Cedar & Pine's owners' equity?

> A. $220,000
> B. $120,000
> C. $508,000
> D. $250,000

---

## Practice — Q2 answer

**Answer: A.**

**Total liabilities** = current ($96,000 + $22,000 + $30,000 = $148,000) + long-term ($260,000 + $100,000 = $360,000) = **$508,000**.

**Owners' equity = assets − liabilities = $728,000 − $508,000 = $220,000** (3.7.A.4).

Check: stock $120,000 + retained earnings $100,000 = $220,000 ✓

**Traps:**
- **B** — that is stock only; retained earnings is equity too
- **C** — that is total liabilities
- **D** — omitted the $30,000 current portion of long-term debt from liabilities, which inflates equity

> **The current portion of a long-term loan is still a liability. Miss it and both sides break.**

---

## Practice — Q3

> What is Cedar & Pine's working capital, and what does it indicate?

> A. $5,000 — current assets just meet current liabilities, so day-to-day funding is tight
> B. $153,000 — current assets are more than enough
> C. −$148,000 — the business is insolvent
> D. $220,000 — owners' equity covers all obligations

---

## Practice — Q3 answer

**Answer: A.**

**Working capital = current assets − current liabilities** = $153,000 − $148,000 = **$5,000** (3.7.B.2).

Current assets **do** meet current liabilities — but only barely. The business satisfies the CED's test while having almost no cushion, which is exactly the fragile position 3.7.B.3 warns about.

**Traps:**
- **B** — that is current assets, not working capital
- **C** — subtracts *all* liabilities from current assets; working capital uses only current items
- **D** — equity is not working capital; the two answer different questions

> **Working capital compares current to current. Nothing else enters the calculation.**

---

## Practice — Q4

> A lender asks the Ruiz household for a net worth statement. Which calculation is correct under 3.7.C.1?

> A. Savings and investments minus liabilities only
> B. All household assets — savings and investments, property, and personal possessions — minus all liabilities
> C. Total income minus total expenses for the year
> D. Assets of the primary earner minus that earner's liabilities

---

## Practice — Q4 answer

**Answer: B.**

**3.7.C.1**: personal net worth is calculated for a **household** (which may include more than one individual) by **adding all of the household's assets, including savings and investments, property, and personal possessions**, and **subtracting all liabilities**.

**Traps:**
- **A** — omits property and personal possessions, which the EK names explicitly
- **C** — that is a cash flow or budget question, not net worth
- **D** — the household is the unit, not the primary earner
- **B** is correct and also explains 3.7.C.2: lenders want it because it measures the cushion behind the loan

> **Household, all assets, all liabilities.**

---

## Trap patterns to watch

| # | Trap type | Where it appears |
|---|-----------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Intangible ≠ asset** — it is an asset with no physical form | Q1 |
| 2 | **Current portion of long-term debt left out** of current liabilities | Q2 |
| 3 | **Retained earnings ignored** — equity = stock **+** retained earnings | Q2 |
| 4 | **Working capital uses all liabilities** — current against current only | Q3 |
| 5 | **Equity = working capital** — different questions, different numbers | Q3 |
| 6 | **Net worth per person** — the household is the unit | Q4 |
| 7 | **Net worth = income − expenses** — that is a budget, not a balance sheet | Q4 |
| 8 | **Balance sheet = income statement** — a point in time vs. a period | 3.7.A.1 |
| 9 | **Bankruptcy = closing** — reorganization under legal supervision is also bankruptcy | 3.7.B.3 |

## Wrap-up — 3.7 The Balance Sheet and Net Worth

**What we covered:**
- A **balance sheet** compares **assets** to **liabilities and owners' equity** at **a specific point in time**
- **Assets = Liabilities + Owners' Equity** — the **balance sheet equation** / **fundamental accounting equation**
- Assets are grouped **by liquidity**: **current** (cash, short-term investments, AR, inventory), **long-term** (fixed assets, long-term investments), **intangible** (patents, brand names, trademarks)
- **Liquidity** = the ease of converting an asset to **cash**
- Liabilities are grouped **by due date**: **current** (within one year) and **long-term**
- **Owners' equity = assets − liabilities** = the business's net worth; often **stock + retained earnings**
- Statements show **comparable prior-year data**, and **internal and external stakeholders** use them to judge financial condition
- The balance sheet reveals **positive net worth**, **sufficient working capital** (current assets ≥ current liabilities), and **comparable debt levels**
- Without enough current assets, owners may **shut down or pursue bankruptcy** — a legal process that can end in **shutdown or reorganization**
- **Personal net worth** is a **household** figure: all assets (including property and possessions) minus all liabilities — used by **lenders** and in **retirement planning**
