# Part 1: Why businesses need outside money
### LO 3.5.A — Need, break-even, and timing

---

## First, spend your own money (bootstrapping)

**3.5.A.1** Many entrepreneurs initially rely on:

| Source | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Bootstrapping** | Using **personal savings** |
| **Personal bank loans** | Borrowing as an individual, not as the business |
| **Personal credit** | Credit cards or personal lines of credit |

> **Bootstrapping keeps full ownership and control — and limits how fast you can grow.** Remember that trade; it returns in 3.5.B.

---

## How much outside money do you actually need?

**3.5.A.2** Entrepreneurs evaluate their need for **external financial capital (cash)** by:

1. **Comparing their personal funds to the business's initial needs**
2. **Calculating how many goods or services they would need to sell in a period to break even** — to cover all costs for the period

**DK Coffee Lab, both steps:**

| Step | Numbers |
|---|---|
| Total startup need | $11,000 one-time + $7,250 initial = **$18,250** |
| Personal funds available | **$5,000** |
| **Gap** | **$13,250** |
| Break-even check | Monthly fixed costs ≈ $4,800; contribution per drink ≈ $4 − $1.30 = **$2.70** → **≈ 1,778 drinks/month** |

> **The gap alone is not the answer — the break-even volume tells you whether the gap is *temporary* or *fatal*.**

---

## When the gap is worth filling (3.5.A.3)

**3.5.A.3** Entrepreneurs seek external financial capital **if they lack the funds required to pay startup costs and operating costs for a sufficient period of time to break even**.

> **Three conditions must all be true:** (1) funds are insufficient, (2) for startup *and* operating costs, (3) for long enough to reach break-even.

**A business that runs out of cash one month before break-even needed the same money as one that runs out a year before — but only the second is a real funding problem.** The exam rewards naming the *time* dimension.

---

## Why *established* businesses raise capital (3.5.A.4)

**3.5.A.4** Established businesses seek external financial capital for reasons that have nothing to do with survival:

| Reason | Example at DK Coffee Lab |
|---|---|
| **Financing new product development** | A bottled cold-brew line |
| **Replacing fixed assets** | The 2019 espresso machine finally dies |
| **Increasing sales volume and revenue** | A second location, more marketing |

> **New businesses raise money to *start*. Established businesses raise money to *grow or replace*.** Same capital, different logic — a favourite distinction in FRQs.

---

## The reason that hits profitable businesses (3.5.A.5)

**3.5.A.5** New and established businesses often seek external financial capital **to manage cash flow** because:

- **revenue collection can be irregular**
- **expenses must nonetheless always be paid**

> **Profitability and liquidity are different clocks.** A profitable café still pays rent on the 1st, wages on the 15th, and gets paid by its corporate catering client on the 45th. That timing gap is a financing need.

# Part 2: Two doors — loans and equity
### LO 3.5.B — Sources of financial capital

---

## The only two doors

**3.5.B.1** Most sources of financial capital are classified as **loans** or **equity financing**.

| | **Loans (debt)** | **Equity financing** |
|---|---|---|
| **What the funder gets** | Repayment **with interest** | **Ownership shares** |
| **What the business gives up** | Cash (interest is a **business expense**) | **Some control** over decisions + **a portion of future profits** |
| **Repayment required?** | Yes, on a schedule | No — investors are paid only if profits are distributed |
| **Who they become** | A **lender** (creditor) | A **part owner** |

> **This table answers half the questions in this topic. Sort the funder: lender or owner?**

---

## Business loans (3.5.B.1.i)

**3.5.B.1.i** Business loans are similar to personal loans: **must be repaid with interest**.

- **Interest on loans is a business expense** — it appears on the income statement as **interest expense** (3.6.A.7)
- **Higher interest rates and/or larger loans increase the cost of borrowing**

> **Debt is a fixed obligation: it must be paid whether or not the business earns a profit. That is the whole risk of the loan door.**

---

## Equity financing (3.5.B.1.ii)

**3.5.B.1.ii** **Equity financing** is a type of business funding that requires:

- **issuing ownership shares**
- **ceding some control over decisions**
- **and a portion of future profits** to investors, who become **part owners**

> **The cost of equity is invisible on the income statement — it is control and future profit. Students routinely say equity is "free" because there is no interest payment. It is not free; it is just not billed.**

---

## Who can raise what — the maturity ladder

**3.5.B.2 / 3.5.B.3 / 3.5.B.4** The source depends on the business's age and legal form:

| Business stage | Typical sources |
|---|---|
| **New businesses** (typically **under two years**) | **Friends and family** loans, or **equity** sold to friends, family, or outside investors |
| **Established businesses** (typically **at least two years**, proven revenue and capacity to repay) | **Business bank loans** |
| **Corporations** | **Bonds** (a loan from an investor) or **shares of stock** (ownership), sold **privately or publicly** |

> **The CED's own cutoffs: under two years = new; at least two years with proven revenue and repayment capacity = established. Use those numbers, not a guess.**

---

## Bonds and stock — the corporation's two doors

**3.5.B.4**

| Instrument | What it is | The buyer becomes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bond** | **A loan from an investor to a business** | A **lender** — receives interest |
| **Stock** | **An ownership share** in the business | An **owner (shareholder)** — may receive dividends and capital gains |

Both can be sold **privately or publicly**. Only **corporations** can issue them.

> **A bond is not ownership, no matter how it is traded. The label on the instrument tells you which door was used.**

# Part 3: What the funder gets — and risks
### LO 3.5.C — Returns, risks, and rate of return

---

## Financial assets and the secondary market

**3.5.C.1** In exchange for capital, lenders and investors obtain **financial assets** — **loans (including bonds) or shares of stock**. These assets **can be resold in a secondary market**.

> **Why this matters:** the business already has the money. When you buy a share on the secondary market, your money goes to **another investor**, not to the company — but you still become an owner (3.5.C.2–C.3).

---

## Three ways a funder earns

| # | Way | Who earns it | EK |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Interest** | Lenders — loans and bonds pay interest | 3.5.C.2 |
| 2 | **Dividends** | Investors — a share of profits; some corporations reinvest instead of paying | 3.5.C.3 |
| 3 | **Capital gain** | Investors — selling an asset for more than they paid | 3.5.C.4 |

**3.5.C.2** notes the identity shift: *when a consumer buys a corporate bond in the secondary market, they become a lender to the business.* **3.5.C.3**: *when a consumer buys a share of stock in the secondary market, they become an investor (shareholder).*

> **A consumer can be a lender, an owner, or both — the instrument decides.**

---

## Prices move — that is where capital gains come from

**3.5.C.4** If an investor sells an asset at a **higher price** than they paid, they achieve a **capital gain**.

**The prices of financial assets such as bonds and stocks change in response to:**

| Driver | Example |
|---|---|
| **Business performance** | Rising profits lift the share price |
| **Investor demand** | More buyers than sellers |
| **PESTEL forces** | Interest rates, regulation, instability |

> **Capital gain is realised only when you sell. Until then it is a paper gain — and it can disappear.**

---

## The rate of return formula (3.5.C.5)

**Annual rate of return = (total dollars gained ÷ price of the asset) × 100**

where **total dollars gained = income (interest or dividends) + any capital gain.**

**Worked example — two funders of DK Coffee Lab:**

| Funder | Paid | Earned in one year | Rate of return |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lender** — $10,000 bond at 6% | $10,000 | $600 interest | 600 ÷ 10,000 = **6%** |
| **Investor** — 100 shares at $50 ($5,000) | $5,000 | $150 dividends + sold at $56 (+$600) | (150 + 600) ÷ 5,000 = **15%** |

> **Include *both* income and capital gain in the numerator. Forgetting the dividend is the most common calculation error.**

---

## The risks each funder carries (3.5.C.6)

**3.5.C.6** Lenders and investors risk financial loss if a business performs poorly — but **not the same loss**:

| Funder | Risk if the business performs poorly | Worst case |
|---|---|---|
| **Lender** | Business cannot make **interest payments or repay the loan** | Loss of the amount lent |
| **Investor** | **Lost dividends** and **decreased stock value** | **May lose the entire financial stake** if the business shuts down |

> **Equity is riskier than debt — and that is why equity expects a higher return.** This single sentence links 3.5.C.6 to 3.5.C.7 and to 3.5.D.3.

---

## Risk tolerance and the price of risk (3.5.C.7)

**3.5.C.7** **Risk tolerance** = willingness to take financial risks.

- Some consumers and institutional lenders/investors have **high risk tolerance** and will fund **risky or unproven ideas**
- Others are **more cautious**
- **Lenders and investors expect a higher rate of return when they invest in riskier assets**

> **The inverse direction is the exam point: risk tolerance is about the *funder's willingness*, expected return is about the *price they charge* for it. Funders do not "expect more because they are brave" — they demand compensation for risk.**

# Part 4: The pitch
### LO 3.5.D — Convincing a funder

---

## The business plan and the pitch (3.5.D.1)

**3.5.D.1** Lenders and investors often require a **business plan** that includes:

| Component | What it justifies |
|---|---|
| **Value proposition** | Why anyone would buy |
| **Market research** | Whether anyone actually will |
| **Marketing strategy** | How they will be reached |
| **Financial projections** | The specific **funding request**, **rate of return**, and **level of risk** |

The owner then develops a **polished pitch** summarizing this key information to **persuade** potential funders.

> **Plan = the full document. Pitch = the condensed persuasion. Same content, different length (3.5.D.1 vs. 3.5.D.1's second sentence).**

---

## Valuation — what a share is worth (3.5.D.2)

**3.5.D.2** When evaluating a request from an **established business**, lenders and investors rely on:

- the business's **financial reports and projections**
- **external industry data**

to estimate the **economic valuation** of the business.

**A valuation provides:**

| For whom | What it gives |
|---|---|
| **Prospective investors** | An estimate of what an **ownership share is worth** |
| **Lenders** | An assessment of the business's **capacity to repay a loan** |

> **Note the asymmetry: valuation serves owners (share price) and lenders (repayment capacity) for different reasons. Same number, two uses.**

---

## What makes a request fundable (3.5.D.3)

**3.5.D.3** A funding request is **more likely to be successful** when the business provides:

1. **Evidence of product-market fit**
2. **A basis for its financial projections**

**Lenders and investors typically prefer funding projects with:**

| Preference | Direction |
|---|---|
| **Lower risk** | ↓ |
| **Higher projected returns** | ↑ |

> **Product-market fit is evidence, not optimism. "Customers already buy it" beats "customers will love it."**

---

## The two soft factors (3.5.D.4)

**3.5.D.4** In evaluating a request, funders may also consider:

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Qualifications of the leadership team** | Can these people execute the plan? |
| **Alignment of value proposition and mission with the funders' goals** | Some funders will not fund certain products or sectors |

> **These are the "may also" items — real, but secondary to the numbers. In an FRQ, mention them *after* product-market fit and projections.**

---

## One page that settles 3.5

| Question the funder asks | EK | The answer they need |
|---|---|---|
| Is this a loan or a share? | 3.5.B.1 | Repayment with interest **or** ownership and control |
| Can this business repay? | 3.5.D.2 | Financial reports, projections, industry data → valuation |
| Will anyone buy the product? | 3.5.D.3 | **Product-market fit** |
| What return do I get? | 3.5.C.5 | (income + capital gain) ÷ price |
| What do I risk? | 3.5.C.6 | Debt: loss of lending. Equity: possibly the whole stake |
| Is the return worth the risk? | 3.5.C.7 | Higher risk demands a **higher expected return** |

---

## 🔑 Exam points to remember — Financial capital

| # | Point |
|---|-------|
| 1 | **Bootstrapping** = personal savings, personal bank loans, personal credit |
| 2 | Evaluate the need by comparing **personal funds to initial needs** and **calculating break-even volume** |
| 3 | Seek external capital when funds fall short of **startup + operating costs until break-even** |
| 4 | Established businesses raise capital to **develop products, replace fixed assets, and increase sales/revenue** |
| 5 | Both new and established businesses raise capital to **manage cash flow** — revenue is irregular, expenses are not |
| 6 | The two doors: **loans** (repaid with interest; interest is a business expense) and **equity** (ownership shares; cede control and future profit) |
| 7 | **New businesses (<2 years)** → friends and family, equity. **Established (≥2 years, proven revenue)** → bank loans |
| 8 | **Corporations** issue **bonds** (a loan) or **stock** (ownership), privately or publicly |
| 9 | **Financial assets can be resold in a secondary market**; buying there makes you a lender or shareholder |
| 10 | Funders earn **interest** (lenders), **dividends**, and **capital gains** (investors) |
| 11 | **Annual rate of return = (income + capital gain) ÷ price** |
| 12 | Lenders risk unpaid interest/principal; **equity investors may lose their entire stake** |
| 13 | **Riskier assets must offer a higher expected return** |
| 14 | A **business plan** (value proposition, market research, marketing, projections) supports a **pitch** |
| 15 | **Valuation** estimates share value (investors) and repayment capacity (lenders) |
| 16 | Fundable requests show **product-market fit** + a basis for projections; funders prefer **lower risk, higher projected return** |
| 17 | Funders may weigh the **leadership team** and **mission alignment** |

---

## Three exam tells — financial capital

**Tell 1 — "Lender or owner?"** Sort the funder first. Lender → interest, repayment obligation, loss limited to the amount lent. Owner → dividends, capital gain, control ceded, can lose the whole stake.

**Tell 2 — "Why raise now?"** New business → to start and reach break-even. Established → to develop, replace, or expand. Either → cash-flow timing. Match the reason to the stage.

**Tell 3 — "What makes the pitch work?"** Evidence beats enthusiasm: product-market fit, defensible projections, a credible team. An answer built only on "a great idea" is the weakest possible response.

## Practice — Stimulus

**DK Coffee Lab** needs **$13,250** beyond the owner's $5,000.

Two offers on the table:

- **Offer A — Bank loan:** $13,250 at **9%** for 5 years. The bank wants monthly financial statements.
- **Offer B — Angel investor:** $13,250 for **25% ownership** and a seat on decisions. No repayment schedule.

Last year's projections: annual revenue $210,000, net profit $18,000.

---

## Practice — Q1

> Which statement correctly classifies the two offers?

> A. A is equity financing; B is a loan from an investor
> B. A is a loan whose interest is a business expense; B is equity financing that cedes control and future profits
> C. Both are loans, because both involve outside money
> D. Both are equity, because both funders expect a return

---

## Practice — Q1 answer

**Answer: B.**

**3.5.B.1**: loans must be repaid with interest, and **interest on loans is a business expense** (3.5.B.1.i). **Equity financing** requires issuing ownership shares and **ceding some control and a portion of future profits** (3.5.B.1.ii). Offer B does exactly that.

**Traps:**
- **A** — reversed, and a bond-style "loan from an investor" is not what B describes (B takes ownership)
- **C** — the distinguishing feature is **repayment and interest vs. ownership**
- **D** — lenders do not receive equity

> **The two doors: repayment, or ownership.**

---

## Practice — Q2

> If the business earns $18,000 net profit and the angel holds 25%, what does the investor's stake pay in dividends (assuming all profit is distributed), and what rate of return is that on the $13,250 invested?

> A. $4,500 → about 34%
> B. $18,000 → about 136%
> C. $4,500 → about 3.4%
> D. $13,250 → about 100%

---

## Practice — Q2 answer

**Answer: A.**

Dividends = 25% × $18,000 = **$4,500**. Rate of return = $4,500 ÷ $13,250 ≈ **0.34 = 34%** (3.5.C.5 — income plus any capital gain, divided by the price paid).

**Traps:**
- **B** — uses the whole profit, not the investor's share
- **C** — decimal error (0.34 written as 3.4%)
- **D** — confuses the amount invested with the return

> **Rate of return = what you gained ÷ what you paid. Keep the denominator straight.**

---

## Practice — Q3

> DK Coffee Lab is 14 months old with growing revenue. Which source does 3.5.B most support it pursuing next?

> A. Equity sold to outside investors, since it is under two years old
> B. A business bank loan, once it shows proven revenue and capacity to repay
> C. A public stock issue, since it is a corporation
> D. Nothing — businesses under two years cannot obtain external capital

---

## Practice — Q3 answer

**Answer: B.**

**3.5.B.3** identifies **established businesses** — typically **at least two years** in operation **with proven revenue and capacity to repay** — as the ones that obtain **business bank loans**. At 14 months DK is still in the "new" category (3.5.B.2), so friends/family or equity remains the more realistic route *until* it crosses that threshold.

**Traps:**
- **A** — premature: DK is still in the new-business category and has not demonstrated the repayment capacity a bank requires
- **C** — only corporations can issue stock publicly, and a small café is not positioned to do so
- **D** — new businesses can and do obtain capital (friends, family, equity)

> **Age plus proven revenue is the CED's own test for the bank-loan door.**

---

## Practice — Q4

> DK Coffee Lab's pitch shows 40 existing wholesale customers and 12 months of sales data. Which element of a fundable request does this primarily provide?

> A. A valuation of the business
> B. Evidence of product-market fit and a basis for financial projections
> C. A guarantee that the business will not fail
> D. Alignment with the funder's mission

---

## Practice — Q4 answer

**Answer: B.**

**3.5.D.3**: a request is more likely to succeed when the business provides **evidence of product-market fit and a basis for its financial projections**. Existing paying customers are precisely that evidence.

**Traps:**
- **A** — valuation (3.5.D.2) comes from reports, projections, and industry data, not customers alone
- **C** — no evidence guarantees success; funders still price risk (3.5.C.7)
- **D** — mission alignment is a **"may also consider"** factor (3.5.D.4), not what customer evidence shows

> **Evidence, not optimism.**

---

## Trap patterns to watch

| # | Trap type | Where it appears |
|---|-----------|-----------------|
| 1 | **All outside money is debt** — equity is a separate door | Q1 |
| 2 | **Equity is free** — it costs control and future profits | Q1 |
| 3 | **Rate of return without income** — numerator needs income **and** capital gain | Q2 |
| 4 | **Denominator = amount raised** — it is the **price paid** for the asset | Q2 |
| 5 | **New business = bank loan** — banks want proven revenue and capacity to repay | Q3 |
| 6 | **Bond = ownership** — a bond is a loan; stock is ownership | Q3 |
| 7 | **Customers prove everything** — they support product-market fit, not valuation or guarantees | Q4 |
| 8 | **Profit vs. cash flow** — a profitable business can still need capital for timing (3.5.A.5) | throughout |

## Wrap-up — 3.5 Financial Capital

**What we covered:**
- **Bootstrapping** (personal savings, loans, credit) is the first source — it keeps control but limits speed
- Evaluate the need by **comparing personal funds to needs** and **calculating break-even volume**
- Seek external capital when funds fall short of **startup + operating costs until break-even**
- Established businesses raise capital to **develop products, replace fixed assets, and expand** — and both stages raise it to **manage cash flow**
- Two doors: **loans** (interest is a business expense, must be repaid) and **equity** (ownership, control, and future profit ceded)
- **New (<2 years)** → friends/family or equity; **established (≥2 years, proven revenue)** → bank loans; **corporations** → bonds or stock
- Funders earn **interest**, **dividends**, and **capital gains**; **rate of return = (income + capital gain) ÷ price**
- **Equity is riskier than debt** — investors can lose the entire stake, so riskier assets must promise **higher expected returns**
- A **business plan** supports a **pitch**; **valuation**, **product-market fit**, and a credible team decide the outcome
