# 1.7 Organization, Roles, and Responsibilities
### AP Business with Personal Finance · Unit 1

Lesson deck — **concepts + practice**

---

## Opening question

You know who the customer is (1.4), what the product is (1.1), what the world looks like (1.3), what the business stands for (1.5), and what the right thing to do is (1.6).

> **But who actually *owns* this thing — and who does what?**

Saturday morning, mid-setup, the cousin stops carrying coolers: **half the work should mean half the profit.** A handshake makes them partners. Twenty minutes later, a parent asks: *"if that cooler lid slams shut on somebody's phone, which of you pays?"*

Neither kid knows. **The handshake divided the profits and never mentioned the debts.**

---

## Today's goals

By the end of this lesson you can:

1. Compare the **four legal structures** and the tradeoffs each carries
2. Describe how **roles and responsibilities** are organized as a business grows
3. Explain when and why a business might **outsource** a function

**Running story continues:** the snack box is three weeks old, the cousin has joined, and a parent just asked a question that the handshake can't answer.

---

## The handshake scenario

Saturday morning. The cousin has been helping for two weeks — carrying coolers, loading vans, handing off boxes at the field.

This Saturday, the cousin stops mid-lift:

> *"Half the work means half the profit. We're partners now."*

A handshake. No paperwork. No filings. No written agreement.

**Twenty minutes later**, a parent picks up a snack box and the cooler lid slams down on their phone screen. The screen cracks. The parent turns to both kids:

> *"Which one of you pays for this?"*

---

## The question the handshake can't answer

The handshake **divided the profits.** It never mentioned the **debts.**

- If something breaks, who is liable?
- If the business owes money, whose pocket does it come from?
- If the business needs to grow, where does the money come from?

> **These are structure questions — and the handshake has no answers.** Today we name the four structures that do.

---

## The four legal structures — definition

Every business sits inside a **legal structure.** The structure decides three things: who **controls** the decisions, who **pays** the debts, and how the business **grows.**

| Structure | Owners | Control & profits | Liability | Funding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sole proprietorship** | 1 | full control, keeps every dollar | **personal** | limited |
| **Partnership** | 2+ | shared control/profits | **personal** | limited |
| **LLC** | owners | retain control, keep profits | **shielded** | limited |
| **Corporation** | shareholders | **ceded** to an elected board | **shielded** | greatest |

---

## The four legal structures — in the examples

Each structure maps onto one moment in the snack box story:

- **Sole proprietorship:** the snack box founder alone — five hats on a single Saturday, every dollar his, every risk his
- **Partnership:** the cousin joins, handshake agreement, no filings — shared control, shared profits, **shared personal liability**
- **LLC:** what the snack box *should* become after the phone-screen question — owners keep control, but the business's debts belong to the business
- **Corporation:** if they needed to sell shares for a 40-league expansion — cede control to shareholders and a board, gain the most funding

> **The structure fits the stage.** One kid, one Saturday = sole prop. Two kids, one handshake = partnership. The phone-screen question = time for an LLC.

---

## Sole proprietorship — definition

A **sole proprietorship** is a business owned and run by **one person.**

- The owner has **full control** over every decision
- The owner **keeps all the profits**
- The owner is **personally liable** for all business debts
- Funding comes from the owner's **own savings and loans**

> **Simplest structure.** No filings, no partners, no board. One person, one business.

---

## Sole proprietorship — in the examples

The snack box founder on the first Saturday:

- **One owner** — nobody else has a claim
- **Full control** — he decides the menu, the price, the route
- **Keeps every dollar** — profit = revenue minus cost, all his
- **Personal liability** — if the cooler lid breaks a phone, **he pays from his own pocket**
- **Limited funding** — growth depends on what he can save or borrow

> **Five hats, one head.** CEO, marketer, product developer, financial manager, operations manager — all on the same Saturday.

---

## Partnership — definition

A **partnership** is a business owned by **two or more people** who share control and profits.

- Owners **share control** and **share profits** — usually by agreement
- All partners are **personally liable** for business debts — **even debts caused by the other partner**
- Funding is still **limited** — comes from the partners' savings and loans
- No legal filings required to form (in most cases) — **a handshake can do it**

> **The exam's favorite trap:** liability is **shared**, not **divided by who did the work.** If the cousin drops the cooler, the founder still pays.

---

## Partnership — in the examples

The cousin joins the snack box:

- **Two owners** — handshake agreement, no paperwork
- **Shared control** — the cousin handles logistics, the founder handles the product
- **Shared profits** — they split the cash box at the end of the day
- **Personal liability** — if the cooler lid breaks a phone, **both** pay, regardless of whose hand was on the lid
- **Limited funding** — still just two kids' savings

> **The handshake divided the profits. It never mentioned the debts.** Both are personally liable — the exam tests this exact point.

---

## LLC — definition

A **limited liability company (LLC)** is a business structure where owners **retain control** and keep profits, but their **personal assets are shielded** from business debts.

- Owners **keep control** — no board, no shareholders
- **Liability is shielded** — business debts belong to the business, not to the owners personally
- Funding is still **limited** — no shares to sell
- Requires **legal filings** to form

> **The LLC draws a wall between the business and the owner's personal money.** The phone-screen question stops here.

---

## LLC — in the examples

The phone-screen question pushes the snack box toward an LLC:

- **Same two owners** — but now with a legal filing
- **Same control** — they still make every decision together
- **Shielded liability** — if the cooler lid breaks a phone, the **business** pays, not their personal savings
- **Limited funding** — they can't sell shares, so growth still depends on savings and loans

> **The LLC answers the parent's question:** "Which one of you pays?" → **The business does.** That's the wall the filing builds.

---

## Corporation — definition

A **corporation** is a business owned by **shareholders** who elect a **board of directors** to make major decisions.

- Owners **cede control** to shareholders and an elected board
- **Liability is shielded** — same wall as the LLC
- **Greatest access to funding** — the corporation can **sell shares** to raise money
- The most **complex and expensive** structure to form and maintain

> **The trade:** you give up control, and in exchange you get the most funding and the strongest liability shield.

---

## Corporation — in the examples

If the snack box needed to expand to 40 leagues at once:

- **Shareholders** buy in — each one owns a piece of the business
- **A board is elected** — the founder no longer makes every call alone
- **Selling shares** raises the money for 40 new vans and 40 new cooler sets
- **Shielded liability** — same wall as the LLC

> **The founder traded control for scale.** He can't decide the menu alone anymore — but the business can reach leagues he could never fund by himself.

---

## Tradeoff line 1 — control vs. funding

> **Control and funding pull in opposite directions.**

Owners of a **sole proprietorship, partnership, or LLC** **retain control** over decision making and keep the profits — but the price is that **growth may be limited**, because these structures have **less access to funding.**

A **corporation flips the trade:** its owners **cede control** to shareholders and an elected board, and in exchange the corporation gains **greater access to funding and room to grow**, with the company itself controlling the profits.

---

## Tradeoff line 1 — in the examples

The snack box faces this trade at every growth decision:

| Stage | Structure | Control | Funding |
|---|---|---|---|
| One kid, one field | Sole proprietorship | Total | His savings |
| Two kids, handshake | Partnership | Shared between two | Two savings pools |
| After the phone-screen filing | LLC | Same two owners | Same limited pool |
| 40-league expansion | Corporation | Board + shareholders | Selling shares |

> **The more control you keep, the harder it is to raise money.** The snack box can stay small and stay in charge — or grow big and share the wheel.

---

## Tradeoff line 2 — liability — definition

> **Who pays when something goes wrong?**

**Sole proprietors and partners** are **personally liable** for all business debts — a broken screen, an unpaid loan, or a lawsuit can reach into **their own pockets.**

Organizing as an **LLC** lets owners **avoid that personal exposure** — the business's debts belong to the business. A **corporation** draws the same wall.

| | Personally liable | Liability shielded |
|---|---|---|
| Structures | Sole proprietorship, Partnership | LLC, Corporation |

---

## Tradeoff line 2 — liability — in the examples

The phone-screen question is the test:

- **Sole proprietorship:** the founder pays from his own savings
- **Partnership:** both the founder and the cousin pay — **regardless of whose hand was on the lid**
- **LLC:** the business pays — the owners' personal savings are untouched
- **Corporation:** same wall — the business pays

> **The parent's question — "which one of you pays?" — has a different answer in every structure.** That's why the structure matters.

---

## The 3-question identification method

> Every identification question in this topic surrenders to these three questions, in order:

1. **How many owners** — one or several?
2. **Who pays the business's debts** — the owners personally, or the business alone?
3. **How does growth get funded** — from savings and loans, or by selling shares?

| Answer pattern | Structure |
|---|---|
| One owner, personal liability, self-funded | **Sole proprietorship** |
| Several owners, personal liability | **Partnership** |
| Owners shielded, no shareholders/board | **LLC** |
| Shareholders, a board, stock funding | **Corporation** |

---

## The 3-question method — in the examples

Run the snack box through the three questions at each stage:

**Stage 1 — one kid, one Saturday:**
> How many owners? **One.** Who pays debts? **He does — personally.** How funded? **His savings.** → **Sole proprietorship.**

**Stage 2 — the cousin joins:**
> How many owners? **Two.** Who pays debts? **Both — personally.** How funded? **Their savings.** → **Partnership.**

**Stage 3 — after the filing:**
> How many owners? **Two.** Who pays debts? **The business — liability shielded.** How funded? **Still their savings.** → **LLC.**

**Stage 4 — 40-league expansion:**
> How many owners? **Shareholders.** Who pays debts? **The business — liability shielded.** How funded? **Selling shares.** → **Corporation.**

---

## Exam tip — start with question 1

> **Start with the first question (how many owners?) and you can eliminate half the options immediately.**

- One owner → sole proprietorship or (rarely) an LLC with one member
- Two or more owners, personal liability → partnership
- Two or more owners, shielded, no board → LLC
- Shareholders and a board → corporation

> **The first question is the fastest filter.** Use it before reading the full scenario.

# Roles and responsibilities

**Structure says who *owns* the business. Roles say who *does the work.***

---

## One owner, every hat — definition

In a sole proprietorship, one person does all of it — **five roles at once:**

| Role | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Chief executive officer** | Setting the direction, making the big calls |
| **Marketer** | Getting the word out, building the brand |
| **Product developer** | Designing and improving what's sold |
| **Financial manager** | Counting the money, tracking costs |
| **Operations manager** | Running the day-to-day — logistics, delivery, execution |

> The hats **compete for hours** — a Saturday spent under the operations hat leaves the marketing hat unworn all week.

---

## One owner, every hat — in the examples

The snack box founder on a single Saturday:

| Role | What he does |
|---|---|
| **CEO** | Decides to add fruit cups after the families' feedback |
| **Marketer** | Posts the new menu in the league group chat |
| **Product developer** | Tests the fruit cup recipe on Friday night |
| **Financial manager** | Counts the cash box, calculates the weekend profit |
| **Operations manager** | Loads the cooler, drives to the field, hands off at halftime |

> **Five hats, one head.** Every minute under one hat is a minute stolen from another. That's why partners matter.

---

## Partners divide the roles — definition

**Partners** carry the same full responsibility, but typically **divide the roles according to each partner's strengths and interests.**

- One partner might handle **operations and logistics** — routes, schedules, handoffs
- The other might handle **product, pricing, and money** — the menu, the cash box, the group chat
- The division isn't about hierarchy — it's about **fit**

> **A partnership where both partners fight over the same hat wastes one of its two advantages.**

---

## Partners divide the roles — in the examples

The cousin joins the snack box:

| Partner | Strengths | Hats worn |
|---|---|---|
| **The founder** | Product design, pricing, customer relationships | CEO, product developer, financial manager, marketer |
| **The cousin** | Logistics, scheduling, physical delivery | Operations manager |

> The cousin loves logistics — routes, coolers, handoffs. The founder keeps the product, the pricing, and the money. **Each partner wears the hats that fit.**

> **The exam tests this as "dividing roles by strengths and interests" — not by seniority or ownership percentage.**

---

## Growing into departments — definition

As a business grows in size and complexity, many businesses organize roles into **specialized departments.**

- **Executive leaders** (e.g., a CEO) are responsible for the **overall vision, operations, strategy, and performance**
- **Managers** lead the specialized departments and **report to** those executives
- In a **corporation**, the executive leaders themselves **report upward** to the **board of directors and the shareholders**

> **Authority flows down the chart; accountability flows right back up.**

---

## Growing into departments — in the examples

The snack box at 40-league scale:

- The **founder becomes CEO** — setting direction, answering to shareholders
- **Department managers** report to the CEO — each one owns a piece of the operation
- The **board of directors** holds the CEO accountable for performance

> **Specialization is the payoff:** departments develop **deep expertise**, which improves the business's ability to meet customer needs **efficiently and effectively.**

---

## Organizational structure — definition

**Organizational structure** = the arrangement of roles, responsibility, authority, and communication. Drawing it as a chart (an **org chart**) makes the reporting lines readable at a glance.

Watch one decision travel the chart:

1. An **operations manager** pitches opening league 41
2. The **CEO** weighs it against strategy and says yes
3. At the next **board meeting** the CEO answers to the **directors and shareholders** for how the expansion performed

> **Authority flows down; accountability flows up.** That's the structure doing its job.

---

## Organizational structure — in the examples

The snack box at league 41:

- **Operations manager:** "League 41 needs a team. Here's the cost."
- **CEO (the founder):** "Does it fit the expansion plan? Can we staff it?"
- **Board meeting:** "How did league 41 perform against the budget?"

> **The org chart isn't a drawing — it's the path every decision follows.** Authority goes down, accountability comes back up.

---

## The six departments — definition

| Department | Function |
|---|---|
| **Sales and marketing** | Market research, sales strategies, brand management, customer relationships |
| **Research and development (R&D)** | Innovates around new goods, services, and processes |
| **Operations** | Making the goods or developing the services and getting products to customers |
| **Accounting** | Tracks expenditures and earnings, prepares financial statements |
| **Finance** | Secures and manages funds, recommends strategies for improving performance |
| **Human resources** | Recruits, trains, and oversees evaluation of employees |

---

## The six departments — in the examples

Six departments, one product (a snack box at 40-league scale):

- **R&D** invents and tests new snack combinations
- **Sales and marketing** surveys families, prices the boxes, and builds the brand across leagues
- **Operations** produces thousands of boxes and routes them to every field
- **Accounting** records what the launch spends and earns
- **Finance** decides the launch budget and reads the early numbers
- **Human resources** hires and trains the extra Saturday staff

> **Six departments, one product.** A question about any single desk is answered by **naming that desk's function.**

---

## The swap pair: Accounting vs. Finance — definition

> **Accounting and Finance are the swap pair the exam loves.**

| | Accounting | Finance |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Records and reports money that **already moved** | Works **forward** |
| What it does | Tracks, prepares statements, monitors health | Secures funds, manages them, recommends what's next |
| Looks | **Backward** | **Forward** |

> **Records look backward, funding looks forward.** Pin this difference now.

---

## The swap pair: Accounting vs. Finance — in the examples

The snack box cash box:

- **Accounting** = the cash box itself — every dollar in, every dollar out, the weekend totals on a sheet of paper. **Looking backward** at what already happened.
- **Finance** = the decision to fund league 41 — do we have enough cash? Should we borrow? Is the return worth it? **Looking forward** at what should happen next.

> A scenario about **preparing statements** belongs to **accounting** — **even when the word "finance" appears in it.**

> **The cash box is accounting. The decision to spend from it is finance.**

---

## Accounting vs. Finance — the exam trick

The exam writes scenarios that **use the word "financial"** to pull you toward the wrong answer:

- *"The department that prepares the financial statements"* → **Accounting** (backward — records)
- *"The department that secures funds for expansion"* → **Finance** (forward — funding)

> **"Financial" is unreliable as a signal.** The direction test works every time: **backward = accounting, forward = finance.**

---

# Outsourcing

**Buy the function, or build it.**

---

## Outsourcing — definition

**Outsourcing** = hiring another business to handle a function **instead of doing it internally.**

A business may outsource a function when doing so is likely to **increase efficiency or reduce costs.**

**Classic triggers:**

1. The business **lacks employees with the specific skills**
2. Existing **labor costs run high**

> **The outsourcing decision in one sentence: "buy the function, or build it."**

---

## Outsourcing — in the examples

The snack box meets the first trigger:

- Pre-orders outgrow the group chat — families want a real ordering system
- Neither the founder nor the cousin can code
- They pay someone to build a simple order page

> **Hiring the skill for a fee beat learning it for a season.** The function moved outside because the skill didn't exist inside.

---

## Outsourcing — the second trigger

A business might also outsource when **labor costs run high:**

- A company in a high-wage city hires an outside firm to run customer support from a lower-cost region
- A manufacturer contracts packaging to a third party because building a packaging line is too expensive

> **Two triggers, same decision:** the function moves outside when **building it in-house costs more** — in money, time, or both.

---

## Outsourcing vs. core competency

> **Outsourcing is the opposite of developing a core competency.**

| | Core competency | Outsourcing |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Develop a function **in-house** | Move a function **outside** |
| Why | The function is a source of **competitive advantage** | The function is a **cost to manage** |
| Example | The snack box's halftime handoff (a skill they built) | The order page (a skill they bought) |

> **If a function is your edge, keep it. If a function is a cost, buy it.** The exam tests this distinction.

## Discussion scenario

A small bakery makes everything in-house. Demand for wedding cakes is growing, but the bakery has no one trained in cake decorating. The owner considers two options:

1. **Hire and train** a cake decorator (build the skill)
2. **Contract** a freelance cake decorator for each order (buy the skill)

> **Pair up. 2 minutes.**

1. What is the outsourcing decision here — buy or build?
2. What are the tradeoffs of each option?
3. Which option would you recommend, and why?

---

## Discussion — key points

**Build (hire and train):**
- The skill stays in-house — becomes a potential **core competency**
- Higher upfront cost (salary, training time)
- Long-term control over quality

**Buy (contract):**
- Lower upfront cost — pay per order
- The skill stays **outside** — no core competency built
- Less control over quality and availability

> **The outsourcing decision depends on whether the function is a cost to manage or an edge to build.** Wedding cakes might be the bakery's future — or just a seasonal spike.

---

## Exam points to remember

- **3-question method:** owners? debts? funding? → run it every time
- **Control and funding** pull in opposite directions
- **Liability:** sole prop + partnership = personal; LLC + corporation = shielded
- **Partnership liability is shared** — not divided by who did the work
- **One owner = five hats;** partners divide by **strengths and interests**
- **Departments:** six types; **accounting = backward, finance = forward**
- **"Financial" is unreliable** — use the direction test
- **Outsourcing:** buy vs. build; triggers = missing skills or high costs
- **Outsourcing ≠ core competency** — one moves out, one develops in

# Practice — 6 questions

**4 on one stimulus (Tumble Route)** · **2 standalone**

Read → pause → **commit to an answer** → then reveal.

---

## Stimulus: Tumble Route

**Aiko Mori** and **Jordan Lee** started **Tumble Route**, a laundry pickup-and-delivery service, with a **spoken agreement and no legal filings.** Aiko manages the routes, the machines, and the schedule. Jordan handles pricing, advertising, and the accounts.

Last month a customer's **designer jacket was ruined** in a machine. The customer demands **$800.** The partners are weighing two options:

1. **Reorganizing as an LLC**
2. **Incorporating** so they can **sell shares** to finance **20 new vans**

> **Watch for the artifacts:** a spoken partnership, a liability event, and two structure-change options.

---

## Q1 — Liability for the ruined jacket

Aiko and Jordan formed Tumble Route with a **spoken agreement and no legal filings.** A customer's jacket was ruined. Which best describes who is **liable** for the $800 claim?

- A. Only Aiko, because she manages the machines
- B. Only Jordan, because he handles the accounts
- C. The business alone — neither Aiko nor Jordan is personally liable
- D. Both Aiko and Jordan are **personally liable**

<!--
Pause. Ask: is this a sole proprietorship, a partnership, an LLC, or a corporation? What does the structure say about liability?
-->

---

## Q1 — Answer: **D**

**Why:** A spoken agreement with no legal filings = a **partnership.** In a partnership, **all partners are personally liable** for business debts — regardless of whose area the damage occurred in.

**Trap: A** — the exam's favorite partnership trap. Liability is **shared**, not **divided by who manages the machines.** Aiko runs the machines, but Jordan is still liable.

**Trap: B** — same trap in reverse. Jordan handles accounts, but the jacket wasn't damaged by accounting. **It doesn't matter.** Both partners are liable.

**Trap: C** — borrows the **LLC/corporation rule.** No filings = no liability shield. The business **is** the partners.

> **Partnership liability is shared and personal — not divided by who did the work.**

---

## Q2 — Classify the role split

Aiko manages routes, machines, and the schedule. Jordan handles pricing, advertising, and accounts. This arrangement best illustrates which concept?

- A. Partners dividing roles by strengths and interests
- B. Departments forming within an organizational structure
- C. Outsourcing specialized functions to external firms
- D. A corporation electing a board of directors

---

## Q2 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** Two partners with a spoken agreement, each handling the areas that match their skills — logistics for Aiko, business operations for Jordan. This is partners **dividing roles by strengths and interests.**

**Trap: B** — departments form when a business grows large enough to need **specialized teams with managers.** Two partners splitting work is not departments — **scale is the tell.** If the scenario said "Tumble Route has 50 employees organized into teams," then departments would apply.

> **Partners dividing roles ≠ departments.** The difference is scale and specialization.

---

## Q3 — Tradeoff of incorporating

If Tumble Route **incorporates** and sells shares to finance 20 new vans, which best describes the **tradeoff**?

- A. The business loses its liability shield but gains funding
- B. The partners gain more personal control over daily decisions
- C. The owners **cede control** to shareholders and a board in exchange for **greater access to funding**
- D. The business can no longer grow because shareholders limit decision making

---

## Q3 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** Incorporating means selling shares — shareholders elect a board, and the founders **cede control** over major decisions. In exchange, the corporation gains the **greatest access to funding** — exactly what 20 new vans require.

**Trap: A** — flips the liability direction. Incorporating **keeps** the liability shield (same as LLC). The trade is control for funding, not liability for funding.

**Trap: D** — reverses the relationship. Shareholders **enable** growth by providing funding — they don't limit it. The limitation is on the **founders' control**, not on the business's ability to grow.

> **Control and funding pull in opposite directions.** Incorporating shrinks personal liability, cedes control, and unlocks funding.

---

## Q4 — Identify the department

At a mid-size company, one department **tracks the year's expenditures and earnings and prepares the financial statements** that management reviews each quarter. Which department is this?

- A. Finance
- B. Accounting
- C. Operations
- D. Research and development

---

## Q4 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Accounting tracks expenditures and earnings and prepares financial statements — the question restates that job almost word for word.

> **Trap: A.** Finance secures and manages funds and recommends strategies **going forward.** Preparing statements about money that **already moved** is accounting — **even when the word "financial" appears.**

> **Records look backward, funding looks forward.** "Financial" is not a reliable signal. The direction test is.

## Q5 — Identify the structure from a data table

A student studies three businesses and records the following data:

| Feature | Business X | Business Y | Business Z |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of owners | 1 | 3 | Shareholders |
| Liability | Personal | Shielded | Shielded |
| Funding source | Owner's savings | Partners' savings | Stock sales |
| Decision maker | Owner alone | Owners together | Board of directors |

Based on the table, which row of structures is **correct**?

- A. X = Partnership, Y = LLC, Z = Sole proprietorship
- B. X = LLC, Y = Corporation, Z = Partnership
- C. X = Sole proprietorship, Y = Partnership, Z = Corporation
- D. X = Sole proprietorship, Y = LLC, Z = Corporation

---

## Q5 — Answer: **D**

**Run the three questions for each business:**

**Business X:** 1 owner + personal liability + self-funded → **Sole proprietorship.**
**Business Y:** 3 owners + shielded liability + partners' savings (no shareholders) → **LLC.**
**Business Z:** Shareholders + shielded liability + stock sales + board → **Corporation.**

**Trap: C** — gets X right but swaps Y and Z. Business Y has **shielded liability** — that eliminates partnership (personal liability). **Check every feature in every row.**

> **ID tables: check every feature in every row.** One wrong column flips the whole answer.

## Q6 — Outsourcing support due to high wages

A software company **hires an outside firm** to run its customer-support phone lines because **support wages in its city are unusually high.** Which best describes this decision?

- A. The company has made customer support one of its core competencies
- B. The company has converted the support staff into internal stakeholders
- C. The company is outsourcing a function because doing so is likely to **reduce costs**
- D. The company has reorganized as a corporation

---

## Q6 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** Outsourcing = hiring another business to handle a function. The two classic triggers are **missing skills** and **high labor costs** — high support wages are the second trigger.

**Trap: A.** Core competencies are capabilities a business **develops itself.** Handing a function to an outside firm is **the opposite move** — the company has decided support is a **cost to manage**, so the function moves outside.

> **Outsourcing ≠ core competency.** One moves a function *out*; the other develops one *in-house.*

## Wrap-up

- The **structure** you choose decides three things: who **controls** the decisions, who **pays** the debts, and how the business **grows**
- **3-question method:** owners? debts? funding? → run it every time
- **Control and funding** pull in opposite directions
- **Liability:** sole prop + partnership = personal; LLC + corporation = shielded
- **One owner = five hats;** partners divide by **strengths and interests**
- **Departments** specialize as the business grows; **accounting = backward, finance = forward**
- **Outsourcing** = buy the function or build it; triggers = missing skills or high costs

> **Get the structure wrong and the org chart is just a drawing.**
