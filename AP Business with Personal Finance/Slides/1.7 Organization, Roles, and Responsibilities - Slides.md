# 1.7 Organization, Roles, and Responsibilities
### AP Business with Personal Finance · Unit 1

Lesson deck — **concepts + practice**

---

## Opening question

You know who the customer is (1.4), what the product is (1.1), what the world looks like (1.3), what the business stands for (1.5), and what the right thing to do is (1.6).

> **But who actually *owns* this thing — and who does what?**

Three weeks in, the cousin stops carrying coolers mid-lift: **half the work should mean half the profit.** A handshake makes them partners. Twenty minutes later a cooler lid slams down on a parent's phone screen, and the parent turns to both kids: *"which one of you pays for this?"*

**The handshake divided the profits and never mentioned the debts.**

---

## Today's goals

By the end of this lesson you can:

1. Compare the **four legal structures** and the tradeoffs each carries
2. Describe how **roles and responsibilities** are organized as a business grows
3. Explain when and why a business might **outsource** a function

**Running story continues:** the snack box is three weeks old, the cousin has joined, and a parent just asked a question the handshake can't answer.

---

## The four legal structures

Every business sits inside a **legal structure**, and the structure decides three things: who **controls** the decisions, who **pays** the debts, and how the business **grows.**

| Structure | Owners | Control and profits | Liability | Funding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sole proprietorship** | 1 | full control, keeps every dollar | **personal** | limited |
| **Partnership** | 2+ | shared control, shared profits | **personal** | limited |
| **LLC** | owners | retain control, keep profits | **shielded** | limited |
| **Corporation** | shareholders | **ceded** to an elected board | **shielded** | greatest |

> **The structure fits the stage.** One kid, one Saturday = sole proprietorship. Two kids, one handshake = partnership. The cracked phone = time for an LLC. A 40-league expansion = a corporation.

---

## Sole proprietorship

**Sole proprietorship** = a business owned and run by **one person.**

- **Full control** over every decision, and the owner **keeps all the profits**
- **Personally liable** for every business debt — the cracked phone is paid for out of pocket
- Funding comes from **the owner's own savings and loans**; no filings, no partners, no board

> **The simplest structure, and the most exposed one.** No filings, no partners, no board — but also no wall: one person's savings answer for every debt, and one person's hours cover every job.

---

## Partnership

**Partnership** = a business owned by **two or more people** who share control and profits.

- Owners **share control** and **share profits**, usually by agreement
- All partners are **personally liable** for business debts — **even debts caused by the other partner**
- Funding is still **limited** to the partners' savings and loans
- No legal filings are required in most cases — **a handshake can do it**

> **The exam's favorite trap:** liability is **shared**, never **divided by who did the work.** If the cousin drops the cooler, the founder still pays.

---

## LLC

**Limited liability company (LLC)** = a structure in which owners **retain control** and keep the profits, while their **personal assets are shielded** from business debts.

- Owners **keep control** — no board, no shareholders
- **Liability is shielded** — the business's debts belong to the business
- Funding is still **limited**, because there are no shares to sell
- **Legal filings** are required to form it

> **The LLC answers the parent's question.** "Which one of you pays?" → **the business does.** The filing draws a wall between the business and the owner's savings.

---

## Corporation

**Corporation** = a business owned by **shareholders** who elect a **board of directors** to make the major decisions.

- Owners **cede control** to the shareholders and the elected board
- **Liability is shielded** — the same wall as the LLC
- **Greatest access to funding** — the corporation can **sell shares**
- The most **complex and expensive** structure to form and maintain

> **The trade:** give up control, gain funding and the strongest liability shield. The founder no longer decides the menu alone — but the business can reach leagues he could never have funded by himself.

---

## The two tradeoffs, in one table

| Stage in the story | Structure | Control | Funding | Liability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One kid, one field | Sole proprietorship | Total | His savings | Personal |
| Two kids, handshake | Partnership | Shared between two | Two savings pools | Personal |
| After the phone-screen filing | LLC | Same two owners | Same limited pool | Shielded |
| 40-league expansion | Corporation | Board and shareholders | Selling shares | Shielded |

> **Control and funding pull in opposite directions.** Keep control and growth is capped by what you can save or borrow; sell shares and the money arrives with a board attached.

---

## The 3-question identification method

1. **How many owners** — one or several?
2. **Who pays the business's debts** — the owners personally, or the business alone?
3. **How does growth get funded** — savings and loans, or selling shares?

| What the scenario says | Structure |
|---|---|
| One owner, personal liability, self-funded | **Sole proprietorship** |
| Several owners, personal liability | **Partnership** |
| Shielded liability, no shareholders, no board | **LLC** |
| Shareholders, a board, stock funding | **Corporation** |

> **Start with question 1 and eliminate.** One owner wipes out partnership and corporation on the spot — the owner count is the fastest filter in the topic.

# Roles and responsibilities

**Structure says who *owns* the business. Roles say who *does the work*.**

---

## One owner, every hat

| The hat the owner wears | What it means | At the snack box |
|---|---|---|
| **Chief executive officer** | Sets the direction, makes the big calls | Adds fruit cups after the families' feedback |
| **Marketer** | Gets the word out, builds the brand | Posts the new menu in the league group chat |
| **Product developer** | Designs and improves what is sold | Tests the fruit cup recipe on Friday night |
| **Financial manager** | Counts the money, tracks the costs | Works out the weekend profit from the cash box |
| **Operations manager** | Runs the day-to-day: logistics, delivery | Loads the cooler, drives to the field, hands off |

> **Five hats, one head** — the hats compete for the same hours. That pressure is what brings a partner in, and later a department.

---

## Partners divide the roles

Partners carry the same full responsibility, but they **divide the roles according to strengths and interests.**

| Partner | Strengths | Hats worn |
|---|---|---|
| **The founder** | Product design, pricing, customer relationships | CEO, product developer, financial manager, marketer |
| **The cousin** | Logistics, scheduling, physical delivery | Operations manager |

> **The division is about fit, not hierarchy.** The exam states it as "strengths and interests" — not seniority, not ownership share, and not who worked more hours.

---

## Growing into departments

As a business grows in **size and complexity**, it organizes roles into **specialized departments.**

- **Executive leaders** (a CEO, for example) are responsible for the overall **vision, operations, strategy and performance**
- **Managers** lead the specialized departments and **report to** those executives
- In a **corporation**, the executive leaders themselves report upward to the **board of directors and the shareholders**

At 40-league scale the founder becomes CEO and answers to shareholders, each department manager owns a slice of the operation, and the board holds the CEO accountable for performance.

> **Specialization is the payoff:** departments build **deep expertise**, which improves the business's ability to meet customer needs efficiently and effectively.

🎬 **Case clip** — Meiyijia: how a 30,000-store convenience chain is organized · https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1sWMuzTEGb/
> *A real chain to sort across the desks: who picks the range, who sells it, who stocks 30,000 shelves, who keeps the books, who funds the next store, who hires the staff.*

---

## Organizational structure

**Organizational structure** = the arrangement of **roles, responsibility, authority and communication.** Drawing it as an **org chart** makes the reporting lines readable at a glance.

Watch one decision travel the chart:

1. An **operations manager** pitches opening league 41
2. The **CEO** weighs it against the expansion plan and says yes
3. At the next **board meeting** the CEO answers to the directors and shareholders for how league 41 performed

> **Authority flows down the chart; accountability flows back up.** The org chart isn't a drawing — it is the path every decision follows.

## The six departments

| Department | Function |
|---|---|
| **Sales and marketing** | Market research, sales strategies, brand management, customer relationships |
| **Research and development (R&D)** | Innovates around new goods, services and processes |
| **Operations** | Making the goods or developing the services and getting products to customers |
| **Accounting** | Tracks expenditures and earnings, prepares financial statements |
| **Finance** | Secures and manages funds, recommends strategies for improving performance |
| **Human resources** | Recruits, trains and oversees the evaluation of employees |

> **Six departments, one product.** R&D invents the snack, marketing prices it and runs the brand across the leagues, operations builds and routes ten thousand boxes, accounting records the weekend, finance sets the league 41 budget, HR hires the Saturday staff.

---

## The swap pair: Accounting vs. Finance

| | Accounting | Finance |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Records money that **already moved** | Works **forward** on money not yet spent |
| What it does | Tracks, prepares statements, monitors financial health | Secures funds, manages them, recommends what comes next |

- *"The department that prepares the financial statements"* → **Accounting** — backward, records
- *"The department that secures funds for expansion"* → **Finance** — forward, funding

> **"Financial" is not a reliable signal; the direction test is.** The cash box is accounting. The decision to spend from it is finance.

# Outsourcing

**Buy the function, or build it.**

---

## Outsourcing — buy the function or build it

**Outsourcing** = hiring another business to handle a function **instead of doing it internally**, when that is likely to increase efficiency or reduce costs.

**Trigger 1 — the skills are missing.** Pre-orders outgrow the group chat, nobody at the snack box can code, so the kids pay for a simple order page. Hiring the skill for a fee beat learning it for a season.

**Trigger 2 — the labor costs run high.** A company in a high-wage city buys customer-support hours from a lower-cost region; a manufacturer contracts packaging out because building a packaging line costs too much.

> **Two triggers, one decision:** the function moves outside whenever building it in-house costs more — in money, time, or both.

---

## Outsourcing vs. core competency

> **Outsourcing is the opposite of developing a core competency.**

| | Core competency | Outsourcing |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Develop a function **in-house** | Move a function **outside** |
| Why | The function is a source of **competitive advantage** | The function is a **cost to manage** |
| The snack box | The halftime handoff — a skill they built | The order page — a skill they bought |

> **If a function is your edge, keep it. If it is a cost, buy it.**

---

## Your turn — the bakery decision

A small bakery makes everything in-house. Demand for wedding cakes is growing, but nobody there is trained in cake decorating. The owner is choosing between:

1. **Hire and train** a cake decorator — build the skill
2. **Contract** a freelance decorator for each order — buy the skill

> 🤔 **Pair up, 2 minutes.** Which is the outsourcing decision, what does each option trade off, and which would you recommend?

---

## Bakery — the two answers

| | Build (hire and train) | Buy (contract per order) |
|---|---|---|
| Skill | Stays in-house, becomes a potential **core competency** | Stays outside, **no competency built** |
| Cost | Higher up front: salary plus training time | Lower up front: pay per order |
| Quality and availability | Controlled | Not controlled |

> **The answer depends on whether decorating is the bakery's edge or just a seasonal cost.** Wedding cakes might be the business it wants to become — or a spike it can rent.

# Practice — 6 questions

**4 on one stimulus (Tumble Route)** · **2 standalone**

Read → pause → **commit to an answer** → then reveal.

---

## Stimulus: Tumble Route

**Aiko Mori** and **Jordan Lee** started **Tumble Route**, a laundry pickup-and-delivery service, with a **spoken agreement and no legal filings.** Aiko manages the routes, the machines and the schedule; Jordan handles pricing, advertising and the accounts.

Last month a customer's **designer jacket was ruined** in a machine, and the customer demands **$800.** The partners are weighing two options: **reorganizing as an LLC**, or **incorporating** so they can **sell shares** to finance **20 new vans.**

> **Watch for the artifacts:** a spoken partnership, a liability event, and two structure-change options.

---

## Q1 — Liability for the ruined jacket

Which best describes who is **liable** for the customer's $800 claim?

- A. Only Aiko, because she manages the machines
- B. Only Jordan, because he handles the accounts
- C. The business alone — neither partner is personally liable
- D. Both Aiko and Jordan are **personally liable**

---

## Q1 — Answer: **D**

**Why:** A spoken agreement with no legal filings is a **partnership**, and in a partnership **all partners are personally liable** for business debts — regardless of whose area the damage happened in.

**Trap: A and B** — the exam's favorite partnership trap, in both directions. Liability is **shared**, not divided by who manages the machines or who took the order.

**Trap: C** — borrows the **LLC/corporation rule.** No filings, no liability shield: the business **is** the partners.

---

## Q2 — Classify the role split

Aiko manages routes, machines and schedule. Jordan handles pricing, advertising and accounts. This arrangement best illustrates which concept?

- A. Partners dividing roles by strengths and interests
- B. Departments forming within an organizational structure
- C. Outsourcing specialized functions to external firms
- D. A corporation electing a board of directors

---

## Q2 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** Two partners with a spoken agreement, each holding the areas that match their skills — logistics for Aiko, business operations for Jordan. That is partners **dividing roles by strengths and interests.**

**Trap: B** — departments form when a business is large enough to need **specialized teams with managers.** Two partners splitting work is not departments: **scale is the tell.**

---

## Q3 — Tradeoff of incorporating

If Tumble Route **incorporates** and sells shares to finance 20 new vans, which best describes the **tradeoff**?

- A. The business loses its liability shield but gains funding
- B. The partners gain more personal control over daily decisions
- C. The owners **cede control** to shareholders and a board in exchange for **greater access to funding**
- D. The business can no longer grow because shareholders limit decision making

---

## Q3 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** Incorporating means selling shares — shareholders elect a board and the founders **cede control** over major decisions. In exchange the corporation gains the **greatest access to funding**, which is exactly what 20 new vans require.

**Trap: A** — flips the liability direction. Incorporating **keeps** the shield, same as an LLC; the trade is control for funding, not liability for funding.

**Trap: D** — shareholders **enable** growth by funding it. What shrinks is the founders' control, not the business's reach.

---

## Q4 — Identify the department

At a mid-size company, one department **tracks the year's expenditures and earnings and prepares the financial statements** that management reviews each quarter. Which department is this?

- A. Finance
- B. Accounting
- C. Operations
- D. Research and development

---

## Q4 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Accounting tracks expenditures and earnings and prepares financial statements — the question restates that job almost word for word.

**Trap: A** — finance secures and manages funds and recommends strategies **going forward.** Statements about money that **already moved** are accounting, **even when the word "financial" appears in the stem.**

---

## Q5 — Identify the structures from a data table

A student studies three businesses and records the data below. Which row of structures is **correct**?

| Feature | Business X | Business Y | Business Z |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of owners | 1 | 3 | Shareholders |
| Liability | Personal | Shielded | Shielded |
| Funding source | Owner's savings | Partners' savings | Stock sales |
| Decision maker | Owner alone | Owners together | Board of directors |

- A. X = Partnership, Y = LLC, Z = Sole proprietorship
- B. X = LLC, Y = Corporation, Z = Partnership
- C. X = Sole proprietorship, Y = Partnership, Z = Corporation
- D. X = Sole proprietorship, Y = LLC, Z = Corporation

---

## Q5 — Answer: **D**

**Why:** Run the three questions on each column. **X:** one owner, personal liability, self-funded → **sole proprietorship.** **Y:** three owners, **shielded** liability, no shareholders and no board → **LLC.** **Z:** shareholders, a board, stock funding → **corporation.**

**Trap: C** — gets X right, then swaps Y and Z. Business Y has **shielded liability**, which eliminates partnership: **check every feature in every row**, because one column decides the answer.

---

## Q6 — Outsourcing because wages are high

A software company **hires an outside firm** to run its customer-support phone lines because **support wages in its city are unusually high.** Which best describes this decision?

- A. The company has made customer support one of its core competencies
- B. The company has converted the support staff into internal stakeholders
- C. The company is outsourcing a function because doing so is likely to **reduce costs**
- D. The company has reorganized as a corporation

---

## Q6 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** Outsourcing is hiring another business to handle a function. The two classic triggers are **missing skills** and **high labor costs**, and expensive support wages are the second one.

**Trap: A** — a core competency is a capability a business **develops itself.** Handing the function to an outside firm is the opposite move: support has been judged a **cost to manage.**

---

## Wrap-up

- The **structure** decides three things: who **controls**, who **pays** the debts, how the business **grows**
- **Sole proprietorship and partnership** carry personal liability; **LLC and corporation** shield it
- **Partnership liability is shared** — never divided by who did the work
- **Control and funding pull in opposite directions;** a corporation buys funding with ceded control
- **3-question method:** owners? debts? funding? — start with question 1 and eliminate
- **One owner wears five hats;** partners divide roles by **strengths and interests**
- **Departments** appear with scale, and the **org chart** is the path authority flows down and accountability flows up
- **Accounting looks backward, finance looks forward** — "financial" is not a reliable signal
- **Outsourcing** is buying a function when skills are missing or labor is costly; **it is the opposite of a core competency**

> **Get the structure wrong and the org chart is just a drawing.**
