# Part 1: Why people save — and why they don't
### LO 3.1.A — Reasons to save, barriers to saving

---

## Money comes in, money goes out

**3.1.A.1** Consumers typically **earn income by working** for an organization — a business, nonprofit, or government entity — and **spend income** to buy products that address their problems, needs, and wants.

**But not all income comes from a job.** Other sources include:

| Source | Example |
|---|---|
| **Self-employment** | A freelance designer or a food-truck owner |
| **Rental properties** | A landlord collecting monthly rent |
| **Government programs** | Benefits or transfers |
| **Investments** | Interest, dividends, capital gains |
| **Retirement accounts** | Pension or retirement withdrawals |

> **Exam habit: "income" ≠ "wage." Income is any money received, from any of these sources.**

---

## Three reasons people save

**3.1.A.2** Consumers often save a portion of their income for **significant future purchases** and for **protection**.

| Reason | What it protects against / buys | Maya's version |
|---|---|---|
| **Large future purchases** | A car, a home, college tuition | $6,000 down payment for a car in 18 months |
| **Future emergencies** | Job loss, illness — income can stop without notice | 3 months of expenses ($4,500) in an emergency fund |
| **Retirement** | A source of income after wages stop | Small automatic contribution every pay period |

> **The three reasons are not the same goal.** An emergency fund must be *liquid*; retirement money can be *locked up*. Mixing them up is the classic savings mistake.

---

## Saving creates an asset that can earn interest

**3.1.A.3** **Saving creates a personal asset and may earn interest** — which can be a source of *current or future* income.

**Interest payments are determined by four things:**

| Factor | What it means |
|---|---|
| **The interest rate** | The percentage paid on the balance |
| **The amount of savings** | A bigger balance earns more, proportionally |
| **The type of savings vehicle** | A CD often pays more than a basic savings account |
| **Conditions in the economy** | Rates rise and fall with the broader economy |

> **None of these four is "how hard you work." Interest is earned by the *money*, not by the person.**

---

## Incentives to save — three designed nudges

**3.1.A.4** Consumers may be **incentivized to save** through structured programs:

| Program | How it works | The incentive |
|---|---|---|
| **Automated savings plans** | Allocate a set amount of income to savings each pay period | Removes the monthly decision — you save *before* you can spend |
| **Retirement savings plans** | Let individuals save on **taxes** when saving for retirement | Tax advantage + (often) employer matching |
| **Health savings accounts (HSAs)** | Let individuals save on **taxes** when allocating money for health expenses | Tax advantage on money earmarked for health care |

> **Notice the pattern: all three work by making saving the default, not the decision.**

---

## Barriers to saving — two kinds

**3.1.A.5** Consumers may face barriers that make saving difficult. They come in two distinct families:

| Type | Barrier | Why it blocks saving |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural (money)** | Inconsistent income | You cannot automate what you cannot predict |
| **Structural (money)** | Recurring expenses that exceed income | There is nothing left to set aside |
| **Psychological** | **Instant gratification** | Spending now feels certain; saving feels abstract |
| **Psychological** | **Lifestyle inflation** | Spending rises with income, so the surplus never appears |
| **Psychological** | **Impulse buying** | Unplanned, emotion-driven purchases drain the surplus |

> **Diagnostic question for any scenario: is this barrier about the money, or about the behaviour?** The fix is different — raise income / cut fixed costs vs. automate and set rules.

# Part 2: PESTEL and your savings
### LO 3.1.B — How external forces hit the value of savings

---

## Why PESTEL belongs in a personal finance topic

The same framework from 1.3 runs here, but the target is different:

> **In 1.3, PESTEL hit a business. In 3.1, PESTEL hits a household's savings.**

| Letter | Effect on savings (3.1.B) |
|---|---|
| **Economic** | A weak economy can cut income; a strong one can raise the cost of living — either way, less is left to save |
| **Economic (inflation)** | Erodes the **purchasing power** of money already saved |
| **Political** | Tax policy can incentivize saving (tax-advantaged accounts) |
| **Legal** | Government regulates banks and credit unions to protect consumers and stabilize institutions |

---

## The economy cuts saving from both directions

**3.1.B.1** The overall stability and performance of the economy affects **incomes** and the **cost of living**.

| State of the economy | What happens | Effect on saving |
|---|---|---|
| **Weak economy** | Loss of income, fewer hours, unemployment | Less income available after necessary expenses |
| **Strong economy** | Cost of living rises | More spent on necessities, less left over |

> **The trap: it *feels* like saving should be easy when the economy is good. In this model, both a boom and a bust can squeeze savings — for different reasons.**

---

## Inflation is the quiet tax on savings

**Inflation** = an increase in the prices of goods and services.

**It erodes the purchasing power of savings** — the same dollars buy fewer goods and services in the future.

**Worked example — Maya's emergency fund:**

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Savings | $4,500 |
| Interest earned (3%) | +$135 → $4,635 |
| Inflation (4%) | Prices rise 4% |
| **Real change in purchasing power** | **≈ −1% → about $4,455 of last year's buying power** |

> **Rule of thumb: what matters is the *real* return = interest rate − inflation.** A "high" 3% rate with 4% inflation still loses ground.

---

## Political and legal factors — the two letters students forget

**Political (3.1.B.3):** government **tax policies may incentivize savings** — for example, allowing individuals to *not pay income taxes* on a portion of income placed in designated accounts (retirement, health care, childcare).

**Legal (3.1.B.4):** government agencies **regulate financial institutions** — commercial banks and credit unions — to:
- improve **consumer protection**
- protect the **financial stability** of those institutions

> **Exam tell: "tax-advantaged" is Political. "Insured and regulated" is Legal. Same account, two different PESTEL letters — read which one the question asks about.**

# Part 3: Building a savings plan
### LO 3.1.C — How much, and where

---

## Step 1 — How much to save

**3.1.C.1** Consumers decide how much of their income to save based on:
- their **personal and financial goals**
- their **current income and expenses**

> **Individuals are likely to save more if they have *defined goals* and face *fewer barriers*.**

That sentence is the whole LO. It connects Part 1 (barriers) to Part 3 (plan):

| If the problem is… | The plan fixes it by… |
|---|---|
| No goal | Naming a target amount and date ("$6,000 by June") |
| Too many barriers | Automating the transfer, cutting a fixed cost, or both |

---

## Step 2 — Where to keep it

**3.1.C.2** Consumers choose *how and where* to keep savings based on:

1. **How much** they are saving
2. **Their goals**
3. **Their available time frame**
4. **The impact of PESTEL factors**
5. **Their evaluation of the benefits and costs** of the various vehicles offered by banks and credit unions

**The three classic vehicles:** savings accounts, money market accounts, certificates of deposit (CDs).

---

## The three insured vehicles — side by side

| Feature | Savings account | Money market account | Certificate of deposit (CD) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Federally insured?** | Yes (up to $250,000) | Yes | Yes |
| **Minimum deposit** | Low | Larger | Larger |
| **Monthly fee** | May charge | May charge (higher) | Typically none |
| **Interest rate** | Baseline | Higher than savings | Typically **highest** |
| **Access to cash** | Anytime | Easier than savings (per 3.1.C.5) | **Restricted** — locked 1 month to 5 years |

**3.1.C.6** A **CD** typically pays a **higher rate** than a savings or money market account, but depositors are **restricted from withdrawing for a set period — usually one month to five years**. It typically requires a **larger minimum deposit but no monthly fee** (varies by institution).

> **Map the vehicle to the goal:** emergency fund → savings or money market (needs access). Money you will not touch for 2 years → CD (accepts restriction for rate).

---

## The tradeoff you are actually choosing (3.1.C.3)

**3.1.C.3** When selecting an institution and account, consumers weigh:

| Consider | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Interest rate** | What the money earns |
| **Fees** | What the account costs you |
| **Minimum deposit** | What you must have to open it |
| **Potential risk** | Insured vs. uninsured |
| **Location, convenience, reputation** | Whether you can actually use it |

> **The tradeoff: accounts that pay higher interest often demand higher minimum balances or other requirements.** Higher return is bought with *less flexibility*, not with magic.

---

## What "federally insured" actually buys you

**3.1.C.4** A savings account is a deposit account at a financial institution that:
- typically pays interest
- is **insured by the federal government up to a maximum amount** ($250,000 as of 2024)
- means **the consumer does not lose their deposit if the bank goes out of business**

**But 3.1.C.4 also says:** depositors **may pay monthly fees**; the minimum deposit, the interest rate, and the maintenance fees **differ by institution**.

> **Insurance protects against bank failure. It does not protect against fees, inflation, or your own withdrawals.**

---

## The vehicles that are *not* insured (3.1.C.7)

Some individuals store savings in:

| Vehicle | Attraction | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Mobile payment accounts** | Make money **easy to spend** | Unless offered by an insured institution: **not federally insured**, **typically pays no interest** |
| **Cryptocurrency accounts** | Users hope it will **gain value as an asset** | **Not federally insured**, **typically pays no interest**, value can fall |

> **3.1.C.7 is an exam favourite because it is a two-part answer: convenience/upside on one side, no insurance and no interest on the other.**

---

## Your turn — Maya's savings plan

> Maya earns **$2,400/month** after tax. Fixed costs are **$1,500/month** (rent, food, transport). She wants a **$6,000 car down payment in 18 months** and a **$4,500 emergency fund**.

**1.** How much must she save per month for the car alone?
**2.** Her employer offers an automated savings plan and a tax-advantaged retirement account. Which barrier does each one attack?
**3.** Inflation is 4% and her savings account pays 3%. What happens to her emergency fund's purchasing power?
**4.** She is offered a 24-month CD at 5%. Should she put the emergency fund there? Why or why not?

---

## Your turn — answers

**1.** $6,000 ÷ 18 = **$333/month**. Her surplus is $2,400 − $1,500 = **$900**, so the car goal is feasible — but the emergency fund needs its own allocation.

**2.** The **automated plan** attacks the *behavioural* barriers (instant gratification, impulse buying) by making saving the default. The **retirement account** attacks the *cost* of saving by reducing taxes — a **Political** (tax policy) incentive.

**3.** Real return ≈ 3% − 4% = **−1%**. The balance grows in dollars but **loses purchasing power** — 3.1.B.2 in one line.

**4. No.** A CD **restricts withdrawals** for a set period. An emergency fund exists to be used *in an emergency* — locking it up converts a liquidity tool into a penalty. Put it in a **savings or money market account**.

---

## 🔑 Exam points to remember — Saving

| # | Point |
|---|-------|
| 1 | **Income ≠ wage** — self-employment, rent, government programs, investments, and retirement accounts all count |
| 2 | Three reasons to save: **large purchases, emergencies, retirement** — with different liquidity needs |
| 3 | **Saving creates an asset**; interest depends on the **rate, the amount, the vehicle, and the economy** |
| 4 | Incentives work by **changing the default**: automated plans, tax-advantaged retirement accounts, HSAs |
| 5 | Barriers split into **structural** (income too low/inconsistent, expenses exceed income) and **psychological** (instant gratification, lifestyle inflation, impulse buying) |
| 6 | **Both** a weak economy (lost income) **and** a strong one (higher cost of living) can reduce saving |
| 7 | **Inflation erodes purchasing power** — compare the interest rate to inflation, not to zero |
| 8 | **Political = tax incentives; Legal = regulation and insurance** — do not swap the letters |
| 9 | Savings account, money market account, and CD are all **federally insured**; they differ in **minimum deposit, fees, rate, and access** |
| 10 | **Mobile payment and crypto accounts** are typically **not insured and pay no interest** |
| 11 | More saving happens when goals are **defined** and barriers are **fewer** (3.1.C.1) |

---

## Three exam tells — saving

**Tell 1 — "Which barrier?"** If the scenario shows a person who *can* save but doesn't, the answer is **psychological** (instant gratification, lifestyle inflation, impulse buying). If the numbers simply do not work, it is **structural**. Never answer "they are bad with money."

**Tell 2 — "Which vehicle?"** Match the vehicle to the **time frame and access need**, not to the highest rate. A 6-month goal is savings/money market. A 4-year goal can be a CD.

**Tell 3 — "Real return?"** Any question that mentions both an interest rate and inflation is asking about **purchasing power**, not the balance. Subtract inflation.

## Practice — Stimulus

**Daniel** is 31 and earns $3,200 a month after tax as a delivery dispatcher. His rent and bills total $2,100. He has **$1,200 in a savings account** paying **2%**, no emergency fund, and no retirement account. Inflation is running at **4%**.

His friend offers him a "guaranteed" crypto savings account paying **9%**, with no federal insurance. Daniel also just financed a new phone on a 24-month plan, raising his monthly fixed costs by $60.

---

## Practice — Q1

> Which of Daniel's situations is best described as a **structural** barrier to saving?

> A. He buys takeout several times a week without planning
> B. His fixed expenses consume most of his income
> C. He prefers spending now rather than waiting
> D. He does not know what a CD is

---

## Practice — Q1 answer

**Answer: B.**

A **structural** barrier is about the money itself — income that is inconsistent or expenses that exceed (or nearly consume) income. $2,100 of $3,200 leaves only $1,100, and the new phone plan shrinks it further.

**Traps:**
- **A** and **C** — these are **psychological** barriers (impulse buying, instant gratification). They are real, but they are the other family.
- **D** — a knowledge gap is not one of the listed barriers; do not invent categories.

> **Structural = the numbers. Psychological = the behaviour.**

---

## Practice — Q2

> Daniel's $1,200 savings account pays 2% while inflation is 4%. What is the most accurate description of what happens to his savings over the year?

> A. His purchasing power grows by 2%
> B. His balance grows but his purchasing power falls
> C. His balance falls and his purchasing power falls
> D. Nothing changes because the account is federally insured

---

## Practice — Q2 answer

**Answer: B.**

The **balance** rises by 2% (to $1,224). Prices rise by 4%. The **real** change is about **−2%**, so the money buys less than it did a year earlier.

**Traps:**
- **A** — confuses the nominal interest rate with a real gain
- **C** — the balance does grow; only purchasing power shrinks
- **D** — federal insurance protects against **bank failure**, not against **inflation**

> **Insurance ≠ inflation protection. 3.1.B.2 is the LO most often missed for exactly this reason.**

---

## Practice — Q3

> Daniel wants an emergency fund he can access immediately. Which vehicle best fits that goal?

> A. A 36-month certificate of deposit at 5%
> B. A cryptocurrency account at 9%
> C. A money market account that is federally insured
> D. A retirement savings plan with a tax advantage

---

## Practice — Q3 answer

**Answer: C.**

An emergency fund must be **liquid, safe, and insured**. A money market account is federally insured and, compared with a savings account, provides **easier access to cash** (3.1.C.5).

**Traps:**
- **A** — a CD **restricts withdrawals** for the term; a penalty defeats the purpose of an emergency fund
- **B** — not federally insured and can lose value; 9% is compensation for risk, not a free lunch
- **D** — tax-advantaged retirement accounts are **locked** for the long term and carry early-withdrawal penalties

> **Match the vehicle to the *job*: emergencies need access, retirement needs time.**

---

## Practice — Q4

> Daniel's employer adds an automated savings plan that transfers $200 to savings on every payday. Which statement best explains why this is likely to increase his saving?

> A. It raises his income, so he has more money available
> B. It removes the need to make a spending decision each pay period
> C. It guarantees a higher interest rate than a CD
> D. It is required by federal law for all employers

---

## Practice — Q4 answer

**Answer: B.**

Automated savings plans work by **changing the default**: the money moves **before** it can be spent. This attacks instant gratification and impulse buying — the psychological barriers — without requiring more income.

**Traps:**
- **A** — automation does not change income
- **C** — automation says nothing about the interest rate; that depends on the vehicle
- **D** — these programs are **incentives offered**, not a legal requirement

> **Incentive design beats willpower — that is the point of 3.1.A.4.**

---

## Trap patterns to watch

| # | Trap type | Where it appears |
|---|-----------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Income = wage only** — ignoring self-employment, rent, benefits, investments | Q1 |
| 2 | **Structural vs. psychological barriers** — the two families are not interchangeable | Q1 |
| 3 | **Nominal vs. real return** — a positive rate can still lose purchasing power | Q2 |
| 4 | **Insurance = inflation protection** — it is not; it covers bank failure | Q2 |
| 5 | **Highest rate wins** — ignoring liquidity and lock-up periods | Q3 |
| 6 | **"Federally insured" applies to everything** — mobile payment and crypto accounts are typically **not** insured | Q3 |
| 7 | **Automation = higher income** — it changes the default, not the paycheck | Q4 |

## Wrap-up — 3.1 Saving for Future Purchases

**What we covered:**
- **Income** can come from work, self-employment, rent, government programs, investments, or retirement accounts
- People save for **large purchases, emergencies, and retirement** — each with a different liquidity need
- **Saving creates an asset**; interest depends on the rate, the amount, the vehicle, and the economy
- **Incentives** (automated plans, tax-advantaged retirement accounts, HSAs) work by changing the default
- **Barriers** are structural (the numbers) or psychological (the behaviour)
- **PESTEL:** economic forces squeeze from both directions, inflation erodes purchasing power, tax policy incentivizes, regulation protects
- **Savings account, money market account, and CD** are all federally insured but trade rate against access
- **Mobile payment and crypto accounts** are typically uninsured and pay no interest
