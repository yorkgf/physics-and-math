## The tables she passed — and what a product actually is


Saturday morning at the flea market. A parent walks the used-bike row.

She **passes three tables** of cheaper bikes without slowing down, stops at Theo's rack, and lifts the **orange tag** hanging off a **$95 commuter.** She reads all 21 checklist lines, asks who signed the bottom, hears that Theo signs every inspection himself, and **buys the bike without asking about a single component.**

> **What Trued Cycles sells: a certified ready-to-ride bike — where the certification does the selling.**



Keep one distinction straight the whole way:

| | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Raw material** | The input you start with | A used bike from a storage-unit auction |
| **Product** | The thing the customer pays for | A certified ready-to-ride bike with a signed inspection tag |

> **The used bike is Theo's raw material. The product is the certified ready-to-ride bike — the thing the parent at the rack paid for.**

### LO 2.4.A — Six stages from idea to market

## What is product development?

Product development is the process of creating or improving a product through **research and iteration.**

It is not one moment — it is a **sequence of six stages**, each with a different key question.

| # | Stage | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ideation | Generate ideas for new or improved products |
| 2 | Validation | Test the idea on potential customers before real money commits |
| 3 | Design | Source materials, build prototypes, estimate costs |
| 4 | Messaging | Set the overall marketing strategy |
| 5 | Production | Build at volume; set the supply chain; match output to demand |
| 6 | Launch | Take orders and distribute the finished product |

## Stage 1 — Ideation

The ideation stage generates ideas through **three feeds:**

| Feed | What it means | Theo's version |
|---|---|---|
| **Market research** | Looking at what customers need, where gaps exist | Scanning local listings: hundreds of used bikes described with two lazy words, "runs great," and **buyers with no way to check** |
| **Technical R&D** | Building the skills and knowledge to deliver | Free repair videos; evenings spent learning wheel truing, brake rebuilds, and drivetrain cleaning |
| **Brainstorming** | Listing every idea that might work | A scrawled list on the inside cover of the notebook — every complaint he had heard about buying a used bike |

> **The idea that survived all three feeds: sell the checked bike, and prove the check.**

> The three feeds produce a **long list**; **idea screening** then cuts it down on **market size · competition · cost · regulatory barriers** — a management step inside ideation/validation, **not a seventh CED stage**. (case: the **Idea Screening — Xiaomi builds a car** page later)

## Stage 2 — Validation and the MVP

The validation stage tests the idea on **potential customers before real money commits.**

The standard tool is an **MVP — minimum viable product**: the simplest sellable version that lets real customers **vote with real dollars.**

Theo's MVP: one **$35 mountain bike** with a bent wheel, repaired with free videos and a borrowed floor pump, sold with a handwritten checklist of every fix.

$$ \$90 - \$35 = \$55 \text{ earned — because the checklist earned the buyer's trust.} $$

> **What validation measures is product-market fit — which a product achieves when customer demand is sufficient to generate profit.**

## The MVP's three requirements — scoring Dropbox, Airbnb and Theo

The definition packs **three** requirements into one line: the **simplest sellable** version that lets real customers **vote with real dollars.**

| # | Requirement | Test | Dropbox | **Airbnb** | Theo |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Simplest** — fewest **features**, not the sloppiest build | Fewest in **number**, but the core one has to genuinely work | ✅ one bet only: sync across devices | ✅ air mattresses + breakfast: one room, one offer | ✅ rebuilds 1 bike |
| 2 | **Sellable** — a real offer with a price and a way to commit | not a demo you only show your friends | ✅ it really went live for strangers to use | ✅ a nightly price offered to strangers, not a favour to friends | ✅ a $90 tag + a deal you can close on the spot |
| 3 | **Real dollars** — money actually changes hands | behavior, not opinion | ⚠️ what the demo video bought was a **waitlist signup**, not a payment | ✅ guests paid for the nights they stayed | ✅ $35 → $90, **$55 banked** |

---
## The MVP's three requirements — verdict, classic misread, exit question

> **Dropbox passes ① and ②, but ③ is the one to argue about** — signup interest is a **usage** signal; only payment is a **fit** signal. **Airbnb passes all three**, which is why it is the cleaner teaching case: same definition, but requirement ③ actually closed.
> (note: the 1:57 clip only argues "fewer features, yet won"; the "75,000-name waitlist" is the demo video from Dropbox's public founding story — it is **not in this clip**, so name the source when you quote it)

> ⚠️ **Common student slip:** reading "minimum" as "crude." A cut-rate MVP **poisons the very feedback it exists to collect** — leaving Theo's brakes broken would not make his bike "more minimum."

> 🗣 **Exit question:** do Dropbox's 75,000 waitlist signups count as product-market fit? State your test in one sentence — it has to be **money** talking, not **head-count**.

## Validation — product-market fit

One profitable sale **hints** at fit. Theo's **confirmation** came 2 weeks later:

A stranger he had never contacted asked for a checked bike — because the first buyer sent him.

> **Unprompted referral sales are the cleanest fit signal a small business gets: demand arriving at a profitable price with zero marketing spent.**

> ⚠️ **Counter-example:** one-off demand created by feeling — the "spend wildly for the creator" kind — **is not fit**: no repeat purchase, and no preference for the product itself. Also, "Cha Yan barely advertised early on" is a public fact the clip may not state; say it out loud.

| Signal                                         | Strength            |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| One profitable sale                            | Hints at fit        |
| Unprompted referral from first buyer           | **Confirms** fit    |
| Repeat purchase                                | Strong evidence     |
| Demand at profitable price with zero marketing | **Cleanest** signal |

## Stage 3 — Design

The design stage **sources materials, builds prototypes, and estimates production and delivery costs.**

| Dimension | Meaning | Theo's version |
|---|---|---|
| **Functionality** | How the product **works** — the mechanical layer | Brakes that stop; gears that shift |
| **User experience** | The **buying layer** — how the customer encounters the product | The test ride; the seat set to rider's height; the tag a parent can read in 10 seconds |
| **Attributes** | Basic measurable features | Frame sizes for middle schoolers; 21-point inspection quality bar; one color — safety orange |

> 🎬 **The stage is also three actions** → next page (sourcing, prototype, cost)

---

## Design — the three actions: source, prototype, estimate

| Action | What it produces | Theo's version | The number it leaves behind |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sourcing** — decide where materials and parts come from | a supply source + a **unit part cost** | batch-buys brake pads, cables, tubes | **≈$18 of parts** per rebuild |
| **Prototype** — build one to try | a sample you can ride, hold, and test | the first rebuild he rides himself | **one bike**, not twenty |
| **Cost estimation** — price the build **and** the delivery | a cost per unit + a cost per sale | the Saturday drive, about six bikes a trip | **$18 + 4 hours + fuel** |

> **Sourcing sets the cost floor. The prototype pins down the design. The estimate decides whether a price is even possible.**

> ⚠️ **Students drop the second half of cost.** "Cost" is not only the $18 of parts — it is also the **drive to the stall**: that is what caps how many bikes Theo can sell in a week.

---

## Design — costs and feedback

**Pricing from design data:** Notebook averages put **parts near $18** per commuter rebuild. Delivery costs Theo the Saturday drive hauling stock to the stall.

**Feedback on prototypes:** Businesses gather feedback to learn who is likely to buy and how they react to specific features.

> Theo's early buyers **ignored scratched paint and squeezed the brake levers first** — so rebuild hours moved from **cosmetics to brakes.**

> **Design pins down what the product is. Feedback tells you what the customer actually cares about.**

## Stage 4 — Messaging

The messaging stage sets the product's **overall marketing strategy** before production scales.

### The value proposition

A statement built from market research that names three things:

1. **Who** the product serves
2. **What** problem or need it addresses
3. **Why** it beats the alternatives

> **Value proposition = who + what problem + why us.**

---

## Messaging — Theo's value proposition

Theo's fits on one line of the tag: **"Half of retail. Inspected. Guaranteed 30 days."**

# Part 2: Branding
### LO 2.4.B — Building identity and trust

---

## What is branding?

**Branding** = the process of developing an **identity** (a **brand**) for a business or product.

A **brand** is a name, term, design, symbol, or combination that **identifies and differentiates** a company's products from competitors.

A brand identity has **three jobs:**

| Job | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Distinguish** the product from competitors | The customer can tell yours apart from the rest |
| **Raise customer awareness** | The target segment knows you exist |
| **Generate loyalty** | Customers return and refer others |

> **Businesses typically start from their vision or value proposition when shaping that identity.**

---

## Theo's brand identity

Theo's value proposition promises inspection and a guarantee. His identity signals **proof** everywhere it appears.

| Element | What it is | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **The orange tag** | A physical inspection certificate zip-tied to the handlebars | Proof you can **hold** |
| **The name — Trued Cycles** | "Trued" = the mechanic's term for a bent wheel bent back to straight | A used thing **restored to true** |

> A brand identity can travel through a **name, an idea, a term, a symbol, a design, or any combination.**

---

## Trademarks

Businesses often protect brand elements by **trademarking** them.

- **Trued Cycles** is a name
- The **orange tag** is a symbol
- A **trademark** on either would keep a copycat stall from hanging orange tags next Saturday

> **A trademark gives the owner legal protection against imitation.**

---

## The boundary of branding — generic products

Some customers **refuse to pay** for branding. Businesses may sell **generic products** — unbranded goods aimed at customers who prioritize **cost savings over brand names.**

Theo watches that trade happen every weekend:

> A shopper on a **$40 budget** passes his rack for the cheapest untagged bike in the row. Store-brand cereal beats the name brand on the same logic.

| | Branded product | Generic product |
|---|---|---|
| **Identity** | Name, logo, symbol, packaging | None — unbranded |
| **Target** | Customers who value trust, quality signals | Customers who prioritize cost savings |
| **Price** | Higher — brand equity supports it | Lower — no brand premium |

---

## Brand equity — earned, not bought

**Brand equity** = the added financial and competitive value a product earns because customers **recognize, trust, and prefer** the brand.

Three advantages of strong branding:

1. **Differentiation** — In a crowded market, a strong brand stands out
2. **Trust and loyalty** — Customers return to brands they recognize and trust
3. **Pricing power** — Strong brands can charge more because customers perceive higher value

> **Branding builds brand equity over time through consistency across every touchpoint. You cannot buy equity — you earn it.**

> 🎬 **An identity can travel through five carriers** → next page (name · idea · term · symbol · design)

# Part 3: Product Life Cycle
### LO 2.4.C — Four stages, four strategies

---

## What is the product life cycle?

The **Product Life Cycle (PLC)** is the series of stages a product passes through — from **introduction to decline** — dictated by **changes in customer demand over time.**

At every stage, the business **adapts its marketing focus** to the competitive challenge: trying to **grow market share early** and **hold it late.**

Understanding the PLC helps businesses:
- Anticipate **changes in sales and profit**
- Adjust **marketing strategy** to each stage
- Manage a **portfolio** of products at different stages

> **Every product has a life cycle. The question is how long each stage lasts.**

---

## The four stages

```
Sales /
Profit
  │        ╱‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾╲
  │       ╱  Growth   ╲  Maturity
  │      ╱             ╲________
  │     ╱ Introduction        ╲
  │    ╱                    Decline
  │___╱
  └──────────────────────────────── Time
```

| Stage | Sales | Profit | Competition |
|-------|-------|--------|-------------|
| Introduction | Low, slowly rising | Negative (losses) | Few or none |
| Growth | Rapidly increasing | Turning positive | Entering fast |
| Maturity | Peak, slowing | High but pressured | Heavy, price wars |
| Decline | Falling | Shrinking | Exiting |

---

## Introduction

**What happens:**
- Almost nobody knows the product exists
- Sales are **low** and grow slowly
- Costs are high — the business often operates at a **loss**

**Marketing focus:** Build **brand awareness** to generate first demand.

**Theo's first tagged month:** flyers, the stall fee, a slow rack — every marketing dollar spent **teaching strangers what an orange tag means.**

> **Introduction is about convincing the market that your product exists and is worth trying.**

---

## Growth

**What happens:**
- Sales and revenue **climb at an increasing rate**
- Production rises to keep pace
- **Competitors enter** the market, attracted by visible demand

**Marketing focus:** **Differentiate** from rivals; improve quality; promote the brand; advertise.

**Theo's growth moment:** A Saturday rival shows up with **photocopied inspection sheets.** Theo's answer: a **signature** on every tag and a **guarantee** that costs a copycat real money to match.

> **Growth is exciting but dangerous — competitors arrive just as the market gets profitable.**

---

## Maturity

**What happens:**
- Sales **flatten** because most customers who want the product already have it
- The market is **saturated**; competition is heaviest
- **Price wars** become common

**Marketing focus:** Build **brand loyalty** through continued differentiation, lower prices, and innovation.

**Trued Cycles at maturity:** Theo's town holds only so many middle schoolers → **tune-up visits, sibling discounts,** and a new tier launched before the old one stalls.

> **Maturity is about holding your position, not expanding it.**

---

## Decline

**What happens:**
- Sales **fall** as customers move to rival products or innovative substitutes
- Profits shrink — the business must decide what to do

**Marketing focus:** **Cut costs** or **fund a redesign** to stay viable.

**Three strategic options:**

| Option | When to choose |
|--------|---------------|
| **Reduce investment** | Harvest remaining profits with minimal spending |
| **Discontinue** | Exit the market and redirect resources |
| **Reinvent** | Update the product to start a new life cycle |

> **Decline is not failure — it is a signal to make a strategic decision.**

---

## Decline — Theo's redesign option

Flip phones declined this way when smartphones arrived.

If cheap **electric bikes** reach his market, Theo's pedal commuters decline on the same curve.

> His **redesign option** is already visible: certify used **e-bikes** with the same orange tag.

---

## Exam distinction — PLC stage signals

| Signal | Stage |
|---|---|
| "Almost nobody knows it exists" / "teaching strangers" | **Introduction** |
| "Sales climbing fast" + "competitors entering with copies" | **Growth** |
| "Most customers already have it" / "saturated" / "flattening" | **Maturity** |
| "Customers moving to substitutes" / "falling" / "discontinued" | **Decline** |

> **PLC stage = what the sales data says, not what the calendar says.**

---

## Exam tell — how the question words the stage

The review video flags a **specific trap**: "flat sales in a saturated market" is **maturity**, not decline.

| Wording | Stage |
|---|---|
| Sales **flattened**, most customers already own it, rivals sell similar models, business launches a loyalty program + lowers price | **Maturity** (retain share through loyalty + lower prices) |
| Sales **falling** as customers leave for rivals or substitutes | **Decline** |

> **Climbing at an increasing rate = growth. Flat = maturity. Falling = decline.** The curve alone decides — not the product's age.

---

## Product portfolio management

Smart businesses manage **multiple products at different life cycle stages:**

| Product | PLC Stage | Role in Portfolio |
|---------|----------|-------------------|
| Product A | Maturity | Cash cow — funds other products |
| Product B | Growth | Rising star — needs investment to capture market |
| Product C | Introduction | Future bet — may succeed or fail |
| Product D | Decline | Harvest or exit |

> **A portfolio with products in different stages balances risk — when one declines, another grows.**

---

## 🔑 Exam points — Product Life Cycle

| # | Point |
|---|---|
| 1 | Four stages: **Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline** |
| 2 | Each stage requires **different** marketing strategies |
| 3 | **Introduction**: low sales, unknown; focus on **awareness** |
| 4 | **Growth**: rising sales, competitors entering; focus on **differentiation** |
| 5 | **Maturity**: saturated, flat sales; focus on **loyalty** |
| 6 | **Decline**: falling sales; **reduce, discontinue, or reinvent** |
| 7 | **PLC stage is determined by sales data**, not by product age |

# Case Discussion: Dyson
### Applying 2.4 concepts to a global brand

---

## Dyson — product development story

James Dyson noticed his vacuum cleaner **lost suction** as the bag filled. At a local sawmill, he observed a **30-foot conical centrifuge** spinning sawdust out of air without any filter.

> **Dyson's idea: What if a vacuum could use cyclone technology instead of a bag?**

| Stage | Dyson's action |
|-------|---------------|
| **Ideation** | Saw industrial cyclone at sawmill; technical R&D on cyclone physics |
| **Validation** | G-Force launch in Japan (1986) at $2,000 — MVP to test demand |
| **Design** | 5,127 prototypes over 5 years — functionality, UX, attributes refined |
| **Messaging** | "Say goodbye to the bag" — value proposition targeting the bag pain point |
| **Production** | Built his own factory after every manufacturer refused to license |
| **Launch** | DC01 in UK (1993) — #1 selling vacuum within 18 months |

---

## Dyson — branding and PLC

**Branding:** Dyson built identity around "We engineer things better" — distinctive industrial design, premium pricing, ~6,000 patents protecting innovation.

| Product | PLC Stage | Strategy |
|---------|----------|----------|
| Bagless vacuum (DC series) | **Maturity** | Premium positioning, continuous innovation |
| Supersonic hair dryer | **Growth** | Premium pricing, influencer marketing |
| Airwrap styler | **Growth → Maturity** | Brand extensions, new attachments |
| Airstrait straightener | **Introduction** | Build awareness, early adopter focus |
| Contra Rotator washing machine | **Decline → Discontinued** | Good reviews, poor sales — learned from failure |
| Electric car project | **Cancelled** | Invested $2.5B, then cut losses |

> **Dyson manages a portfolio: mature vacuums fund the growth of newer product lines.**

# Practice
### AP Exam–style questions

---

## Practice — Stimulus

**Artisan Pops** is a small company that makes gourmet ice pops using local fruit.

- **Year 1:** Sold at one farmers' market; 120 pops/week; lost money on a rented freezer cart and custom molds
- **Year 2:** Added two farmers' markets and a food truck; sales tripled; two copycat brands appeared
- **Year 3:** Sold in 15 grocery stores; sales grew 20%; launched a "tropical collection" with mango and passion fruit flavors
- **Year 4:** Sales flatlined; grocery stores demanded slotting fees; a new competitor launched organic pops at a lower price

---

## Practice — Q1

> In Year 1, Artisan Pops operated at a loss despite selling 120 pops per week. Which statement **best** explains why?

> A. The company failed to validate its idea before launching
>
> B. The costs of the freezer cart and custom molds exceeded early revenue
>
> C. The company should have launched in grocery stores first
>
> D. Customer demand was too low to support the business

---

## Practice — Q1 answer

**Answer: B.**

In the introduction stage, fixed costs (equipment, setup) are high and sales volume is low. Revenue from 120 pops/week did not cover the rental and mold costs.

**Traps:**
- **A** — Validation happened before launch; the question asks about Year 1 performance
- **C** — Farmers' markets are a common MVP launch channel; no evidence grocery stores would have been better
- **D** — 120 pops/week is decent demand; the problem is cost structure, not demand

> **In the introduction stage, losses are normal — high fixed costs + low volume = operating at a deficit.**

---

## Practice — Q2

> In Year 2, two copycat brands appeared. This is most consistent with which stage of the product life cycle?

> A. Introduction — competitors test the concept
>
> B. Growth — visible demand attracts new entrants
>
> C. Maturity — all competitors are already established
>
> D. Decline — competitors try to capture remaining demand

---

## Practice — Q2 answer

**Answer: B.**

Sales tripled (rapid growth signal), and competitors entered because they could see the demand. This is the hallmark of the growth stage.

**Traps:**
- **A** — In introduction, few or no competitors exist; the market is unproven
- **C** — Maturity has heavy existing competition, not new entrants arriving
- **D** — Decline has falling sales, not tripling sales

> **"Sales rising fast + competitors appearing" = growth stage every time.**

---

## Practice — Q3

> Artisan Pops launched the "tropical collection" in Year 3. This is an example of:

> A. Product development — generating a new idea
>
> B. Product extension — adding variations to a mature product
>
> C. Brand repositioning — changing how customers see the brand
>
> D. Market penetration — entering a new geographic market

---

## Practice — Q3 answer

**Answer: B.**

Adding new flavors (mango, passion fruit) to an existing product line is a classic product extension strategy used during the maturity stage to boost slowing sales.

**Traps:**
- **A** — This is not a new product; it is a new flavor of the same product
- **C** — The brand positioning did not change; only the product variety expanded
- **D** — Market penetration means selling more of the same product in the same market; new flavors are product variation, not market expansion

> **Product extension = new variations of the same product to re-energize sales.**

---

## Practice — Q4

> In Year 4, grocery stores demanded slotting fees and a new competitor launched organic pops at a lower price. Which PLC stage is Artisan Pops most likely entering?

> A. Introduction — still paying to enter the market
>
> B. Growth — expanding distribution with new costs
>
> C. Maturity — saturated market, intense competition, margin pressure
>
> D. Decline — sales have already fallen to zero

---

## Practice — Q4 answer

**Answer: C.**

Sales flatlined (saturation), slotting fees squeeze margins, and a competitor is undercutting on price. All three are signals of the maturity stage.

**Traps:**
- **A** — Slotting fees are a retailer demand, not a launch cost; the product is already in stores
- **B** — Sales are not rising; "flatlined" ≠ growth
- **D** — Sales have not fallen to zero; they have plateaued. Decline requires falling sales

> **Flat sales + margin pressure + price competition = maturity. Falling sales = decline.**

---

## Practice — Q5

> Artisan Pops wants to avoid decline. Which strategy is **most** appropriate for a product in the late maturity stage?

> A. Increase prices to maximize revenue from loyal customers
>
> B. Reduce investment and harvest remaining profits
>
> C. Launch a new product line to diversify the portfolio while defending the existing brand
>
> D. Discontinue the product before losses begin

---

## Practice — Q5 answer

**Answer: C.**

Portfolio diversification (new product lines) is the strongest long-term strategy during late maturity. Defending the existing brand while developing new products balances risk.

**Traps:**
- **A** — Raising prices in a price-competitive market accelerates customer loss
- **B** — "Harvest" is a decline-stage strategy; in late maturity, there is still time to reinvest
- **D** — Discontinuing a product with still-positive sales is premature; reinvention is better

> **Late maturity strategy: defend what you have + invest in what comes next.**

---

## Practice — Q6

> A student says: "Dyson's bagless vacuum is in the decline stage because the technology is old." Which statement **best** evaluates this claim?

> A. Correct — old technology always enters decline
>
> B. Incorrect — the vacuum is still in the introduction stage because Dyson keeps launching new models
>
> C. Incorrect — PLC stage is determined by sales trends and competitive dynamics, not by the age of the technology
>
> D. Correct — Dyson should discontinue the vacuum and focus only on hair products

---

## Practice — Q6 answer

**Answer: C.**

The PLC stage is determined by **sales trends, market saturation, and competitive dynamics** — not by how old the underlying technology is. A product with old technology can still be in growth or maturity if sales are strong.

**Traps:**
- **A** — Age of technology ≠ PLC stage. Many products use old technology and remain in maturity (e.g., bicycles, umbrellas)
- **B** — New models do not reset the PLC to introduction; they are product extensions within the current stage
- **D** — The vacuum line still generates significant revenue; discontinuing it would be a poor business decision

> **PLC stage = what the sales data says, not what the calendar says.**

---

## Trap patterns to watch

| # | Trap type | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Cost ≠ demand** — introduction losses come from high fixed costs, not low demand | Q1 |
| 2 | **"Competitors entering" = growth**, not maturity (maturity = competitors already there) | Q2 |
| 3 | **New flavors ≠ new product** — product extension is not the same as product development | Q3 |
| 4 | **Flat sales ≠ decline** — flat sales = maturity; falling sales = decline | Q4 |
| 5 | **Harvest is a decline strategy** — in maturity, invest in new products instead | Q5 |
| 6 | **Technology age ≠ PLC stage** — stage is determined by sales data, not by calendar time | Q6 |

## Wrap-up — 2.4 Product

**What we covered:**
- **Six stages of product development:** Ideation → Validation → Design → Messaging → Production → Launch
- **Raw material ≠ product** — the certified bike is what the customer pays for
- **Value proposition** = who + what problem + why us; **product positioning** = vs. rivals
- **Branding** = building an identity with three jobs: distinguish, raise awareness, generate loyalty
- **Trademarks** protect brand elements; **generic products** skip branding for cost savings
- **Product Life Cycle:** Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline
- Marketing focus changes at **every** stage: awareness → differentiation → loyalty → harvest or reinvent
- **PLC stage = what the sales data says**, not what the calendar says
