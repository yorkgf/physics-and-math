# Part 1: Product Development
### LO 2.4.A — Six stages from idea to market

---

## What is product development?

Product development is the process of turning a **customer problem** into a product people will **pay for**.

It is not one moment — it is a **sequence of six stages**, each with a different key question.

> **The goal at every stage is the same: reduce uncertainty before spending more money.**

---

## The six stages at a glance

| Stage | Key Question | Output |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| 1. Generate Idea | What customer problem can we solve? | List of product concepts |
| 2. Screen & Refine | Is this idea feasible? | Shortlist of viable ideas |
| 3. Develop Prototype | What will the product look and function like? | Visual or working model |
| 4. Test Concept | Does this solve a real problem for real people? | Validated customer interest |
| 5. Develop MVP | What is the simplest version customers will pay for? | Basic sellable product |
| 6. Refine with Feedback | What do early users want changed? | Improved product, ready to scale |

---

## Stage 1 — Generate Idea

Ideas come from many sources:

- **Customer input** — complaints, reviews, surveys, social media
- **Brainstorming** — team sessions, design thinking workshops
- **Observation** — watching how people actually use (or struggle with) existing products
- **Trend analysis** — spotting gaps in the market

> **Best ideas start with a problem, not a product.**

If you start with "I want to build X," you may build something nobody needs.
If you start with "People struggle with Y," you already have a customer.

---

## Stage 2 — Screen & Refine

Not every idea deserves to move forward. Screening filters out weak concepts **before** you spend money building.

**Screening criteria:**

| Factor | Question to Ask |
|--------|----------------|
| Market size | Are enough people experiencing this problem? |
| Competition | Is someone already solving it well? If so, can we do it differently? |
| Cost | Can we build this at a price customers will accept? |
| Regulatory | Are there legal barriers (safety, patents, licenses)? |

> **Screening is an internal exercise — you haven't talked to customers yet.**

---

## Stage 3 — Develop Prototype

A prototype is a **preliminary model** of the product.

It can range from:
- A **sketch or 3D render** (visual prototype)
- A **cardboard mockup** (physical prototype)
- A **clickable app wireframe** (digital prototype)
- A **working model** with real materials (functional prototype)

> **Prototypes are built to learn, not to sell.**
> Each version teaches you something — what works, what breaks, what confuses users.

---

## Stage 4 — Test Concept with Customers

Concept testing means presenting the idea to **real potential customers** to check:

- Does this solve a problem **they actually have**?
- Would they **pay** for it?
- What would make it **better**?

This is different from idea screening:
- **Screening** = internal evaluation (costs, competition, regulations)
- **Concept testing** = external validation (what do real customers think?)

> **A product that excites your team but bores your customers is not a product.**

---

## Stage 5 — Develop MVP

**MVP = Minimum Viable Product**

The most basic version of a product that includes only **core features** — and that customers are **willing to pay for**.

| MVP is NOT... | MVP IS... |
|---------------|----------|
| A prototype (built to explore) | A product (built to sell) |
| Full-featured | Core features only |
| Perfect | Good enough to learn from |
| Free | Something customers pay for |

> **The MVP exists to answer one question: "Will real people exchange real money for this?"**

---

## Stage 6 — Refine Based on Feedback

After early customers use the MVP, collect feedback and improve:

- Which features do they **love**? → Keep and strengthen
- Which features do they **ignore**? → Consider removing
- What is **missing**? → Add in next version
- What **frustrates** them? → Fix immediately

This is an **iterative cycle** — version 1 → feedback → version 2 → feedback → version 3...

> **The best products are not designed in one shot. They evolve through repeated customer feedback loops.**

---

---

- Wireframes → Stage 3 (prototype) ✅ done
- Surveyed students → Stage 4 (concept testing) ✅ done

---

## 🔑 Exam points to remember — Product Development

| # | Point |
|---|-------|
| 1 | The six stages are **sequential** — each reduces uncertainty before the next investment |
| 2 | **Screening** (internal) ≠ **Concept testing** (external/customer-facing) |
| 3 | A **prototype** is for exploring; an **MVP** is for selling |
| 4 | The MVP includes only **core features** customers will pay for |
| 5 | Product development is **iterative** — feedback loops refine the product over time |
| 6 | **Value proposition** = why a customer should choose your product over competitors |

---

## Dyson — A product development story

James Dyson (born 1947) had already invented the **Ballbarrow** — a wheelbarrow with a ball instead of a wheel. It sold well in the UK, but US competitors **copied the design**. Dyson lost that battle.

Then, while renovating his farmhouse, he noticed his vacuum cleaner **lost suction** every time the bag filled with dust.

At a local sawmill, he observed a **30-foot conical centrifuge** — spinning air that separated sawdust without any filter.

> **Dyson's idea: What if a vacuum could use cyclone technology instead of a bag?**

---

## Dyson — 5,127 prototypes

Dyson spent **five years** (1979–1984) building prototypes in a barn behind his house.

- **Prototype 1:** Did not work at all
- **Prototype 500:** Partial suction, but air leaked
- **Prototype 2,000:** Cyclone worked, but the machine was too large
- **Prototype 5,127:** Finally worked perfectly

He had **no income** during this period. His wife supported the family by teaching art.

> **Each failed prototype was not wasted — it was a lesson that informed the next version.**

---

## Dyson — Rejection and MVP

When Dyson approached vacuum manufacturers with his working prototype:

- **Every major brand refused** to license it
- Why? They made **billions selling replacement bags** — a bagless vacuum would destroy that revenue stream

So Dyson **manufactured it himself**:

- Launched the **G-Force** in Japan (1986) — sold for $2,000, a premium price
- Japan launch = **MVP test** — would customers pay for bagless technology?
- Japanese customers loved it → validated the concept

> **The G-Force was Dyson's MVP: the simplest version of his technology, sold to test demand.**

---

## Dyson — From MVP to global brand

After Japan validated demand, Dyson launched the **DC01** in the UK (1993):

- Priced **twice** as high as competitor vacuums
- Became the **#1 selling vacuum** in the UK within 18 months
- Expanded to the US, Europe, and Asia

**Key product development stages in Dyson's story:**

| Stage | Dyson's Action |
|-------|---------------|
| Generate Idea | Saw industrial cyclone at sawmill |
| Screen & Refine | Analyzed vacuum market, found bag pain point |
| Develop Prototype | 5,127 versions over 5 years |
| Test Concept | Showed working prototype to manufacturers (rejected) |
| Develop MVP | G-Force launch in Japan (1986) |
| Refine with Feedback | DC01 in UK (1993) — refined design, price, and features |

# Part 2: Branding
### LO 2.4.B — Building identity and trust

---

## What is a brand?

A **brand** is a name, term, design, symbol, or combination that **identifies and differentiates** a company's products from competitors.

Examples:
- **Apple** — minimalist design, innovation, premium pricing
- **Nike** — athletic performance, "Just Do It" attitude
- **Dyson** — engineering excellence, premium innovation

> **A brand is not a logo. A brand is what customers feel when they see that logo.**

---

## Brand vs. Branding vs. Brand Equity

| Concept | What It Is | Example |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| **Brand** | The identifiable mark (name, logo, symbol) | The Dyson logo and name |
| **Branding** | The strategic process of shaping customer perception over time | Dyson's consistent message: "Better engineering, no compromise" |
| **Brand Equity** | The added financial value from customer recognition, trust, and loyalty | Customers pay $500+ for a Dyson vacuum when a $100 vacuum exists |

> **Branding builds brand equity over time. You cannot buy equity — you earn it.**

---

## Why does branding matter?

Branding creates **three advantages**:

1. **Differentiation** — In a crowded market, a strong brand stands out
2. **Trust and loyalty** — Customers return to brands they recognize and trust
3. **Pricing power** — Strong brands can charge more because customers perceive higher value

> **Brand equity is the reason a $50 t-shirt with a logo sells better than a $10 t-shirt without one.**

---

## Building brand equity — Consistency is key

Brand equity is built through **consistent branding across every touchpoint**:

| Touchpoint | What It Communicates |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Packaging | Quality, personality, values |
| Advertising | Message, tone, target audience |
| Social media | Voice, engagement, authenticity |
| Customer service | Care, reliability, responsiveness |
| Product design | Innovation, attention to detail |

> **One bad experience at any touchpoint can damage years of brand building.**

---

## Dyson — Branding strategy

Dyson built one of the strongest brands in consumer electronics:

| Brand Element | How Dyson Does It |
|--------------|-------------------|
| Product design | Distinctive industrial look — you recognize a Dyson instantly |
| Pricing | Always premium — signals quality and innovation |
| Advertising | "Say goodbye to the bag" — focused on solving the customer's pain point |
| R&D investment | **15–20% of annual revenue** on R&D; labs in UK, Singapore, US |
| Patents | **~6,000** granted and pending patents — protects innovation from imitation |
| Leadership | James Dyson hired a CEO so he could personally focus on design and engineering |

> **Dyson's brand message is consistent: "We engineer things better."**

---

## Dyson — Brand equity in action

Why do customers pay **2–3x more** for a Dyson vacuum?

- They trust the brand to deliver **superior performance**
- The distinctive design **signals quality** to visitors in their home
- Years of consistent messaging created **perceived value** beyond the product

> **Brand equity turns a $150 manufacturing cost into a $500 retail price — and customers feel they got a good deal.**

---

## Your turn — Brand check

A new coffee shop opens in a neighborhood with 6 existing coffee shops.

They name it "Coffee Shop" with a plain white sign and generic cups.

**What is the branding problem, and what would you recommend?**

---

## Your turn — Brand check answer

**Problem:** No differentiation. "Coffee Shop" tells customers nothing about why this shop is different from the 6 others.

**Recommendations:**
- Choose a **distinctive name** that communicates a value proposition
- Design a **logo and visual identity** that stands out
- Use **consistent packaging** (cups, bags, signage) to build recognition
- Define a **brand voice** — what does this coffee shop stand for? (local beans? cozy atmosphere? speed?)

> **Branding is not decoration — it is a strategic decision about how customers will perceive and remember you.**

---

## 🔑 Exam points to remember — Branding

| # | Point |
|---|-------|
| 1 | **Brand** = the mark; **Branding** = the process; **Brand Equity** = the value earned |
| 2 | Brand equity is built through **consistent** branding across all touchpoints |
| 3 | Strong branding creates **differentiation**, **trust/loyalty**, and **pricing power** |
| 4 | Brand equity allows businesses to charge **premium prices** |
| 5 | One negative experience can **damage** years of brand equity |

# Part 3: Product Life Cycle
### LO 2.4.C — Four stages, four strategies

---

## What is the product life cycle?

The **Product Life Cycle (PLC)** is a model describing the **four stages** every product passes through from launch to withdrawal.

Understanding the PLC helps businesses:
- Anticipate **changes in sales and profit**
- Adjust **marketing, production, and financial strategy**
- Manage a **portfolio** of products at different stages

> **No product sells forever. Every product has a life cycle — the question is how long each stage lasts.**

---

## The four stages

```
Sales /
Profit
  │        ╱‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾╲
  │       ╱  Growth   ╲  Maturity
  │      ╱             ╲________
  │     ╱ Introduction        ╲
  │    ╱                    Decline
  │___╱
  └──────────────────────────────── Time
```

| Stage | Sales | Profit | Competition |
|-------|-------|--------|-------------|
| Introduction | Low, slowly rising | Negative (losses) | Few or none |
| Growth | Rapidly increasing | Turning positive | Entering fast |
| Maturity | Peak, slowing | High but pressured | Heavy, price wars |
| Decline | Falling | Shrinking | Exiting |

---

## Stage 1 — Introduction

**What happens:**
- Product just launched — most customers **don't know it exists**
- Sales are **low** and grow slowly
- Costs are **high** (R&D, production setup, promotion)
- The business often operates at a **loss**

**Strategy focus:**
- Build **awareness** — advertising, demonstrations, free trials
- Educate **early adopters** — explain why this product matters
- Invest heavily in **promotion** — customers need a reason to try something new

> **Introduction is about convincing the market that your product exists and is worth trying.**

---

## Stage 2 — Growth

**What happens:**
- Sales rise **rapidly** — word spreads, demand accelerates
- Profits begin to appear as **economies of scale** reduce unit costs
- **Competitors enter** the market, attracted by visible demand

**Strategy focus:**
- **Expand distribution** — get the product into more stores, channels, regions
- **Differentiate** from new competitors — improve features, branding, service
- **Scale production** — invest in capacity to meet rising demand

> **Growth is exciting but dangerous — competitors arrive just as the market gets profitable.**

---

## Stage 3 — Maturity

**What happens:**
- Sales reach their **peak** but growth slows
- The market is **saturated** — most potential customers already have the product
- Competition is **heaviest** — many competitors fight for the same customers
- **Price wars** become common

**Strategy focus:**
- **Defend market share** — loyalty programs, brand reinforcement
- **Optimize costs** — improve efficiency to protect margins
- **Product extensions** — new features, new colors, limited editions

> **Maturity is about holding your position, not expanding it.**

---

## Stage 4 — Decline

**What happens:**
- Sales **fall** as customer preferences shift or new technology arrives
- Profits **shrink** — the business must decide what to do

**Three strategic options:**

| Option | When to Choose |
|--------|---------------|
| **Reduce investment** | Harvest remaining profits with minimal spending |
| **Discontinue** | Exit the market and redirect resources |
| **Reinvent** | Update the product to start a new life cycle |

> **Decline is not failure — it is a signal to make a strategic decision.**

---

## Product portfolio management

Smart businesses manage **multiple products at different life cycle stages**:

| Product | PLC Stage | Role in Portfolio |
|---------|----------|-------------------|
| Product A | Maturity | Cash cow — funds other products |
| Product B | Growth | Rising star — needs investment to capture market |
| Product C | Introduction | Future bet — may succeed or fail |
| Product D | Decline | Harvest or exit |

> **A portfolio with products in different stages balances risk — when one declines, another grows.**

---

## Dyson — Product life cycle in action

| Product | PLC Stage | Dyson's Strategy |
|---------|----------|-----------------|
| Bagless vacuum (DC series) | **Maturity** — market saturated, many competitors | Defend with premium positioning, continuous innovation |
| Airblade hand dryer | **Growth** — expanding into commercial spaces | Expand distribution, differentiate from paper towels |
| Supersonic hair dryer | **Growth** — disrupting a stagnant market | Premium pricing, heavy promotion, influencer marketing |
| Airwrap styler | **Growth** → approaching **Maturity** | Brand extensions, new attachments, limited editions |
| Airstrait straightener | **Introduction** — new category | Build awareness, educate market, early adopter focus |
| Contra Rotator washing machine | **Decline → Discontinued** | £1,000 price, positive reviews, but poor sales — learned from failure |
| Electric car project | **Cancelled before launch** | Invested $2.5B, then cut losses — bold risk-taking |

> **Dyson manages a portfolio: mature vacuums fund the growth of newer product lines. Failed projects teach lessons.**

---

## Your turn — PLC stage check

Match each scenario to its PLC stage:

1. A new electric skateboard launches with a YouTube ad campaign and a $50 discount for the first 100 buyers
2. A soda brand releases a "limited edition summer flavor" to boost slowing sales
3. A smartphone model sees sales double each quarter and three competitors release similar products
4. A DVD rental chain closes 40% of its stores as streaming services dominate

---

## Your turn — PLC stage check answer

1. **Introduction** — launch promotion, discount to attract early adopters
2. **Maturity** — limited edition = product extension to defend slowing sales
3. **Growth** — rapid sales increase + competitors entering
4. **Decline** — falling sales, closing stores, new technology replacing it

> - "Slowing" / "limited edition" / "saturated" → Maturity
> - "Closing" / "replaced by" / "falling demand" → Decline

---

## 🔑 Exam points to remember — Product Life Cycle

| # | Point |
|---|-------|
| 1 | Four stages: **Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline** |
| 2 | Each stage requires **different** marketing, production, and financial strategies |
| 3 | **Introduction**: low sales, high costs, losses; focus on awareness |
| 4 | **Growth**: rising sales, new competitors; focus on expansion and differentiation |
| 5 | **Maturity**: peak sales, heavy competition, price wars; focus on defending share |
| 6 | **Decline**: falling sales; reduce, discontinue, or reinvent |
| 7 | **Product portfolio management** = managing multiple products at different PLC stages |

## Practice — Stimulus

**Artisan Pops** is a small company that makes gourmet ice pops using local fruit.

- **Year 1:** Sold at one farmers' market; 120 pops/week; lost money on a rented freezer cart and custom molds
- **Year 2:** Added two farmers' markets and a food truck; sales tripled; two copycat brands appeared
- **Year 3:** Sold in 15 grocery stores; sales grew 20%; launched a "tropical collection" with mango and passion fruit flavors
- **Year 4:** Sales flatlined; grocery stores demanded slotting fees; a new competitor launched organic pops at a lower price

---

## Practice — Q1

> In Year 1, Artisan Pops operated at a loss despite selling 120 pops per week. Which statement **best** explains why?

> A. The company failed to screen its idea before launching
>
> B. The costs of the freezer cart and custom molds exceeded early revenue
>
> C. The company should have launched in grocery stores first
>
> D. Customer demand was too low to support the business

---

## Practice — Q1 answer

**Answer: B.**

In the introduction stage, fixed costs (equipment, setup) are high and sales volume is low. Revenue from 120 pops/week did not cover the rental and mold costs.

**Traps:**
- **A** — Screening happened before launch; the question asks about Year 1 performance
- **C** — Farmers' markets are a common MVP launch channel; there is no evidence grocery stores would have been better
- **D** — 120 pops/week is decent demand; the problem is cost structure, not demand

> **In the introduction stage, losses are normal — high fixed costs + low volume = operating at a deficit.**

---

## Practice — Q2

> In Year 2, two copycat brands appeared. This is most consistent with which stage of the product life cycle?

> A. Introduction — competitors test the concept
>
> B. Growth — visible demand attracts new entrants
>
> C. Maturity — all competitors are already established
>
> D. Decline — competitors try to capture remaining demand

---

## Practice — Q2 answer

**Answer: B.**

Sales tripled (rapid growth signal), and competitors entered because they could see the demand. This is the hallmark of the growth stage.

**Traps:**
- **A** — In introduction, few or no competitors exist; the market is unproven
- **C** — Maturity has heavy existing competition, not new entrants arriving
- **D** — Decline has falling sales, not tripling sales

> **"Sales rising fast + competitors appearing" = growth stage every time.**

---

## Practice — Q3

> Artisan Pops launched the "tropical collection" in Year 3. This is an example of:

> A. Product development — generating a new idea
>
> B. Product extension — adding variations to a mature product
>
> C. Brand repositioning — changing how customers see the brand
>
> D. Market penetration — entering a new geographic market

---

## Practice — Q3 answer

**Answer: B.**

Adding new flavors (mango, passion fruit) to an existing product line is a classic product extension strategy used during the maturity stage to boost slowing sales.

**Traps:**
- **A** — This is not a new product; it is a new flavor of the same product
- **C** — The brand positioning did not change; only the product variety expanded
- **D** — Market penetration means selling more of the same product in the same market; new flavors are product variation, not market expansion

> **Product extension = new variations of the same product to re-energize sales.**

---

## Practice — Q4

> In Year 4, grocery stores demanded slotting fees and a new competitor launched organic pops at a lower price. Which PLC stage is Artisan Pops most likely entering?

> A. Introduction — still paying to enter the market
>
> B. Growth — expanding distribution with new costs
>
> C. Maturity — saturated market, intense competition, margin pressure
>
> D. Decline — sales have already fallen to zero

---

## Practice — Q4 answer

**Answer: C.**

Sales flatlined (saturation), slotting fees squeeze margins, and a competitor is undercutting on price. All three are signals of the maturity stage.

**Traps:**
- **A** — Slotting fees are a retailer demand, not a launch cost; the product is already in stores
- **B** — Sales are not rising; "flatlined" ≠ growth
- **D** — Sales have not fallen to zero; they have plateaued. Decline requires falling sales

> **Flat sales + margin pressure + price competition = maturity. Falling sales = decline.**

---

## Practice — Q5

> Artisan Pops wants to avoid decline. Which strategy is **most** appropriate for a product in the late maturity stage?

> A. Increase prices to maximize revenue from loyal customers
>
> B. Reduce investment and harvest remaining profits
>
> C. Launch a new product line to diversify the portfolio while defending the existing brand
>
> D. Discontinue the product before losses begin

---

## Practice — Q5 answer

**Answer: C.**

Portfolio diversification (new product lines) is the strongest long-term strategy during late maturity. Defending the existing brand while developing new products balances risk.

**Traps:**
- **A** — Raising prices in a price-competitive market accelerates customer loss
- **B** — "Harvest" is a decline-stage strategy; in late maturity, there is still time to reinvest
- **D** — Discontinuing a product with still-positive sales is premature; reinvention is better

> **Late maturity strategy: defend what you have + invest in what comes next.**

---

## Practice — Q6

> A student says: "Dyson's bagless vacuum is in the decline stage because the technology is old." Which statement **best** evaluates this claim?

> A. Correct — old technology always enters decline
>
> B. Incorrect — the vacuum is still in the introduction stage because Dyson keeps launching new models
>
> C. Incorrect — PLC stage is determined by sales trends and competitive dynamics, not by the age of the technology
>
> D. Correct — Dyson should discontinue the vacuum and focus only on hair products

---

## Practice — Q6 answer

**Answer: C.**

The PLC stage is determined by **sales trends, market saturation, and competitive dynamics** — not by how old the underlying technology is. A product with old technology can still be in growth or maturity if sales are strong.

**Traps:**
- **A** — Age of technology ≠ PLC stage. Many products use old technology and remain in maturity (e.g., bicycles, umbrellas)
- **B** — New models do not reset the PLC to introduction; they are product extensions within the current stage
- **D** — The vacuum line still generates significant revenue; discontinuing it would be a poor business decision

> **PLC stage = what the sales data says, not what the calendar says.**

---

## Trap patterns to watch

| # | Trap type | Where it appears |
|---|-----------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Cost ≠ demand** — introduction losses come from high fixed costs, not low demand | Q1 |
| 2 | **"Competitors entering" = growth**, not maturity (maturity = competitors already there) | Q2 |
| 3 | **New flavors ≠ new product** — product extension is not the same as product development | Q3 |
| 4 | **Flat sales ≠ decline** — flat sales = maturity; falling sales = decline | Q4 |
| 5 | **Harvest is a decline strategy** — in maturity, invest in new products instead | Q5 |
| 6 | **Technology age ≠ PLC stage** — stage is determined by sales data, not by calendar time | Q6 |

**What we covered:**
- Product development has **six stages**: idea → screen → prototype → concept test → MVP → refine
- **Screening** (internal) ≠ **Concept testing** (customer-facing)
- A **prototype** explores; an **MVP** sells
- **Branding** builds identity, trust, and pricing power over time through consistency
- **Brand equity** = the added value from customer recognition and loyalty
- **Product life cycle**: Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline
- Each PLC stage requires **different strategies** for marketing, production, and finance
- **Product portfolio management** balances risk across multiple products
