# 1.5 Vision
### AP Business with Personal Finance · Unit 1

Lesson deck — **concepts + practice**

---

## Today's goals

By the end of this lesson you can:

1. Distinguish between **core values** and **core competencies**, and explain how each shapes business and personal decisions
2. Evaluate **vision statements** and **mission statements** — and tell them apart
3. Classify organizations as **businesses**, **social enterprises**, or **nonprofits**

**Running story continues:** the snack box is three weeks old, and **two offers** arrive in the same week that the cash box can't decide between.

---

## The two offers

The snack box has regulars — then one week brings **two offers:**

1. A parent offers to **bankroll a candy-only mega cart**: double the inventory, double the margin — but **the fruit the families asked for would have to go**
2. The league asks him to take over the **far field's snack table** — a bigger job that **pays less at first** and runs entirely on trust

One pair of hands, one Saturday, two doors.

> **Nothing in his cash box answers this**, because it's a decision about **what the business is *for*** and **what it is *best at*** — not about the arithmetic.

---

## Core values — definition

**Core values** = the **defining beliefs and principles** that guide an individual's or a business's actions.

Examples: creativity, excellence, transparency, empathy, reliability.

He writes three on the **lid of his cooler:**
- *Show up on time*
- *Listen to the families*
- *Keep prices fair*

---

## Why communicate core values?

Businesses communicate core values for **two reasons:**

1. **Align employees** to a shared purpose
2. **Guide decision makers** toward choices consistent with what the business believes

> His cousin helps on Saturdays, and **the cooler lid is how the cousin makes the right call when nobody's watching.** That's the job values do: **turn one person's judgment into the whole team's default.**

---

## Testing a stated value

**Run the mega cart past the lid:** double margin, but the fruit goes — and the families asked for that fruit two weeks ago.

The cart **fails "listen to the families" before the math even starts.**

> **A value the business abandons the first time money appears was a decoration.** Test any stated value by asking: **what did it cost the business at the last real decision?**

- Transparency → what goes on the label?
- Empathy → how a refund is handled?
- Excellence → whether a flawed batch ships?

---

## Patagonia — three values, three bills

Three tests. Three values. **Every one cost money.**

| Value | The decision | Revenue impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Transparency** | Published its own supply-chain failures **before anyone asked**, then printed the plan to fix them | Short-term reputation risk |
| **Environmental commitment** | A full-page Black Friday ad: *"Don't Buy This Jacket"* — with the environmental cost of making it | Told customers **not to buy** on the biggest sales day of the year |
| **Quality** | **Free lifetime repairs** | A jacket that lasts fifteen years costs fifteen years of replacement sales |

> The value said *"build things that last"* — not *"build things that break on schedule so customers come back."* All three held anyway. **Ask the same of the cooler lid:** if a parent doubled the margin in exchange for the fruit, would *"listen to the families"* survive?

## Decoration — the 4-question check

> ⚠️ **A value that has never met a decision which costs money is *untested*, not fake. The decoration is the one that ran at the first price.**

**Run any claimed value through four questions:**

| # | Question | What it catches |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Falsifiable?** Name one concrete decision it forced. ("Integrity" → which one?) | Points to no decision = can't be tested |
| 2 | **Cost trace?** Was there a deal where it made the business earn less or lose money? | Never paid a bill = never proven |
| 3 | **Incentives aligned?** Do bonuses / commissions / promotions reward the opposite? | Incentives beat declarations (deep dive in 1.6) |
| 4 | **Would anyone oppose it?** Who would pick the other side of "excellence, teamwork"? | A value everyone agrees with constrains nothing |

## Core competencies — definition

**Core competencies** = the **capabilities, skills, and expertise** that help an individual or a business **outperform rivals and achieve competitive advantage.**

Examples: innovation, customer service, communication, ethical behavior, efficiency.

His list:
- The **pre-order system** in the group chat (nobody else takes orders before game day)
- The **halftime handoff** (one minute per team)
- A **reputation for showing up** (carried from the hallway in 1.1)

---

## Core value vs. core competency

**The pair the exam loves to swap — pin the difference now:**

| | Core **value** | Core **competency** |
|---|---|---|
| It is… | what the business **believes** | what the business **is good at** |
| Example | Reliability on the cooler lid (a belief he enforces) | The 60-second handoff (a skill he built) |
| Scenario signal | "Believes" | "Good at" |
| Opposite of it | **Decoration** — abandoned at the first cost | **Weakness** — no edge over rivals |

> **A value is what the business *believes*; a competency is what it is *good at*.** Speed is a **built skill** → competency. **Values are chosen, competencies are built** — and what a business *must not violate* is neither: that is **business ethics**, and 1.6 tests the same act against that third standard.

---

## Competencies feed competitive advantage

**Competitive advantage** (from 1.2) = the edge that lets one win customers from a rival.

**Core competencies feed that edge.** A differentiated product, a lower cost, a faster service — trace an advantage backward and you usually find **a capability somebody developed.**

> **Rule of thumb:** pursue the opportunities your **strengths can win**, and spend resources where those strengths **compound.**

The far-field table leans on his logistics + reputation; the mega cart leans mostly on **someone else's money.**

---

## Values and competencies for individuals

The same tool points at **you.** Individuals consider their own core values and core competencies when choosing **educational paths, careers, and jobs.**

- A student who values **independence** and is skilled with **systems** reads one set of options
- A student who values **service** and is skilled with **people** reads another

> **Two job offers:** one pays **20% more** but demands weekend hours that collide with something you refuse to give up; the other pays **less** but builds a skill already on your strengths list. **Knowing your list before the offer arrives is the entire advantage.**

## Vision and mission — the two statements

| | **Vision statement** | **Mission statement** |
|---|---|---|
| Describes | the **future** the business is trying to create — its **aspirations** and **core values** | **what the business does** and **how** it will achieve its long-term goals |
| In one word | Aspiration | Operation |
| His snack box | *"Game days where no family scrambles."* — **no operations**, just a future worth wanting | *"Deliver pre-ordered snack boxes to every team by halftime, reliably, at prices families can trust."* — **the work** |
| Scenario signal | "Aspires" | "Does and how" |

**Test either one with the same question:** does it describe a **future worth wanting**, or does it describe **the work**?

---

## Classify by content — not by label

> **"A city where every student graduates ready"** → no operations, all aspiration → **vision.**
>
> **"We provide afterschool math tutoring in three neighborhoods, priced by family income"** → what it does + how → **mission.**

**The labels are unreliable:** the words "vision" and "mission" rarely appear inside the statements themselves — **classify by content.**

## Why vision and mission matter — externally

Outside the business, vision and mission statements **earn trust before a single transaction:**

- **Community members** gauge whether the business shares their values

## Patagonia vs. Seesaw — setup

**Two specialty brands. Two relationships with their own vision.**

| | Patagonia | Seesaw Coffee |
|---|---|---|
| Stated vision | *"Save our home planet"* | *"Bring specialty coffee into everyone's daily life"* |
| Vision connected to business? | **Yes** — outdoor gear, supply chain, planet | **Started yes** — then chased the 9.9-yuan price war |

## Seesaw — the vision, and the gap

Seesaw Coffee was founded in 2012 in Shanghai — one of the **pioneers of China's specialty coffee movement.** Its stated vision:

> *"Bring specialty coffee into everyone's daily life."*

Core values: **craft, innovation, community** — identity built on quality beans, skilled baristas, a curated café experience. Then the **9.9-yuan price war** arrived.

- Luckin Coffee and Cotti flooded the market with **deep-discount coupons** (9.9 yuan a cup)
- Seesaw **cut prices and ran its own 9.9 promotions** to keep customers
- Specialty beans and trained baristas **cost more than 9.9 yuan a cup** — every discounted sale was a **loss**
- The brand that stood for **craft** was now competing on **price** — a war it could not win

---

## Seesaw — the result

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 2012 | Founded in Shanghai — specialty coffee pioneer |
| 2017–2021 | VC-funded expansion; stores in major cities across China |
| 2023–2024 | Price war with Luckin/Cotti; 9.9-yuan promotions erode margins |
| 2024–2025 | Mass store closures; debts unpaid; **bankruptcy** |

> **The vision could not survive the first competitive shock because the business stopped acting on it.** When the price war arrived, "craft" was the first thing to go — and the brand followed.

## Business — definition

A **business** seeks to **achieve and increase profits**, fulfill its stated mission/goals, and **remain competitive and viable** long-term.

Profit improves in exactly **two ways:**
- **Increase revenues**
- **Decrease costs**

> That's the **revenue − cost** arithmetic from 1.1, now running a whole organization. **Owners keep or reinvest the profit** — that is the whole test.

---

## Social enterprise — definition

A **social enterprise** seeks **profit while also, by design, achieving social objectives.** Owners still keep profit; the social aim is built in.

Impact travels through **three channels:**

| Channel | Example |
|---|---|
| The **products** themselves | A company whose product is a low-cost water filter |
| **Operations** | A moving company that hires workers other employers overlook |
| The **financial model** | A brand that routes a share of every sale to a local scholarship fund |

## Nonprofit organization — definition

A **nonprofit** serves the **public good** rather than generating profit for owners.

> ⚠️ **Mark the precision — the exam tests the edges:** a nonprofit **can** sell goods and services, and its revenues **can** exceed its costs. The law draws **one hard line: any surplus must be reinvested in the organization, and none of it may flow to owners.** **Surplus reinvestment is the legal signature of a nonprofit.**

It also raises money through channels that **don't require a sale:**

- **Grant** = funding awarded for a stated purpose, usually by a government or foundation; earned **by application, not by sale**
- **Donations** arrive with **no product attached**
- **Volunteers** lower the **labor** side of the cost ledger

## The organization decision tree

**Ask two questions in order:**

1. **Can owners keep the profit at all?**
   - If **no** (every surplus feeds the mission) → **nonprofit**
2. If **yes** → look for a **social objective** built into the products, operations, or financial model:
   - **Find one** → social enterprise
   - **Find none** → business

## Stimulus: Bright Thread

**Rosa Delgado** owns **Bright Thread**, a clothing alteration and repair shop. Posted **core values:** craftsmanship, honesty, community. Bright Thread is known across the neighborhood for the **fastest turnaround of any tailor** — a strength built on Rosa's **custom scheduling system.** Two framed statements hang by the register:

1. *"Clothing that lasts, in a neighborhood that thrives."*
2. *"Bright Thread repairs and alters clothing with same-week service at prices working families can afford."*

Rosa is weighing two expansions: (1) **reselling inexpensive imported garments she has not inspected** at a high markup, or (2) **launching a same-day uniform repair service** for local businesses.

---

## Q1 — Classify the fastest turnaround

Bright Thread's fastest-in-the-neighborhood turnaround is best classified as which?

- A. A core value
- B. A vision statement
- C. A social objective
- D. A core competency

<!--
Pause. Ask: is "fastest turnaround" something the business believes, or something it is good at?
-->

---

## Q1 — Answer: **D**

**Why:** Core competencies are capabilities/skills/expertise that help a business **outperform rivals** and achieve competitive advantage. Fastest turnaround is a capability with the **rival comparison built right into the phrase**, resting on a specific skill — Rosa's scheduling system.

**Trap: A** — the **paired-concept swap** for this topic. Core **values** are beliefs/principles (the craftsmanship, honesty, community posted on the wall).

> **A value is what the business *believes*; a competency is what it is *good at*.** Speed here is a **built skill** → competency.

---

## Q2 — How values + competencies should guide the choice

Which best explains how Rosa's core values and core competencies should influence her choice between the two expansions?

- A. Choose the resale option, because higher markups increase profit fastest
- B. Favor the option that aligns with her values and uses her existing strengths — the same-day uniform service
- C. Pursue both options, because businesses grow by accepting every opportunity
- D. Core values apply only to employees, so they shouldn't affect an owner's decision

---

## Q2 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Businesses consider core competencies when deciding which opportunities to pursue and how to allocate resources, and choose actions **consistent with their values.** The uniform service runs directly on her speed + scheduling system **and** serves the community her values name; reselling uninspected garments strains craftsmanship + honesty while using **none** of her strengths.

**Trap: A** — high-margin move that abandons strengths/values **risks the reputation the profits depend on.**

**Trap: C** — one shop cannot fund and staff every opportunity; **choosing well is the entire skill being tested.**

---

## Q3 — Classify statement 1

The first framed statement — *"clothing that lasts, in a neighborhood that thrives"* — is best classified as which?

- A. A vision statement, because it expresses the shop's values and aspirations
- B. A mission statement, because it describes the shop's daily operations
- C. A code of conduct for employees
- D. A business hypothesis to be tested

---

## Q3 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** A vision statement is a concise description of **core values and aspirations** — this names **a future the shop wants to exist** (durable clothing, thriving neighborhood) and describes **no operations.**

**Trap: B** — the **vision-mission swap.** Statement 2 is the **mission:** what it does — repairs/alterations — and how — same-week service at fair prices.

> **Run the test:** a **future worth wanting** = vision; a **description of the work** = mission.

---

## Q4 — Why communicate core values?

Which best explains why businesses communicate core values to their employees?

- A. To **guarantee** that no employee ever behaves unethically
- B. To increase revenue in the following quarter
- C. To align employees to a shared purpose and guide decisions consistent with the business's beliefs
- D. To satisfy a legal requirement that businesses publish their values

---

## Q4 — Answer: **C**

**Why:** Businesses communicate core values to **align employees** to a shared purpose and **guide decision makers** toward choices consistent with the business's beliefs.

**Trap: A** — **"guarantee"** is an absolute. Values **guide** choices; **no statement on a wall guarantees behavior.**

**Trap: D** — borrows **legal-factor thinking from Topic 1.3** — no law requires a business to publish core values.

## Q5 — What does the data support?

The **$30,000 surplus** was spent expanding the organization's **free cooking classes**, and **no payment went to any owner.** Based on the table, Harbor Kitchen is best classified as which?

- A. A traditional business, because it earns revenue from selling meals
- B. A nonprofit organization, because its surplus is reinvested in its mission and it relies partly on grants and donations
- C. A social enterprise, because it sells meals and offers free classes
- D. A failing organization, because its costs exceeded its sales revenue

---

## Q5 — Answer: **B**

**Why:** Every marker points the same direction: **grants and donations** alongside sales revenue, the surplus went **back into the mission**, and **no owner received a payment.**

> **Surplus reinvestment is the legal signature of a nonprofit.**

**Trap: C** — the tell is the **destination of the money:** a social enterprise seeks profit **for its owners** alongside social objectives; Harbor Kitchen pays **no owner anything.**

**Trap: D** — reads one row and stops: sales alone fall short of costs, but **total revenue is $280,000 against $250,000** = a $30,000 surplus. **A nonprofit running a surplus is healthy, legal, and common.**

## Q6 — Social enterprise vs. traditional business

Which best distinguishes a social enterprise from a traditional business?

- A. A social enterprise intentionally pursues social objectives through its products, operations, or financial model while still seeking profit
- B. A social enterprise is legally required to reinvest all surplus funds into the organization
- C. A social enterprise must be owned by a charity
- D. A social enterprise **always** donates the **majority** of its profits

---

## Q6 — Answer: **A**

**Why:** A social enterprise seeks profit while also achieving social objectives; impact travels through **three channels:** products, operations, or financial model.

**Trap: B** — borrows the **nonprofit's legal rule.** Reinvestment of **all** surplus defines nonprofits; social enterprises **keep real profit.**

**Trap: D** — fails on **"always"** and **"majority"** — a fixed donation share is **one possible** financial model, and nothing about the category requires it.

## Wrap-up

- **Core values** = what the business **believes**; **core competencies** = what it is **good at**
- **Test a stated value** by what it cost at the last real decision — **"ran at the first price" = decoration**
- **Decoration check (4 questions):** falsifiable? · cost trace? · incentives aligned? · would anyone oppose it?
- **Competencies feed competitive advantage** — pursue opportunities your strengths can win
- **Vision** = the **future** worth wanting; **Mission** = **what** it does and **how** — **classify by content**, not by the label on the document
- **Three organization types:** business (owners keep profit), social enterprise (profit + social objective), nonprofit (surplus → mission, no owner payment)
- **Organization decision tree:** owners keep profit? → No = nonprofit; Yes → social objective = social enterprise; else = business
