### Explanations

**Q1 — B.** Artisan production relies on skilled labor and close attention to detail in smaller quantities; mass production relies on technology, assembly lines and machinery for far larger quantities. Customer priorities drive the choice: customization and quality point to artisan, low price points to mass.

**Q2 — D.** Businesses consider cost, quality, efficiency, convenience and risk when choosing suppliers. Risk is the factor that hides: natural disasters, political instability, resource shortages, production errors and supplier reputation can delay delivery or raise costs. No factor wins automatically — the weighting depends on the business and the market.

**Q3 — B.** Scaling works when revenue growth outruns cost growth: fixed costs are spread over more units, so profit grows faster than either revenue or cost. Costs always rise when output doubles — the test is whether revenue rose faster.

**Q4 — A.** Businesses seeking competitive advantage through low prices typically use mass production and build supply chains focused on reducing costs — cheaper resources and more efficient production processes, sometimes at scale. High-quality resources serve a quality strategy; exclusive agreements serve a barriers-to-entry strategy.

**Q5 — C.** Identifying target customers and developing customer profiles helps a business design products and marketing elements that appeal to that population, which is typically more effective and less costly than appealing to a wide audience. Narrowing the audience does not reduce the number of rivals in the market.

**Q6 — A.** Digital tools include subscriber lists, online accounts, click-tracking software, tracking apps and social media monitoring; traditional research tools include surveys and interviews. Businesses also buy customer data from other businesses — all of it must be weighed against privacy and breach risks.

**Q7 — D.** Brand loyalty is ongoing preference based on trust and positive experience; a repeat purchase may simply be convenience or price. Repetition alone does not create brand equity, and price plus convenience here is closer to a situational influence and a personal factor than to loyalty.

**Q8 — B.** Substitute products satisfy the same underlying need and give consumers a choice when one option is restricted or unavailable. The need for caffeine did not disappear when the ban did — it moved to another product.

**Q9 — C.** Technology and innovation make new products accessible and change how customers find information and buy — the internet and smartphones enabled online shopping, price comparison and peer reviews. Sellers gain reach and data; buyers gain information. Both sides shift purchasing patterns.

**Q10 — D.** Reciprocity is the felt obligation to reciprocate after receiving a favor, gift or concession; free samples, trials and gifts are the classic tactic. Liking is attraction or similarity; consensus is quantity of buyers; consistency is alignment with one's own past actions.

**Q11 — C.** Secondary-source research gathers quantitative and/or qualitative information that already exists — government, commercial and academic publications, competitor sites — and is more cost-effective than extensive primary research. It is normally done FIRST to map the landscape before primary research refines the questions.

**Q12 — D.** Method follows the data needed: surveys for large quantitative samples, focus groups and one-on-one interviews for in-depth qualitative explanations with follow-up questions, experiments and observation for behaviour rather than opinion. Using the wrong method for a question is the classic error.

**Q13 — A.** Despite the use of market research to inform decisions, businesses are not always able to accurately predict customer and market responses; they often must innovate and respond using limited, unclear or contradictory data. The professional move is to act on the evidence, not to cherry-pick or loop the research until it agrees with the idea.

**Q14 — C.** Some customers prioritise cost savings over branding, so businesses may sell generic products. Preference for the plain version is a price decision, not differentiation, and no brand equity or barrier to entry is involved.

**Q15 — C.** During introduction a business launches the product; sales volume and revenue are typically low and losses common, so marketing raises brand awareness to generate initial demand. Growth means rapidly rising sales and new competitors; maturity means flat sales in a saturated market; decline means falling sales.

**Q16 — B.** Businesses typically consider their vision and/or the value proposition when developing a brand identity so the brand appeals to the target customer — for example, a brand built around quick delivery should look fast. A brand identity is the name, idea, term, symbol or design (often trademarked) that distinguishes the product.

**Q17 — A.** The order is raw materials → manufacturing → warehouse → distribution/retail → customer. A warehouse stores; a distribution center stores and moves goods onward, so they cannot be swapped; manufacturing cannot come before raw materials.

**Q18 — D.** A service supply chain is people + resources + delivery system. Services have no visible factory or warehouse, but they still have a chain — 'services have no supply chain' is the classic trap.

**Q19 — A.** A customer profile is a fictional description of one specific sample customer; a market segment is the group. Lin is one person, so 'segment' is wrong. Customer data is the raw information the persona is built from; a purchasing pattern is timing/frequency/quantity of buying.

**Q20 — A.** CAC = (marketing + advertising + sales costs) ÷ customers acquired = ¥12,000 ÷ 400 = ¥30. LTV = ¥150. Judge them together (LTV − CAC = ¥120 net): a higher CAC can still be the better buy when LTV grows faster, and CAC must never be judged on its own.

**Q21 — B.** Collecting and selling data without the customer's awareness is a privacy violation; a data breach is data exposed to outsiders. The business must balance the benefits of customer data against the risks — loss of customers, violation of core values, and damage to reputation.

**Q22 — C.** Low-involvement routine buying = habitual; a high-stakes comparison of alternatives = the rational decision-making process. Consequential purchases (a home, a laptop) trigger the rational process; small daily purchases do not.

**Q23 — C.** Colleagues set group norms → social and cultural factor (permanent expectations). Store atmosphere right now → situational influence (temporary, moment-specific). Personal factors are who the shopper is (age, income, lifestyle); psychological factors are what she thinks and feels.

**Q24 — C.** The limited quantity creates urgency → scarcity (honest use: real limited stock; manipulative misuse: a fake countdown timer). Consensus = 'many people bought it'; authority = 'an expert endorses it'; consistency = 'stay true to your past choice'.

**Q25 — B.** The assumption fed into the test is the hypothesis; randomly assigning real customers to two versions and comparing outcomes is an A/B test — a form of primary-source research. New data was collected (so not secondary); no survey was administered; and the business deliberately changed one variable, which is the opposite of observation.

**Q26 — B.** Surveying only classmates is sample bias — the sample is not representative of the target market. Monthly visits over time is a trend, best shown with a line graph; a pie chart shows part-to-whole shares. Percentages from a biased sample cannot be generalized.

**Q27 — D.** The order matters: desirability (do customers want it?) → feasibility (can it be built and produced?) → viability (can it profit?). This idea passes the first test but fails the second and third, so it should not be pursued. An existing prototype makes neither feasibility nor viability true.

**Q28 — D.** A prototype explores and tests the design (design stage); the MVP is the simplest version with only core features, released to real customers to test willingness to pay and product-market fit (validation stage). Slides rule of thumb: MVP feedback → Validation; prototype feedback → Design.

**Q29 — D.** Brand equity is the value the market assigns because customers recognize, trust and prefer the brand. A value proposition is the company's own claim about why its product is better; product messaging is the communication stage; market saturation is when most potential customers already own the product.

**Q30 — A.** Flat sales in a saturated market with heavy price competition = maturity; the strategy is to defend share through differentiation, lower prices and innovation. Introduction = low sales and awareness building; growth = rapidly rising sales and new entrants; decline = falling sales.
