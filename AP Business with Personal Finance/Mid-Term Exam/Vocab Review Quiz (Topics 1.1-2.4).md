## Topic 1.1

**Q1.** A juice startup gives away free samples at a school fair. Nearly every student who tastes the juice says they would happily buy it, but the company cannot yet charge more than it costs to produce a bottle. Which statement best describes the business?

- A. No value has been created, because no money has been earned.
- B. The business has created value but has not yet captured value.
- C. The business has created and captured value, because customers like the product.
- D. The business has captured value but has not created value.

**Q2.** A parent buys a ¥80 school lunch box for a child, who eats from it every day; the box is sold for ¥150. Which statement best describes the business?

- A. The parent is a consumer but not a customer.
- B. Value has been captured but not created, because a lunch box is a want rather than a need.
- C. The child is the customer and the parent is the consumer.
- D. Value creation has occurred and value has been captured, because the product responds to the child's need and the price exceeds the cost of producing it.

## Topic 1.2

**Q3.** Three vendors sell the same kind of phone case outside the school gate. Vendors want to charge ¥60; buyers want to pay ¥40. Over the term all three sell nearly identical cases at ¥50. Which best explains the ¥50 price?

- A. One of the vendors is a monopoly and set the price for the others.
- B. In a competitive market, sellers seeking higher prices and buyers seeking lower prices interact until a prevailing market price is established.
- C. The government set the ¥50 price to protect consumers.
- D. The vendors differentiated their cases, so buyers paid a premium.

**Q4.** A city grants one company the only license to run a private waste-collection service on an island, and the company charges high fees because no other firm may operate there. The license best creates:

- A. A barrier to entry that gives the company a monopoly.
- B. Differentiation, because the service is better than the alternatives.
- C. Cost leadership, because the company controls the whole market.
- D. A situation in which the company has no competitive advantage, because it has no rivals.

**Q5.** A hand-iced cake shop charges ¥380 per cake while a supermarket bakery sells similar cakes for ¥200. The owner says customers choose her shop because nobody else offers the same flavors and designs, and she has never cut prices to match the supermarket. The shop's competitive advantage comes from:

- A. Monopoly
- B. Barriers to entry
- C. Differentiation
- D. Cost leadership

## Topic 1.3

**Q6.** A city government introduces a subsidy for businesses that install rooftop solar panels. Which PESTEL factor does this subsidy represent, and why?

- B. Environmental, because it supports an environmentally friendly product.
- C. Legal, because the subsidy is written into the municipal code.

**Q7.** Students at a school increasingly choose plant-based meals and are willing to pay more for them. Which PESTEL factor best explains this change, and why?

- A. Technological, because plant-based meals are a new innovation.
- B. Political, because the government subsidizes plant-based meals.

## Topic 1.4

**Q8.** A bakery in one neighborhood is famous for its chili buns and queues every morning. A friend of the baker opens a branch three kilometers away, using the same recipes, equipment, and opening hours, and business is good. The second shop's business idea came from:

- A. A gap — a customer need nobody in the new neighborhood was meeting.
- B. Replication — copying a working business model into a new market.
- C. An opportunity created by a change in technology.
- D. Observation of a customer complaining that no good buns were available.

**Q9.** A student invests ¥3,000 in a phone-skin business with no customers yet, expecting to earn ¥12,000 if the trend takes off. Which statement best describes this risk–return tradeoff?

- A. The return is guaranteed, because the investment is small.
- B. The potential return is low because no customer has bought yet, so the risk is low too.
- C. The potential return is high because there is no proven demand, which also means a high chance of losing the investment.

## Topic 1.5

**Q10.** A company's vision statement reads: "To be the most trusted snack brand in the city by 2030." Its mission statement reads: "We deliver freshly baked snacks to neighbourhood schools and reinvest 10% of profits into food-safety training." Which statement is correct?

- A. The vision is a value statement and the mission is a financial target.
- B. Both are mission statements, because both mention what the business does.
- C. The mission describes the future, and the vision lists current activities.

**Q11.** A café is known for baristas who can latte-art and for remembering regular customers' names and orders. Which statement best explains where the café's competitive advantage comes from?

## Explanations

**Q1 — B.** Value creation = the product responds to a real customer problem/need/want (students want it). Value capture = the business can charge more than it costs to produce — not yet true here. Trap: customer liking is not value capture; value creation does not require revenue.

**Q2 — D.** The product addresses a real need (value creation) and ¥150 > ¥80 cost (value capture). The buyer is the customer (parent) and the user is the consumer (child), so the options that reverse those terms are wrong. A lunch box serves a need, so 'want not need' is wrong.

**Q3 — B.** In a competitive market the interaction of sellers pushing prices up and buyers pushing prices down establishes a prevailing price no individual seller can hold above. Three vendors is not a monopoly; identical cases are not differentiated, so there is no premium.

**Q4 — A.** A license only one firm can hold is a barrier to entry (regulation), and one business producing a unique service in a market with no rivals is a monopoly. Classic trap: having no rivals means there is no competitive-advantage contest, so 'no competitive advantage because no rivals' is right.

**Q5 — C.** The shop wins on distinguishing features rivals do not offer → differentiation. It does not win on lowest cost (it charges more) → not cost leadership. Nothing stops rivals from copying the recipe, and rivals exist, so it is neither a barrier to entry nor a monopoly.

**Q6 — A.** Classify by the source of the force: a subsidy is a government policy tool → political. A subsidy is a policy choice, not a rule a business must comply with (that would be legal). Subject matter ≠ source: solar technology is the topic, but the force acting is a government policy choice.

**Q7 — D.** A shift in what consumers choose to value (cultural norms, lifestyle trends) is a social factor. The fact that it costs money does not make it economic — that is the standard trap pair. Nothing in the scenario points to income, innovation, or government action.

**Q8 — B.** The second shop copies a model that already works elsewhere → replication. The distinction is the source of the idea: an existing business vs. an observed unmet need vs. a change that opens a new way of doing something.

**Q9 — C.** No proven demand plus money already invested = high risk, and that is exactly what makes a high potential return possible; the return is never guaranteed. Willingness to invest time is not viability, and a small investment does not remove risk.

**Q10 — D.** Vision = the future worth wanting (aspiration + values); mission = what the business does and how it pursues its goals (the work). Test by content, not by label: 'by 2030' is a future, 'we deliver… and reinvest…' is the work.

**Q11 — C.** Core competencies are the capabilities, skills, and expertise that let a business outperform rivals. Core values are beliefs — they do not create advantage by themselves; a menu can be copied (so it is not a barrier to entry), and rivals exist (so it is not a monopoly).

**Q12 — D.** No profit is paid to owners and surplus must be reinvested → nonprofit. A social enterprise keeps profit for its owners while pursuing a built-in social objective, so that option is wrong. Members voting does not change the ownership structure.

**Q13 — A.** A specific wrong act (filming customers) is unethical behavior, not a dilemma with trade-offs on both sides. Modeling ethical behavior is one of four prevention tools — it names a tool rather than describing the situation.

**Q14 — C.** An incentive is offered before the behavior and pulls employees toward it; here it produces cutting corners that damage trust. A repercussion is a consequence applied after a transgression. Employees are internal stakeholders, so they must be included in the analysis — and a correct verdict built on invalid reasoning ('both numbers rise, so keep it') is wrong reasoning.

**Q15 — C.** Two owners sharing control and profits, with personal liability on the loan → partnership. A corporation and an LLC both shield owners from personal liability; a sole proprietorship has exactly one owner, so two friends cannot be one.

**Q16 — B.** Handing a whole function to another business = outsourcing (trigger: missing in-house skill / high labor cost). Preparing financial statements = accounting (looks backward at money already moved); advising which lines to expand = finance (looks forward, secures and manages funds).

**Q17 — A.** The order is raw materials → manufacturing → warehouse → distribution/retail → customer. A warehouse stores; a distribution center stores and moves goods onward, so they cannot be swapped; manufacturing cannot come before raw materials.

**Q18 — D.** A service supply chain is people + resources + delivery system. Services have no visible factory or warehouse, but they still have a chain — 'services have no supply chain' is the classic trap.

**Q19 — A.** A customer profile is a fictional description of one specific sample customer; a market segment is the group. Lin is one person, so 'segment' is wrong. Customer data is the raw information the persona is built from; a purchasing pattern is timing/frequency/quantity of buying.

**Q20 — A.** CAC = (marketing + advertising + sales costs) ÷ customers acquired = ¥12,000 ÷ 400 = ¥30. LTV = ¥150. Judge them together (LTV − CAC = ¥120 net): a higher CAC can still be the better buy when LTV grows faster, and CAC must never be judged on its own.

**Q21 — B.** Collecting and selling data without the customer's awareness is a privacy violation; a data breach is data exposed to outsiders. The business must balance the benefits of customer data against the risks — loss of customers, violation of core values, and damage to reputation.

**Q22 — C.** Low-involvement routine buying = habitual; a high-stakes comparison of alternatives = the rational decision-making process. Consequential purchases (a home, a laptop) trigger the rational process; small daily purchases do not.

**Q23 — C.** Colleagues set group norms → social and cultural factor (permanent expectations). Store atmosphere right now → situational influence (temporary, moment-specific). Personal factors are who the shopper is (age, income, lifestyle); psychological factors are what she thinks and feels.

**Q24 — C.** The limited quantity creates urgency → scarcity (honest use: real limited stock; manipulative misuse: a fake countdown timer). Consensus = 'many people bought it'; authority = 'an expert endorses it'; consistency = 'stay true to your past choice'.

**Q25 — B.** The assumption fed into the test is the hypothesis; randomly assigning real customers to two versions and comparing outcomes is an A/B test — a form of primary-source research. New data was collected (so not secondary); no survey was administered; and the business deliberately changed one variable, which is the opposite of observation.

**Q26 — B.** Surveying only classmates is sample bias — the sample is not representative of the target market. Monthly visits over time is a trend, best shown with a line graph; a pie chart shows part-to-whole shares. Percentages from a biased sample cannot be generalized.

**Q27 — D.** The order matters: desirability (do customers want it?) → feasibility (can it be built and produced?) → viability (can it profit?). This idea passes the first test but fails the second and third, so it should not be pursued. An existing prototype makes neither feasibility nor viability true.

**Q28 — D.** A prototype explores and tests the design (design stage); the MVP is the simplest version with only core features, released to real customers to test willingness to pay and product-market fit (validation stage). Slides rule of thumb: MVP feedback → Validation; prototype feedback → Design.

**Q29 — D.** Brand equity is the value the market assigns because customers recognize, trust and prefer the brand. A value proposition is the company's own claim about why its product is better; product messaging is the communication stage; market saturation is when most potential customers already own the product.

**Q30 — A.** Flat sales in a saturated market with heavy price competition = maturity; the strategy is to defend share through differentiation, lower prices and innovation. Introduction = low sales and awareness building; growth = rapidly rising sales and new entrants; decline = falling sales.
