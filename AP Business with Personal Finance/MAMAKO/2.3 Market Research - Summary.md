## 1. The Twenty Dollar Question (0:00)

A buyer stands in front of a **\$95 Trued Cycles commuter bike** and holds up his phone, where a similar bike on an online marketplace asks **\$75.** He wants to know why Theo's bike costs **\$20 more.** Theo points at the orange inspection tag (the signed 21-point checklist). The buyer shrugs and walks.

That night Theo writes one sentence in the notebook: *"an identical bike with a documented inspection tag sells for twenty dollars more, and faster."*

> Today that sentence is a **guess.** This topic is how a business turns a guess into **evidence** before it bets real money on being right.

---

## 2. What Market Research Is (1:14)

**Market research** = collecting detailed information about markets, products, and customer behavior so that **marketing decisions run on evidence.** (Theo's been collecting informally since line one of the notebook; this topic makes the collection **deliberate.**)

Everything he gathers lands in one of two families:

| | Quantitative data | Qualitative data |
|---|---|---|
| Form | **numerical** — can be measured and analyzed | **descriptive, non-numerical** — words and images |
| Answers | **how many / how much / how often** | **why / how** |
| Example | 7 bikes sold out of 9 | a parent saying the signature on the tag is what convinced her |

> A real decision usually needs **both** — the count shows **what happened**; the sentences **explain it.**

---

## 3. Desirable, Feasible, Viable (2:08)

Businesses developing a new product use market research to check **three things** before committing significant resources to a launch:

| Test | Means | Theo's case |
|---|---|---|
| **Desirable** | creates value because the solution fits a problem customers actually have → **problem-solution fit** | parents **can't tell a safe used bike from a dangerous one**; a documented inspection answers exactly that |
| **Feasible** | the business can produce/deliver it within its real **resources, technology, expertise, and time** | the 21-point inspection costs ~1 hour/bike + a printed checklist + a zip tie — his Saturdays can absorb it |
| **Viable** | a realistic **path to profit** in its market | if the tag truly earns \$20 extra for 1 hour of work, it's viable — **exactly the claim his research has to test** |

**Research keeps mattering after launch:** businesses use it to **track** customers' changing needs/wants, **spot openings** for product innovation, and **build strategies** that hold or grow market share. (Theo's notebook shows commuter demand climbing before the school year → tells him where his August rebuild hours should go.)

## 4. Secondary Sources First (3:26)

To learn about customers, competitors, and the wider landscape, businesses run **two kinds of research.**

### Secondary-source research

Gathers information that **already exists**, from external sources: **government, commercial, and academic publications and databases.**

- Exposes the outside forces shaping an opportunity — **PESTEL factors** (political, economic, social, technological, environmental, legal). *Example: a city announcing new bike lanes, or a school district cutting bus routes.*
- Can report **market size** (in dollars and total customers), **market trends**, **market segments**, and **factors driving customer decisions** — the information that signals whether a product could be viable.
- A **government census** can tell Theo how many families with school-age kids live within riding distance; a **commercial industry report** can estimate what the used-bike market turns over in a year.

### Theo's main secondary move (costs nothing)

One evening he sweeps **60 local marketplace listings** for used bikes and logs **three fields** per listing: **asking price, days listed, and whether the seller documents any inspection/service history.** That's **rival data** — interpreting it is how businesses assess the landscape around a product idea (reading existing data costs **far less** than generating new data).

> The sweep shows untagged commuter bikes clustering near **\$75**, and **no listing that documents an inspection.** Secondary research just **sized his landscape** — but it **cannot test his specific \$20 claim.** Testing claims is **primary work.**

### Primary-source research (the next section)

Generates **new data straight from customers.**

## 5. The Hypothesis and the A/B Test (5:19)

A **business hypothesis** states an **assumption** about a customer, a product, or a market, and a business tests that assumption before committing to a course of action. Theo's: *"an identical bike with a documented inspection tag sells for \$20 more, and faster."*

### The primary-research menu — the right method follows from the type of data the hypothesis needs

| Method | Data type | What it captures |
|---|---|---|
| **Surveys** | quantitative | a large amount, reflecting a **whole population's** views (how many parents would consider a used bike at all) |
| **Focus groups + one-on-one interviews** | qualitative | deep data from a **small number** of engaged customers; allow **follow-up questions** |
| **Experiments + observations** | behavior | what customers **actually do** (not say) — **controlled** setting (experiment) or **natural** setting (observation) |
| **A/B testing** | behavior | authentic responses to **two viable alternatives** in a **real-world** environment |

### The test (method chooses itself → A/B)

Across **two Saturdays**, Theo racks **17 comparable commuter bikes**:

- **9** carry the signed orange tag + **\$95** price
- **8** carry **no tag** + **\$75** price

By close on the second Saturday:

$$
\text{Tagged: } \frac{7}{9} \approx 78\% \text{ sold} \qquad \text{Untagged: } \frac{3}{8} \approx 38\% \text{ sold}
$$

> The tagged rack sold at **roughly double the rate while charging \$20 more** — authentic customer behavior in a real-world environment.

The numbers confirm the **what**; he still wants the **why** — so he runs **12 one-on-one parent interviews at pickup.** The follow-up questions do the real work: most parents point at the **signature line** specifically, and several say the tag **saved them the bike-shop inspection they would have paid for.**

> **Qualitative data just explained the quantitative result.**

---

## 6. Keeping the Data Honest (7:45)

Hypothesis testing can produce **skewed data.** Businesses reduce that risk in **two ways:**

1. **Design the study with a sample large enough, and populated appropriately, to reflect the population being studied.**
   - 17 bikes + 12 interviews is **small**, and everyone Theo talked to was already standing at a **flea-market stall** → his results describe **flea-market parents** and may **miss buyers who only shop online.**

2. **Ask unbiased questions in neutral phrasing** (wording can steer the answer):

| Biased | Neutral |
|---|---|
| *"Don't you feel safer buying a bike with a signed inspection tag?"* | *"What, if anything, influenced your decision today?"* |
| plants the answer inside the question | lets the parent reveal it |

> **Even careful research doesn't guarantee prediction.** Businesses routinely innovate and respond using **limited, unclear, or contradictory** data. (Theo's evidence covers two Saturdays at one market; a rainy weekend or a holiday crowd could have bent the numbers. He tags every bike going forward anyway — the evidence is strong and the notebook keeps the test running.)

## 7. Showing the Findings (9:03)

Research ends with **communication.** **Data visualizations** turn rows of data into patterns, trends, and insights stakeholders can grasp quickly → decisions rest on evidence.

> **The rule:** pick the chart type that **best shows the specific pattern** you mean to communicate.

### Four standard chart types

| Chart | Best for | Theo's example |
|---|---|---|
| **Bar chart** | compares **individual data points** | one bar per rack: 78% vs 38% sold |
| **Stacked bar chart** | compares a **total and its subcategories** at once | monthly sales split into \$60 / \$95 / \$150 tiers — shows whether the total grew *and* which tier grew it |
| **Line graph** | shows a **trend over time** | customers per month climbing toward the school year |
| **Pie chart** | shows a **part-to-whole** relationship | the share of a market's sales each business holds |

### Theo's most useful chart — two measures against each other (scatter plot)

Each of the **60 swept listings** becomes **one dot**: asking price on one axis, days listed on the other. The dots expose a **dead zone**:

> Undocumented bikes asking above **~\$85** sit unsold for weeks, while cheaper bikes clear steadily.

Set his A/B result beside the chart and the insight sharpens: **the market refuses a high price without proof**, and his tagged bikes cleared at **\$95** in two Saturdays. The chart shows the pattern the 60 notebook rows hide — and **that insight prices every bike he rebuilds from here.**

## 8. Exam Points the Video Emphasizes

- **Quantitative (how many/much/often) vs. qualitative (why/how)** — most decisions need **both**.
- **Desirable / Feasible / Viable** before a launch (desirable = problem-solution fit); research also tracks changes and spots openings after launch.
- **Secondary first** (government/commercial/academic sources): PESTEL forces, market size/trends/segments — costs far less than generating new data, but **can't test a specific claim.**
- **Primary menu:** surveys (quant, population) · focus groups/interviews (qual, small + follow-up) · experiments/observations (behavior, controlled vs. natural) · **A/B testing** (two alternatives, real-world).
- **Match the method to the data the hypothesis needs.**
- **A/B math:** 7/9 ≈ 78% vs 3/8 ≈ 38% → tagged sold ~2× the rate at +\$20.
- **Honest data:** sample large enough + representative; **neutral phrasing** (don't plant the answer).
- **Research ≠ guaranteed prediction** — decide on limited/unclear data.
- **Four chart types:** bar (compare points), stacked bar (total + subcategories), line (trend over time), pie (part-to-whole) — pick the one that best shows your pattern; a **scatter plot** reveals the dead zone two single measures hide.
