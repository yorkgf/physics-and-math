## 1. The Receipt (0:00)

Theo is 16 and rebuilds neglected bikes in his family's garage under the name **Trued Cycles.** His first customer is **Ricky, the candy seller from Unit 1**, who needs to haul crates to his soccer-park snack box. Ricky buys a **\$60 used bike with a cargo rack**, pays cash, and asks for a receipt.

Theo has no receipts, so he opens a **\$2 spiral notebook** and writes the sale as one line: **buy price, parts cost, hours, sale price.** He tapes the notebook inside the garage door and writes a line for every bike after that.

> One request for paperwork created a **ledger** — and a ledger full of buyers is **customer data.** This topic teaches Theo to read it.

---

## 2. What Marketing Is (1:12)

**Marketing** = all the activities a business undertakes to **identify customers' problems, needs, and wants, and to promote, sell, and deliver products that meet them.**

That covers **far more than advertising** — Theo studying local listings to see which bikes sell fast is marketing; so is pricing a finished bike, posting it, and handing it over with the seat set to the rider's height.

**Customer data feeds three decisions:**

1. Which **customers** the business should serve
2. Which **products** it should produce
3. **How** to market them effectively and profitably

Six weeks in, the notebook answers all three: its first line is the \$60 cargo bike, and the **six sales after it show the same buyer six times** — a parent purchasing a safe first commuter bike for a middle schooler. Six lines of ledger told Theo **who to serve, what to rebuild, and who his listings should speak to.**

---

## 3. Two Kinds of Customer Data (2:07)

| | Demographic characteristics | Psychographic characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | **measurable qualities** that describe a population | **cognitive and behavioral factors** within a population |
| Examples | age, sex, race, ethnicity, income, location | interests, activities, values, lifestyles |
| In the notebook | buyers in their late 30s–40s, budgets near \$90, homes a short drive from the Saturday flea market | parents who value **safety over price**, want to watch their kid ride before paying, respond to the **30-day tune-up promise** more than a discount |

That safety value explains the **tag**: every Trued bike leaves with a **signed orange tag** zip-tied to the handlebars, listing the **21-point safety inspection** the rebuild passed.

> **The sorting test:** if a **census form** could record the trait → **demographic**; if it describes **what a person cares about or how they spend their time** → **psychographic.**

## 4. Where the Data Comes From (3:18)

### Digital tools

| Tool | What it does | Theo's version |
|---|---|---|
| **Subscriber list** | records who asked to hear from the business | an order form where parents leave a name + number to hear when the next commuter bike is ready |
| **Online accounts** | store each customer's history with a business | (the marketplace he lists on) |
| **Click-tracking software** | records what visitors tap | (the marketplace does it — tells him 200 people viewed a bike that 5 messaged about) |
| **Tracking apps** | log where and when customers show up | (the marketplace does it) |
| **Social media monitoring** | reading what people say publicly about a product | rereading marketplace chat logs for the questions every parent asks |

> Theo runs none of the digital tools himself — the **online marketplace he lists on** runs them, which is how it can report views and messages he never tracked.

### Traditional tools

- **Survey** = the same short questions put to many customers.
- **Interview** = a longer conversation with one.
- Theo's version of both: the **two questions he asks at every pickup** — *"how did you find this bike?"* and *"what almost stopped you from buying it?"*

### Purchased data

Businesses also **buy customer data** from other businesses (a national parts retailer might pay a race organizer for its rider mailing list). Theo buys nothing — but the names in his own notebook are exactly the kind of asset the market trades.

## 5. Segments, Targets, and Renee (4:36)

**Market segmentation** aggregates potential customers into groups — **market segments** — based on **shared demographic and psychographic characteristics**, so a business can see what each group needs.

Theo's buyers sort into **three segments** that map onto the three tiers of his price rack:

| Segment | Buys | Cares about |
|---|---|---|
| Parents of younger kids | the **\$60** bikes | mostly working brakes |
| **Parents of middle schoolers** | the **\$95** commuters | safety, the test ride, the guarantee |
| Adult hobbyists | the **\$150** road bikes | the parts list (read line by line), negotiate hardest |

**Target customers** = the buyers **most likely to purchase** a specific product because their wants, needs, and preferences already point at it. Theo's notebook makes the choice: **6 of 6 sales sit in the commuter-parent segment**, so that segment gets his rebuild hours, stall space, and listing wording.

**Customer profile** = a **fictional description of a single sample customer** combining demographic data, psychographic data, and that person's wants, needs, and preferences. Theo writes his on an index card:

> **Renee, 41.** Wants a safe first commuter bike for her middle schooler. **Budget: \$90. Deadline: Saturday.** Values the test ride and the tune-up promise more than the price.

Renee is **fictional** — but every line on her card is backed by **six real buyers** in the notebook.

**Why narrow the aim?** Products, branding, pricing, and advertising designed for **one target population** usually work better and cost less than appeals to everyone. Two listings that cost the same to post:

- **Broad:** *"used bikes for sale, all sizes, good prices."*
- **Targeted:** *"inspected commuter bikes for middle schoolers, 21-point safety check on an orange tag, test rides Saturday, 30-day tune-up included."*

> The second answers **Renee's exact worries in order** — and Renee is the one holding the money.

## 6. Keeping the Customers You Win (6:49)

A sale to Renee is worth more if the **relationship outlives it.** Businesses build customer relationships with **three standard tactics:**

| Tactic | Definition | Theo's version |
|---|---|---|
| **Personalized service** | treats a customer as a known individual | the **30-day tune-up** — bike comes back within a month, he trues the wheels free, greets the kid by name |
| **Rewards programs** | give repeat buyers a reason to return | **\$10 off a sibling's bike** for any family already in the notebook |
| **Customer feedback opportunities** | (e.g., satisfaction surveys) ask buyers what to fix | his two pickup questions already do that job |

Social media and the internet make all three **cheaper to run** — with written permission, he posts a photo of each kid with their finished bike, and every post reminds past buyers Trued Cycles is still open.

### Relationships justify themselves through two numbers

**Customer acquisition cost (CAC)** = total marketing, advertising, and sales spending used to acquire customers, **divided by** the number of customers acquired.

Theo's first month at the flea market: he spends **\$20** (stall fees + photocopied flyers), and **4 buyers** come in through them.

$$
\text{CAC}=\frac{\$20}{4}=\$5 \text{ per customer}
$$

> Strong relationships push CAC **down**, because satisfied customers **refer** new customers — and a referred buyer arrives at **zero marketing cost.**

**Lifetime value of a customer (LTV)** = the estimated amount that customer will spend on the business's products **over time.** Theo tracks his version in **margin** (the part of the spending he keeps), so value and cost stay in the same units. Building **Renee's** number in three steps:

| Step | Value |
|---|---|
| Margin on her first bike | \$38 |
| Two paid tune-ups over the years the family owns it, at \$12 margin each | \$24 |
| A sibling bike later (~\$65), given a 40% chance → $0.40 \times 65$ | \$26 |

$$
\text{LTV (in margin)} = 38 + 24 + 26 = \$88
$$

> **\$88 of lifetime value in margin against \$5 of acquisition cost.** (By the strict definition — total spending over time — Renee's LTV is higher still; the margin version just keeps value and cost in the same units.) That gap is the **business case** for the free tune-up, the sibling discount, and the pickup photos — and satisfied customers **widen it further** through brand loyalty and repeat purchases.

## 7. What the Data Costs (9:30)

Every tactic in this topic runs on collected data — and **collection creates risk.** Theo's notes app holds **19 customer phone numbers** (some from the order form, some copied from marketplace chats). He never wrote down **why** he keeps them, and nothing on the form says **how long** a number stays or what else it might be used for.

**Collecting, compiling, and storing customer data can violate customers' privacy** — especially when people are **unaware** the collection is happening or **uninformed** about how the data may be used. The same problem scales: larger businesses hold search histories, purchase records, credit card numbers, social media posts, location trails.

**Unsecured data adds a second layer of risk:**

| Risk | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Data breach** | exposes stored information to outsiders |
| **Identity theft** | uses stolen personal details to impersonate a person |
| **Fraud** | uses deception (often powered by stolen data) to take money or property |

> Theo's whole database is **one phone carried through a flea market every Saturday** — one stolen bag hands a stranger 19 families' names + numbers plus chat logs showing which kid rides which bike home.

**The business side is a balance.** A business that collects, stores, and uses customer data weighs the benefits against the potential costs: **customers who leave when trust breaks, violations of the business's own core values, and harm to its reputation.** (Theo built Trued Cycles on a tag anyone can inspect — a data habit he couldn't explain at the stall would undercut the brand itself.)

**His fix — three steps:** **(1)** lock the phone · **(2)** delete every number that has no purpose · **(3)** add one line to the order form stating what gets kept and why.

## 8. Exam Points the Video Emphasizes

- **Marketing ≠ advertising** — it's identify + promote + sell + deliver; data feeds the *who / what / how* decisions.
- **Demographic (measurable, census-recordable) vs. psychographic (cares about / how they spend time)** — use the **sorting test**.
- **Data sources:** subscriber lists, online accounts, click-tracking, tracking apps, social media monitoring (digital); surveys + interviews (traditional); plus **purchased data**.
- **Segmentation → target customer → customer profile** (Renee): narrow the aim because targeted appeals work better and cost less.
- **Three relationship tactics:** personalized service, rewards programs, customer feedback opportunities.
- **CAC** = acquisition spending ÷ customers acquired (referrals push it toward zero).
- **LTV** = estimated spending over time — track in margin to compare with cost; $0.40 \times 65 = \$26$ shows the **expected-value** step.
- **Data risks:** privacy violation, data breach, identity theft, fraud — weigh benefits against trust/core-values/reputation costs.
- **Fix pattern:** lock storage → delete purposeless data → disclose what's kept and why.
