## 2. The Stimulus: Copper Kettle Candles (0:23)

> **Nadia Farouk** owns **Copper Kettle Candles**, pouring **small batches by hand** with **premium wax** and charging roughly **twice** what machine-made candles cost. Customers consistently praise the **quality** and the **custom scents.** Nadia buys wax from an **overseas supplier** whose prices run **20% below** the local option — but **two of the supplier's last six shipments arrived weeks late**, and one arrived with the **wrong wax blend.** A boutique chain has now offered Nadia a deal: it will feature Copper Kettle in all **twelve** of its stores **if she signs an agreement giving the chain exclusive rights to sell her candles at retail.**

Watch for the artifacts: an **artisan** process, a **supplier** decision (cost vs. risk), and a **barriers-to-entry** exclusive agreement.

## 3. Q1–Q3 (shared Copper Kettle scenario)

### Q1 (1:03) — classify the production approach

> Copper Kettle's production approach is best described as which?
>
> A. Mass production, because the candles reach many customers.  B. An artisan process, because it relies on skilled labor, attention to detail, and smaller quantities.  C. A service supply chain, because customers value the scents.  D. Outsourcing, because Nadia buys her wax from another business.

**Answer: B.** Artisan processes rely on **skilled labor and close attention to detail** and produce goods in **smaller quantities** — hand-poured small batches with custom scents fit **every clause.**
**Trap: D** — **confuses buying inputs with outsourcing a function.** Every producer buys raw materials from somebody; purchasing wax leaves the **actual production in Nadia's hands.** Outsourcing (Topic 1.7) hands an entire **function** to another business — and **nothing here has been handed off.**

### Q2 (2:11) — the supplier decision

> Which best describes the supplier decision Nadia faces?
>
> A. Switch suppliers, because cost is the only factor that matters.  B. Current supplier is better, because a 20% discount **always** outweighs late shipments.  C. Weigh the supplier's low cost against risks, including late deliveries, production errors, and the supplier's reputation.  D. Supplier choice is irrelevant to an artisan producer.

**Answer: C.** Businesses weigh **cost, quality, efficiency, convenience, and risk**; the overseas option is a **cost advantage carrying visible risks** — two late shipments and a wrong blend are **delivery delays and supplier errors already on the record**, and they threaten the quality her whole business stands on.
**Traps: A and B** — fail the **same way from opposite directions:** each crowns **a single factor** (cost *or* reliability) as the whole decision. The **"always" in B** is the absolutes tell.

> **A question that hands you one factor as the entire answer is handing you a wrong answer.**

### Q3 (3:35) — the exclusive retail agreement

> The boutique chain's proposed **exclusive retail agreement** is best understood as which?
>
> A. A supply chain choice that could support a competitive advantage through **barriers to entry.**  B. A move to mass production.  C. A way to reduce the cost of raw materials.  D. A **guarantee** that Copper Kettle's revenue will grow.

**Answer: A.** Businesses seeking competitive advantage through **barriers to entry** build supply chains with **exclusive or restrictive agreements** with suppliers or distributors — a retailer committing to feature one candle maker **while locking her products to its shelves** is that concept in action.
**Trap: D.** Twelve stores of visibility is a real benefit, and exclusivity is a **real cost** (every other retail door closes). Favorable deals **shift the odds and guarantee nothing** — and the word **"guarantee"** should ring the alarm it has rung **all unit long.**

## 4. Q4–Q6 (standalone)

### Q4 (4:44) — order the supply chain

> Which best identifies the order of the supply chain for a mass-produced good, **from start to finish**?
>
> A. Manufacturing, raw materials, retail store, warehouse, customer.  B. Raw materials, warehouse, manufacturing, customer, distribution center.  C. Distribution center, raw materials, manufacturing, warehouse, customer.  D. Raw materials, manufacturing, warehouse, distribution center or retail store, customer.

**Answer: D.** The chain runs in one direction: **raw materials → manufacturing → warehouse → distribution/retail → customer.**
**Trap: B** — **fails twice:** it puts the warehouse **before** manufacturing (the warehouse stores **finished** goods), and it ends with a distribution center **after the customer** — an order no reading of the chain allows.

### Q5 (6:01) — scaling table

**BatchBox Snacks doubled its production scale last year:**

| | Before scaling | After scaling |
|---|---|---|
| Units | 20,000 | 40,000 |
| Revenue | \$80,000 | \$160,000 |
| Total costs | \$60,000 | \$96,000 |
| **Profit** | **\$20,000** | **\$64,000** |

> Which conclusion is best supported by the table?
>
> A. Scaling failed, because total costs increased by \$36,000.  B. Scaling succeeded, because revenue grew 100% while costs grew only 60%, raising profit from \$20,000 to \$64,000.  C. Scaling **guarantees** higher profits every year going forward.  D. The business should return to its original scale.

**Answer: B.** Scaling aims for **revenue increases greater than cost increases** — so compute both growth rates:

- **Revenue:** \$80k → \$160k = **+100%**
- **Costs:** \$60k → \$96k = **+60%**
- **Profit:** \$20k → **\$64k** confirms it.

Revenue growth **outran** cost growth → that's the definition of scaling working.
**Traps:**

- **A reads one number and stops** — costs rose (as they typically do when production doubles); the question is whether revenue rose **faster**, which the two percentages answer.
- **C converts one good year into a permanent promise** — guarantee reasoning fails here **exactly as it fails everywhere else.**

### Q6 (7:52) — the service supply chain

> Which best describes the supply chain of an **online tutoring company**?
>
> A. Raw materials flowing to a manufacturing facility for assembly.  B. A warehouse storing finished lessons until customers order them.  C. Acquiring tutors, learning resources, and a virtual delivery platform to provide the service.  D. Services cannot have supply chains.

**Answer: C.** A **service supply chain** acquires the **employees, the resources, and the delivery systems** needed to provide the service (in person or virtually) — tutors, materials, and a video platform are those three parts named.
**Traps:**

- **B borrows the goods chain** — warehouses store **physical products**; a tutoring session is **delivered live.**
- **D is the misconception the topic exists to correct** — **services absolutely run supply chains**, built from people, resources, and delivery systems instead of trucks and pallets.

## 5. Trap Summary (patterns across all six — and the whole unit)

| # | Trap type | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Buying inputs ≠ outsourcing a function** — every producer buys raw materials; outsourcing hands a whole *function* to another business | Q1 |
| 2 | **One factor as the whole answer** (cost *or* reliability) + **"always"** — supplier choice weighs **all five factors** | Q2 |
| 3 | **"Guarantee"** in a favorable-deal scenario — deals shift odds, guarantee nothing (**the absolutes rule, all unit long**) | Q3 |
| 4 | **Out-of-order stages** — warehouse comes **after** manufacturing, never after the customer | Q4 |
| 5 | **Reading one number and stopping** (costs rose → "failed") — must **compare revenue growth vs. cost growth** | Q5 |
| 6 | **Permanent promise from one good year** ("guarantees") | Q5 |
| 7 | **Borrowing the goods chain for a service** (warehouse storing "finished lessons"); "services can't have supply chains" misconception | Q6 |

**Three cross-question tips:**

- **The absolutes rule (since 1.1 — confirmed "all unit long"):** "always / guarantee / never" is almost always wrong; favorable conditions **raise the odds and promise nothing.**
- **Good's chain order:** raw materials → manufacturing → **warehouse** → distribution/retail → customer.
- **Services DO have supply chains:** people, resources, delivery system — no factory, no warehouse.
