## 1. The Running Story: The Kid with the Backpack (0:00)

Every school has one: a student who shows up Monday with a backpack **half full of candy**. He bought it in bulk at a supermarket over the weekend, sells bars one at a time between classes for about **triple** what he paid, and by Friday the backpack is empty and he restocks.

The video says: **this student meets the course's complete definition of a business.** By the end you should be able to describe his whole operation in exam vocabulary. Topic 1.1 has two skills:

1. Describe how businesses address customers' problems, needs, and wants.
2. Tell the difference between value creation and value capture.

---

## 2. What a Business Is (0:54)

**Definition:** A business is an organization or entity that **produces and distributes products**. Products means **goods, services, or both**.

**Notice what the definition leaves out:** size, buildings, employees, legal paperwork. A business can be any size and can serve customers face-to-face or virtually.

**Scale examples (all are businesses):**

| Example | Scale |
|---|---|
| The kid with the backpack | Smallest |
| A single ice cream shop | Small shop |
| Mixue (蜜雪冰城, called "Mishe" in the video) — a Chinese chain that sells ice cream for under $1 and runs tens of thousands of stores | Huge |

> All three produce and distribute products, so all three are businesses. **Scale changes the numbers and never changes the definition** — the exam tests whether you know that.

**Goods vs Services:**

- **Goods** = tangible items (a candy bar, a phone, a desk).
- **Services** = work performed for compensation (a haircut, a tutoring session, a flight).
- Businesses often **sell both at once**: a phone comes with years of software support; a restaurant sells you food plus preparation plus the table.

> Classification tip: when a question asks you to classify, go by **what the customer is primarily paying for in the scenario as written**.

**Two money terms used throughout the course:**

- **Revenue** = money earned from selling, **before** expenses.
- **Profit** = what remains after **all** expenses are subtracted.

$$
\text{Profit}=\text{Revenue}-\text{Total Cost}
$$

For the candy seller: revenue is everything collected during the week; profit is what's left after the supermarket run and restocking. Most businesses exist to **earn profit** — profit is what lets them survive, grow, and reward their owners.

## 3. Customer vs. Consumer (3:05)

Two rules the exam tests constantly:

- **Customer** = the individual or business that **purchases** a good or service.
- **Consumer** = the individual who **uses** it, whether or not they bought it.

Buy yourself lunch and you hold both roles in one transaction.

**The key split (video scenario):** A student hands the candy seller $3 for **two** candy bars — one for himself, one for a friend in the next classroom.

- The buyer is the **customer for both bars**.
- The friend **consumes a bar he never purchased** → he is the consumer of that bar.

> **Purchase defines the customer; use defines the consumer.**

**The customer can be a business:** A school that purchases educational software is the customer; the students who use it in class are the consumers. Businesses, hospitals, and governments buy products every day that other people use.

**Exam tip:** Underline **who paid** and underline **who used**. If those are different parties, that difference is exactly what the question is testing.

**Practical consequence:** Businesses aim their messaging at **whoever makes the buying decision**. Software sold to schools is advertised on cost and administration features (because the school signs the payment), but the product design has to work for the student using it. **A business must satisfy both roles to keep selling** (more in Unit 2 on marketing).

## 4. Opportunities & Problem-Solution Fit (5:25)

Three definitions, then applied:

- **Need** = something essential for survival (food, water, shelter, basic healthcare).
- **Want** = improves life without being essential (a Netflix subscription, new sneakers, a candy bar).
- **Market opportunity** = a customer problem, need, or want that a business **might** be able to address. **Every business starts at one of these.**

**Problem-solution fit** = the match between a customer problem and a product built to solve it.

**The candy seller's fit:**

- **Problem:** students want a snack during the school day; vending machines are empty or off-limits; leaving campus is against the rules.
- **Solution:** candy available in the hallway at the exact moment the want occurs.
- That match is the reason he has customers at all.

**Same structure at scale — Uber / DiDi:** the problem was unpredictable taxis and unclear pricing; the product matched riders to drivers with an upfront price.

> **Strong fit raises what the solution is worth** to the person with the problem. Businesses usually reach it by testing, collecting feedback, and adjusting.

**A principle stated directly by the exam creators:** A business **cannot** satisfy all potential customers. So it must **select** which problems, needs, and wants to address and which customers to serve. Businesses make that selection with a short list of questions:

1. How severe is the problem?
2. How many people share it?
3. Can we solve it better than others can?
4. Will customers pay more than it costs us?
5. Can we reach them?

The candy seller passes all five within one hallway, and he **only works his own grade's hallway** — the selection principle in practice.

> **Exam tip:** an answer choice claiming a business should try to serve everyone is the **wrong answer**.

## 5. Value Creation vs. Value Capture (7:15)

The pair the topic is named for.

**Value** = the worth or benefit of a product to customers. The standard way to measure it = **the highest price the customer would willingly pay**.

> **Value rises as problem-solution fit improves.** The same candy bar is worth more during fifth period than next to a supermarket checkout.

**Value creation** = when a business provides a product that responds to customers' problems, needs, and wants. It describes **benefit flowing to the customer**. The candy seller creates value by making a snack available at the moment and place the want occurs.

**Value capture** = when a business charges a higher price for a product than it costs to produce and deliver. The formula is price charged minus cost.

$$
\text{Value Captured}=\text{Price Charged}-\text{Cost}
$$

> The video stresses: **the exam computes with exactly that formula.**

**The candy numbers:** He buys a 20-bar box for $10, so cost = $0.50/bar. He sells each bar for $1.50.

$$
\text{Capture}=1.50-0.50=\$1.00 \text{ per bar}
$$

**The customer side:** a student who would have paid $2 rather than go without pays $1.50, so the customer **keeps $0.50 of benefit** by his own measure. **Both sides end the trade better off** — which is why the trade happens voluntarily.

**Both halves are required, in that order:**

1. **Create value first** — a product that benefits nobody produces no lasting revenue at any price.
2. **Then capture some of that value.**

**What happens when it doesn't capture — the MoviePass case:** an American subscription service, roughly $10/month to see a movie in theaters every day. It paid theaters close to full price for many of the tickets its members used. So a member who watched four movies in a month cost the company **several times** the $10 that member actually paid — "price charged minus cost" was **negative**. And the heaviest users were exactly the people who signed up. Millions of subscribers, real value created, but the company **shut down in 2019** because it didn't actually capture any of the value it created.

> **Exam signal:** a scenario describing popularity plus prices below cost is describing **value creation without value capture**.

## 6. Value Proposition

A **value proposition** is a clear statement of how a product solves a customer problem and why customers should choose it over alternatives.

**Working template:**

> For **[target customer]** who has **[this problem]**, our product delivers **[this benefit]**. Unlike **[the alternative]**…

**The candy seller's version:**

> For students stuck on campus who want a snack now, a candy bar in the hallway for $1.50. Unlike a vending machine that's empty or a store they can't go to.

One sentence naming the **customer, the problem, the solution, and the edge**.

---

## 7. Exam Points the Video Emphasizes

- **Business definition is about what it does, not its size** — the backpack kid, an ice cream shop, and Mixue are all businesses.
- **Classifying goods/services:** go by what the customer is primarily paying for in the scenario.
- **customer vs consumer:** underline who paid and who used; if they differ, that's the test.
- **Value capture formula:** the exam uses $\text{Price Charged}-\text{Cost}$ directly.
- **"Serve everyone" is a wrong answer** — businesses must select.
- **Popularity + price below cost = value creation without value capture** (e.g., MoviePass).
