## 2. The Stimulus: Cobalt Finch Ceramics (0:27)

> **Ivy Santos**, a high school senior, runs **Cobalt Finch Ceramics**, a stall selling handmade mugs at a monthly craft fair. **Three other stalls sell mugs**, so shoppers can meet the same want **four ways** and weigh price, glaze, and feel before choosing. Behind her table Ivy pins a **corkboard of short notes from past buyers** describing their mugs in daily use (**two dozen** so far). At the front edge sits a **dish of small glazed clay magnets, free** to anyone who stops. The vendor at the next stall urges her to tell shoppers **each design is the last one available** — including designs with **duplicates boxed under the table.** Ivy declines the line, and letters a small card reading **"one of a kind"** for the three mugs whose copper glaze she cannot repeat.

Watch for the artifacts: **consensus** (the corkboard), **reciprocity** (the free magnets), and **scarcity** (the honest "one of a kind" card vs. the faked line).

## 3. Q1–Q3 (shared Cobalt Finch scenario)

### Q1 (1:14) — the corkboard of buyer notes

> The corkboard of buyer notes is best identified as a sales tactic built on which principle of influence?
>
> A. **Authority**, because the notes come from experienced mug owners.  B. **Liking**, because the notes make the stall feel friendly.  C. **Consensus**, because displayed customer feedback signals that many buyers like the product.  D. **Unity**, because reading the notes makes shoppers part of a group.

**Answer: C.** Consensus: individuals **follow the norms of their social group** → promoting positive customer feedback creates the sense that **most buyers like the product.** Two dozen notes from satisfied strangers tell a browsing shopper that **people like her already chose these mugs.**
**Traps:**

- **A** — the test is **expertise.** Authority persuades through **experts and credentials** (a doctor endorsing, a certificate on a wall); Ivy's note writers **claim nothing beyond owning a mug.**
- **D** — overreads **membership.** Unity works when customers feel **part of a group** (exclusive communities, co-creation); reading strangers' notes **confers no membership.**

> **Consensus = what many buyers did. Authority = what a credentialed expert says. Unity = makes the buyer a member.**

### Q2 (3:00) — the dish of free magnets

> Which best explains how the dish of free magnets can increase mug sales?
>
> A. Receiving an item of value makes shoppers feel **obligated to give something back**, often a purchase.  B. The magnets make shoppers **like Ivy**, and people buy from sellers they like.  C. The magnets appeal to shoppers' **self-image** as supporters of young artists.  D. The magnets make the mugs seem **rarer** than the rival stalls' mugs.

**Answer: A.** **Reciprocity:** individuals feel obligated to reciprocate when given something of value (why marketers offer free samples, trials, gifts). A shopper who pockets a free magnet carries a small felt debt to the stall — **buying a mug settles it.**
**Traps:**

- **B** names a real **side effect** but the **wrong engine**: liking persuades through **rapport/relatability**; a giveaway's tested mechanism is the **obligation** it plants.
- **C** describes **consistency** (people repeat behavior matching their self-image); a magnet in a dish makes **no identity claim.**

> **A free item persuades through the obligation it creates = reciprocity.**

### Q3 (4:25) — refuse the line, keep the cards

> Which best explains Ivy's decision to refuse the vendor's line while keeping the one-of-a-kind cards?
>
> A. Scarcity persuades **only** when the seller invents the shortage, so the cards cannot motivate buyers.  B. Refusing the line means Ivy has **given up** the scarcity principle entirely.  C. Scarcity claims are **prohibited** at in-person markets.  D. A **true claim of rarity** applies the scarcity principle honestly, while the false line risks deception and lost trust.

**Answer: D.** Scarcity runs on **perceived rarity**; **real rarity produces that perception honestly** — so the card on an unrepeatable glaze keeps the persuasive pull **and** keeps the claim true. The false version invites the deception **consumer protection laws exist to catch**, and at a monthly fair with returning shoppers, **one duplicate spotted in a box undoes months of corkboard trust.**
**Traps:**

- **A** — the tell is the **absolute.** Nothing in the principle requires **invention**; a genuine shortage is the **cleanest** source of perceived rarity.
- **B** — fails on the stimulus: Ivy **still labels** the three unrepeatable mugs, so the principle stays in use **where it is true.**

> **A true rarity claim applies the same principle as a false one — and costs nothing when checked.**

## 4. Q4–Q6 (standalone)

### Q4 (6:07) — purchasing-pattern table

**Bluestem Coffee** (drive-through) pulls one member's loyalty-app records:

| | Week 1 | Weeks 2–4 |
|---|---|---|
| Visits | 5 | 5 each |
| Window | 7:10–7:25 am | **same** |
| Order | 1 medium coffee | **same** |
| Price | \$4 each | **same** |

> Which conclusion is supported by the table?
>
> A. The member **systematically evaluates** rival coffee options before each visit.  B. The purchases form a **habitual purchasing pattern** — consistent in timing, frequency, and quantity — totaling **\$80** across the four weeks.  C. The member's **lifetime value** to Bluestem is **\$80**.  D. The app **guarantees** the member will keep buying five coffees a week.

**Answer: B.** A purchasing pattern is a consumer's typical routine, read through **timing, frequency, quantity** — all three steady here:

$$
5 \text{ visits} \times 4 \text{ weeks} = 20 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 20 \times \$4 = \$80
$$

> Small routine buys run on **habit**; a rational process of comparing alternatives fits **consequential** purchases (a home) and costs **time.** The loyalty app = the **technology** layer changing how consumers shop and what businesses can see.

**Traps:**

- **A** claims **deliberation** the table contradicts — a shopper comparing rivals would show **varied stops and varied orders.**
- **C** borrows last topic's vocabulary: LTV estimates total spending across the **whole** customer relationship; this table shows **one month** of it.

> **A conclusion the table cannot compute is never supported.**

### Q5 (8:16) — the straw ordinance

> A city ordinance bars restaurants from handing out single-use plastic straws. Within a year, local stores stock **paper and metal straws**, and reusable-straw sales climb. Which best explains the change in purchasing patterns?
>
> A. **Legal restrictions can give rise to substitute products** as consumers find new ways to meet an existing need.  B. The ordinance **eliminated the need** the product served, so demand disappeared.  C. The change is a **situational influence**, because store layouts shifted.  D. **Consumer protection laws require** every banned product to be replaced by a licensed alternative.

**Answer: A.** When a law makes a product **harder to obtain, the want it served survives** → consumers find **substitutes** and businesses arrive to sell them. (The straw leaves, the drink stays; paper and metal fill the gap.)
**Traps:**

- **B** breaks the chain at the **wrong link**: the ordinance changed **availability**, and the underlying want persisted **untouched.**
- **C** borrows a **neighboring factor family**: situational influences are **temporary** (noise, lighting, timing); an ordinance is a **legal factor** that **holds until repealed.**

> **When the law removes a product, the need remains, and substitutes appear to serve it.**

### Q6 (9:58) — the candle shop

> A candle shop plays **quiet music**, keeps the **lighting warm**, and sets **open testers** by the entrance so shoppers smell the candles immediately. Shoppers who came in to browse frequently leave with a purchase. Which best identifies the factor influencing these purchases?
>
> A. A **personal** factor (occupation and budget).  B. A **social and cultural** factor (peers set the norm).  C. A **situational influence** (store design creates temporary conditions that encourage buying).  D. A **psychological** factor (the store changes each shopper's values and beliefs).

**Answer: C.** Situational influences = **temporary conditions** that encourage/discourage buying, created by **store design** (noise, lighting, organization), timing, and product availability. The music, warmth, and testers work on the shopper **only while she stands inside them.**
**Trap: D** — the test is **what changed:** the store adjusted the shopper's **surroundings for twenty minutes**, and her **values and beliefs walked out the same as they walked in.**

> **The four-family sorting key:**
>
> | Family | Where it lives |
> |---|---|
> | **Personal** | **who** the buyer is (age, income, budget, occupation, lifestyle) |
> | **Psychological** | **what** the buyer thinks (values, beliefs, perceptions, learning, motivation) |
> | **Social & cultural** | **who surrounds** the buyer (peers, family, status, norms, media) |
> | **Situational** | **where and when** the buying happens (store design, timing, availability — temporary, not product features) |

## 5. Trap Summary (patterns across all six)

| # | Trap type | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sort principles by **mechanism**: authority = expert/credentials, consensus = many buyers' behavior, unity = membership | Q1 |
| 2 | **Reciprocity vs. liking vs. consistency** — the engine is the **obligation** planted, not rapport or identity | Q2 |
| 3 | **Same principle, honest vs. manipulative** — true rarity = scarcity too; "only when invented" = absolute tell | Q3 |
| 4 | Data table = **habitual pattern** (timing/frequency/quantity); one month ≠ **lifetime value** | Q4 |
| 5 | **Laws create substitutes; the need persists;** an ordinance is **legal**, not **situational** | Q5 |
| 6 | **Situational vs. psychological** — what *changed*? surroundings for 20 min ≠ values/beliefs | Q6 |

**Three cross-question tips:**

- **Sort the seven principles by mechanism** (expert, many buyers, membership, obligation, self-image, perceived rarity, rapport) — the *word* in the answer is unreliable; the *engine* decides.
- **Honest vs. manipulative use the same principle** — the **face test** (does it survive full disclosure?) decides which is which.
- **Four influence families** sort by *where they live*: personal = who, psychological = what they think, social = who surrounds, situational = where/when (temporary, **not** product features).
