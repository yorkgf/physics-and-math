## 1. The Notebook (0:00)

Saturday morning at the soccer league, and the candy seller watches the **sidelines** instead of the game. He notices:

- A dad juggles a folding chair, a phone, and two juice boxes — and drops all three.
- A coach begs the group chat for a snack-duty volunteer for the **third week in a row.**
- A forgotten cooler sits in a trunk two parking lots away.

He writes each one down in a **pocket notebook**, because every line is a problem somebody would pay to make disappear. **That notebook is where businesses come from.**

---

## 2. The Entrepreneur (1:01)

**Entrepreneur** = an individual who **develops a new business and assumes its risks and potential rewards.**

> The candy seller has been one since 1.1 — he built the hallway business, ate the losses in the price war, kept the profits when there were profits to keep. **Assuming the risk is what separates an entrepreneur from someone with a suggestion.**

Ideas begin as **opportunities**: an opportunity = a **customer problem, need, or want** that a business could address with a solution. The notebook is a list of opportunities.

---

## 3. Where Ideas Come From (1:45)

Three common strategies for finding opportunities:

### Watch and ask

Observe potential customers, **interview** them, and **survey** them.

- Sideline notebook = **observation**
- Asking five parents what they wish existed = **interview**
- A one-question poll in the team group chat = **survey**

### Research

- **Market research** gathers information about customers, competitors, and the market; it can reveal **market gaps** — unmet customer needs that no existing product currently serves.
- **Technical research** answers a different question: whether a solution can **actually be built and delivered** with the technology available.

### Experiment

Businesses develop new capabilities by **trying things**; some of the best product ideas surface **while building something else.**

> None of this is limited to startups — **established businesses run the same three plays** to find their next product.

**Each tool earns a different kind of truth:**

| Tool | What it catches |
|---|---|
| Observation | What people **actually do** (people do surprising things they never report) |
| Interviews | The **why** behind the behavior, one customer at a time, in depth |
| Surveys | **How many** — turning a hunch into a number |

> **A market gap can hide inside a market that looks full:** a town with three pizza places and **no late-night delivery** still holds an unmet need, because gaps live in **convenience, price, or timing** as often as in the product itself.

## 4. Risk and Reward (3:26)

Bringing a new product to market **incurs risk** because it consumes **financial, physical, and human resources**, and each has a cost. There is **no guarantee** the product will generate enough revenue to cover those costs, let alone earn a profit.

> That sentence is true at **every scale** — it covers the candy seller's savings *and* a corporation betting a billion dollars on a new device.

His version: the cooler and vendor permit cost real money, inventory ties up more, and every Saturday behind a table is a Saturday he can't spend another way. **If parents don't buy, the money and the time are simply gone.**

**Why accept the risk? Three honest reasons:**

1. The **potential to earn future profits.**
2. The **satisfaction of solving a problem** (he felt it in 1.2, when the heat-wave line formed at his cooler).
3. The ability to **pursue a passion** — doing work you'd choose even when the profit is thin.

> Most entrepreneurs run on a **mix of all three.** An exam scenario can hinge on any one of them, so **treat the three as equals.**

## 5. Design Thinking: Validate the Problem (4:42)

The **entrepreneurial design-thinking process** is a **repeatable method** for generating and validating an idea.

**Stage 1 — observe, interview, survey.** The goal is to **identify and validate a problem, need, or want.**

> **Validation at this stage means gathering evidence for three things:** (1) the problem **exists**, (2) it can be **clearly defined**, and (3) it is experienced by **multiple** potential customers.

One dad dropping juice boxes is an **anecdote.** So he asks: over two Saturdays he talks to **20 parents and 4 coaches**, and **17 parents** describe the same halftime scramble — kids need snacks/drinks mid-game, nobody remembers whose turn it is, and the nearest store is a 10-minute drive. Now the problem is **validated**: real, clearly defined, and shared by many.

---

## 6. Develop a Solution (5:44)

With a validated problem, the entrepreneur develops a **potential solution** (the product idea). This stage runs on **brainstorming, sketching, and prototyping.**

- **Brainstorming** generates a wide list of ideas **without stopping to judge any of them** — judging too early kills the strange ideas that sometimes win.
- His list: a full snack stand at every field · a wagon delivering field to field · a **pre-ordered team snack box** picked up at halftime.
- The stand needs money he doesn't have; the wagon needs time he doesn't have → the **snack box wins on fit.**
- **Sketch:** one box per team, ordered in the group chat by Friday night, handed to the coach at halftime.
- **Prototype** = an early, simple version of the product built to explore the idea and gather feedback. His first prototype: a **cardboard box with a taped-on menu**, packed for an imaginary team.

---

## 7. Validate the Idea with an MVP (6:46)

A sketch convinces the **builder**; evidence has to come from **customers.** So the final stage validates the idea by gathering feedback on a **minimum viable product (MVP).**

- **MVP** = the **simplest** version of the product idea, carrying only its **core features.** It can be as simple as a sketch, a written description, or a model.
- **"Minimum" trips students every year — read it as a count of features.**
- The MVP puts the **core** of the idea in front of customers, because that core is the one thing being tested — a **sloppy or cut-rate version would poison** the very feedback it exists to collect.
- His MVP: **one box, one team, one Saturday** (order form in the group chat, drinks and snacks inside, handoff at halftime).

**Business hypothesis** = a **testable assumption** the MVP will confirm or reject. His: *"at least five families on the team will pre-order at \$2 each."*

> **A useful hypothesis is specific enough to fail.** "Five families at \$2" can be proven wrong **in public**; a vague hope that "people will like it" can wriggle out of any result.

Saturday: **seven families order.** Two ask for fruit instead of candy; one coach asks for a flat team price. That's **validation working exactly as designed** — evidence the idea solves a real problem, **plus feedback he never would have guessed**, gathered **before any serious money is at risk.**

## 8. The Cheap Test at Any Size (9:10)

> **Exam habit: validation appears TWICE in this process**, and questions love to test whether you noticed.

| Validation | Target | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| First | The **problem** | Evidence it exists, can be defined, and is shared by many |
| Second | The **idea** | Customer feedback on the MVP |

- A scenario full of **interviews with no product in sight** = validating the **problem.**
- A scenario where customers **react to something they can see or try** = validating the **idea.**

**Scale changes nothing structural.** An established snack company testing a new flavor bakes a **small batch**, stocks it in a **dozen stores** for a month, and reads the sales before committing a factory line. That small batch is an **MVP wearing a corporate badge**; the dozen stores are the interview. Existing businesses run design thinking for the same reason the candy seller does:

> **The cheap test protects the expensive bet.**

**The process earns its keep when the answer is "no."** If one family had ordered, the hypothesis fails — and a failed hypothesis at the MVP stage costs **one Saturday and one cardboard box.** The same discovery made **after buying a trailer of inventory** costs everything those resources cost.

> Research and testing **reduce uncertainty** (some risk survives every test), so the method is: **make the expensive bets only after the cheap tests pass.** Feedback goes straight back into the product — fruit joins the box next week, and the flat team price gets a small test of its own.

---

## 9. Exam Points the Video Emphasizes

- **Entrepreneur** = develops a new business **and assumes its risk** — risk assumption is the defining line.
- **Opportunity** = a customer problem/need/want a business could address.
- **Three idea-finding strategies:** watch & ask (observe/interview/survey), research (market + technical), experiment.
- **Market gaps hide** in convenience, price, and timing — not just the product itself.
- **Three reasons to accept risk:** future profit, satisfaction of solving, pursuing a passion — treat as equals.
- **Design-thinking stages:** validate the **problem** → develop a **solution** (brainstorm/sketch/prototype) → validate the **idea** with an **MVP**.
- **MVP "minimum" = a count of features**, not a sloppy version.
- **Two validations:** problem-validation (interviews, no product) vs. idea-validation (customers react to an MVP).
- **Hypothesis must be specific enough to fail.**
- **Cheap tests first, expensive bets only after they pass** — true at any scale.
