## 2. The Stimulus: Bright Thread (0:21)

> **Rosa Delgado** owns **Bright Thread**, a clothing alteration and repair shop. Posted **core values:** craftsmanship, honesty, community. Bright Thread is known across the neighborhood for the **fastest turnaround of any tailor** — a strength built on Rosa's **custom scheduling system.** Two framed statements hang by the register:
>
> 1. *"Clothing that lasts, in a neighborhood that thrives."*
> 2. *"Bright Thread repairs and alters clothing with same-week service at prices working families can afford."*
>
> Rosa is weighing two expansions: (1) **reselling inexpensive imported garments she has not inspected** at a high markup, or (2) **launching a same-day uniform repair service** for local businesses.

Note the artifacts hidden in the scenario: a **competency** (fastest turnaround), a **value** set (craftsmanship/honesty/community), a **vision** (statement 1), and a **mission** (statement 2).

## 3. Q1–Q3 (shared Bright Thread scenario)

### Q1 (1:14) — classify the fastest turnaround

> Bright Thread's fastest-in-the-neighborhood turnaround is best classified as which?
>
> A. A core value  B. A vision statement  C. A social objective  D. A core competency

**Answer: D.**
**Why:** Core competencies are capabilities/skills/expertise that help a business **outperform rivals** and achieve competitive advantage. Fastest turnaround is a capability with the **rival comparison built right into the phrase**, resting on a specific skill — Rosa's scheduling system.
**Trap: A** — the **paired-concept swap** for this topic. Core **values** are beliefs/principles (the craftsmanship, honesty, community posted on the wall).

> **A value is what the business *believes*; a competency is what it is *good at*.** Speed here is a **built skill** → competency.

### Q2 (2:19) — how values + competencies should guide the choice

> Which best explains how Rosa's core values and core competencies should influence her choice between the two expansions?
>
> A. Choose the resale option, because higher markups increase profit fastest.  B. Favor the option that aligns with her values and uses her existing strengths — the same-day uniform service.  C. Pursue both options, because businesses grow by accepting every opportunity.  D. Core values apply only to employees, so they shouldn't affect an owner's decision.

**Answer: B.** Businesses consider core competencies when deciding which opportunities to pursue and how to allocate resources, and choose actions **consistent with their values.** The uniform service runs directly on her speed + scheduling system **and** serves the community her values name; reselling uninspected garments strains craftsmanship + honesty while using **none** of her strengths.
**Traps:**

- **A** — businesses *do* seek profit, but competencies and values still guide **which** opportunities: a high-margin move that abandons strengths and beliefs **risks the reputation the profits depend on.**
- **C** fails on **resources** — one shop cannot fund and staff every opportunity; **choosing well is the entire skill being tested.**

### Q3 (3:54) — classify statement 1

> The first framed statement — *"clothing that lasts, in a neighborhood that thrives"* — is best classified as which?
>
> A. A vision statement, because it expresses the shop's values and aspirations.  B. A mission statement, because it describes the shop's daily operations.  C. A code of conduct for employees.  D. A business hypothesis to be tested.

**Answer: A.** A vision statement is a concise description of **core values and aspirations** — this names **a future the shop wants to exist** (durable clothing, thriving neighborhood) and describes **no operations.** (Statement 2 is the **mission:** what it does — repairs/alterations — and how — same-week service at fair prices.)
**Trap: B** — the **vision-mission swap.**

> **Run the test:** a **future worth wanting** = vision; a **description of the work** = mission.

## 4. Q4–Q6 (standalone)

### Q4 (4:58) — why communicate core values?

> Which best explains why businesses communicate core values to their employees?
>
> A. To **guarantee** that no employee ever behaves unethically.  B. To increase revenue in the following quarter.  C. To align employees to a shared purpose and guide decisions consistent with the business's beliefs.  D. To satisfy a legal requirement that businesses publish their values.

**Answer: C.** Businesses communicate core values to **align employees** to a shared purpose and **guide decision makers** toward choices consistent with the business's beliefs.
**Traps:**

- **A** uses **"guarantee"** — the absolutes rule: values **guide** choices; **no statement on a wall guarantees behavior.**
- **D** borrows **legal-factor thinking from Topic 1.3** — no law requires a business to publish core values.

### Q5 (6:03) — financial data

**Harbor Kitchen's year in numbers:**

| Line item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue from meal sales | \$180,000 |
| Grant funding | \$60,000 |
| Donations | \$40,000 |
| Total costs | \$250,000 |

> The **\$30,000 surplus** was spent expanding the organization's **free cooking classes**, and **no payment went to any owner.** Based on the table, Harbor Kitchen is best classified as which?
>
> A. A traditional business, because it earns revenue from selling meals.  B. A nonprofit organization, because its surplus is reinvested in its mission and it relies partly on grants and donations.  C. A social enterprise, because it sells meals and offers free classes.  D. A failing organization, because its costs exceeded its sales revenue.

**Answer: B.** Every marker points the same direction: **grants and donations** alongside sales revenue, the surplus went **back into the mission**, and **no owner received a payment.**

> **Surplus reinvestment is the legal signature of a nonprofit.**

**Traps:**

- **C** is close — the tell is the **destination of the money:** a social enterprise seeks profit **for its owners** alongside social objectives; Harbor Kitchen pays **no owner anything**, so the social-enterprise label **overexplains** what the nonprofit definition already covers.
- **D reads one row and stops:** sales alone fall short of costs, but **total revenue is \$280,000 against \$250,000** = a \$30,000 surplus. **A nonprofit running a surplus is healthy, legal, and common.**

### Q6 (7:55) — social enterprise vs. traditional business

> Which best distinguishes a social enterprise from a traditional business?
>
> A. A social enterprise intentionally pursues social objectives through its products, operations, or financial model while still seeking profit.  B. A social enterprise is legally required to reinvest all surplus funds into the organization.  C. A social enterprise must be owned by a charity.  D. A social enterprise **always** donates the **majority** of its profits.

**Answer: A.** A social enterprise seeks profit while also achieving social objectives; impact travels through **three channels:** products, operations, or financial model.
**Traps:**

- **B** borrows the **nonprofit's legal rule** — the **neighboring-concept trap one question after you just used that rule correctly.** Reinvestment of **all** surplus defines nonprofits; social enterprises **keep real profit.**
- **D** fails on **"always"** and **"majority"** — a fixed donation share is **one possible** financial model, and nothing about the category requires it.

## 5. Trap Summary (patterns across all six)

| # | Trap type | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Paired-concept swap:** core value (believes) vs. core competency (good at) | Q1 |
| 2 | High-margin move that abandons strengths/values **risks reputation**; "pursue every opportunity" fails on **resources** | Q2 |
| 3 | **Vision-mission swap:** a future worth wanting vs. a description of the work | Q3 |
| 4 | **Absolutes** ("guarantee") + borrowing **legal-factor** thinking from Topic 1.3 | Q4 |
| 5 | **Nonprofit vs. social enterprise:** the tell is the **destination of the money** (no owner paid = nonprofit) | Q5 |
| 6 | **Reading one row and stopping:** sum ALL revenue (sales + grants + donations) before judging profit/surplus | Q5 |
| 7 | **Neighboring-concept trap:** reusing the nonprofit's reinvest-all-surplus rule for a social enterprise; "always/majority" absolutes | Q6 |

**Three cross-question tips:**

- **Two swap pairs to hold cold:** value (believes) vs. competency (good at); vision (future) vs. mission (work).
- **Absolutes rule (since 1.1):** "guarantee / always / never / majority" is almost always the wrong answer.
- **Organization decision tree:** owners keep profit? → **No** + surplus to mission = **nonprofit**; **Yes** + built-in social objective = **social enterprise**; **Yes** + no social objective = **business**. And never judge a surplus from sales revenue alone — add grants and donations.
