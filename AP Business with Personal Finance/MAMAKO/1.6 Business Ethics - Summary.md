## 1. The Hot-Trunk Chocolate (0:00)

Friday night before game day. The week's chocolate order sat in a **hot car trunk for 5 days**; every bar has melted and hardened again with a **pale gray "bloom"** across the surface. Still safe to eat, still sealed, **noticeably worse** (dull, crumbly, stale at the edges). **Nobody would know until after they paid.**

His cousin shrugs: *"Sell it. Nobody checks a chocolate bar."* Tomorrow's families would hand over real money for a product he knows is damaged — and **the only person who can decide what happens next is the owner.**

---

## 2. Unethical Behavior and Incentives (0:55)

**Unethical behavior in business includes three named forms:**

| Form | Example in the story |
|---|---|
| **Falsifying or concealing information** | Selling the bloom chocolate without a word (customer pays believing something the seller knows is false) |
| **Misusing company property** | A cousin handing free drinks to his friends from the cooler |
| **Causing harm to employees or customers** | A stand ignoring a wobbling table until it collapses on a customer |

> Unethical behavior can occur at **every level** of a business — from the newest employee to the owner.

**Incentive structures** can push people toward unethical choices that benefit themselves.

> An **incentive** = a reward or motivation that encourages a particular action.

- Pay the cousin **per box sold** → every damaged bar he hides earns him money.
- Pay a salesperson **only on commission** → the incentive rewards the sale and ignores the honesty.

> **A business that rewards the wrong behavior manufactures its own ethics problems.** Leaders design incentives with the behavior they will produce in mind. The same force pushes the *right* way when the design is right: pay the cousin a **flat rate + a small bonus tied to family satisfaction** (not boxes moved) → the incentive now rewards the honesty. **The difference was a design choice the owner controlled.**

---

## 3. Four Tools (2:31)

Businesses use four tools to **encourage ethical behavior:**

| Tool | What it is | In the story |
|---|---|---|
| **Code of conduct** | A formal, **written** set of expectations/guidelines for behavior inside the organization | The three values on the cooler lid grow up into written rules: damaged stock gets disclosed, free product goes to nobody, cash gets counted twice |
| **Training** | Employees **learn** the guidelines and **practice** what to do before the situation is live | The cousin hears the damaged-stock rule on a calm Tuesday — not live on a Friday night with money on the table |
| **Internal repercussions** | **Consequences** inside the business for crossing the line | Employees measure a rule by **what happens when someone breaks it** |
| **Modeling** | **Leaders demonstrate** the behavior themselves | Employees copy what the owner **does** long before they memorize what the owner **wrote** |

> **Modeling is the linchpin:** if he sells the bloomed bars, **no code of conduct** will convince his cousin that honesty matters here.

---

## 4. Why Ethics Pays (3:34)

Why invest in all four tools? Because **ethical practices can attract customers and employees and contribute to brand loyalty.** A business's **response to an ethical failure** shapes its customer/employee relationships, public perception, and profitability.

**The hallway arithmetic:** selling the damaged chocolate earns **~\$40** this weekend. But one parent bites a stale bar, posts a photo in the team group chat, and the trust built across three topics **drains out in an afternoon** — customers stop pre-ordering, the league remembers, and the far-field table he earned in 1.5 goes to somebody else.

> **\$40 today against every Saturday that follows.** Run that comparison before calling the concealment profitable.

The pull works on **workers, too:** ethical businesses attract employees along with customers. The best helper at the park wants to work somewhere that will **never ask him to lie to a customer** — when the snack box hires beyond family, **its reputation does half the recruiting.**

---

## 5. The Ethical Dilemma (4:49)

An **ethical dilemma** exists when a **core value conflicts with other core values** *or* with the **business's goals and practices.**

> The bloom chocolate is **barely a dilemma** — honesty points one direction, and only profit argues back. **Real dilemmas pinch from both sides.**

**Value vs. value:**

- **Transparency vs. empathy:** disclosing the damage means the weekend earns nothing, and the cousin was counting on his share to replace a broken bike lock. Transparency toward **customers** collides with empathy toward the **person who works for you.**
- **Honesty vs. a promise:** a caterer who promised a supplier confidentiality discovers the supplier's prices **gouge a fellow small business** — honesty toward a peer pulls against a promise already made.

> The exam expects you to **name both values before judging any response** to one.

**Value vs. business goal:** his fair-prices pledge meets a cost spike (wholesale chocolate jumps 20%). Holding prices **honors the pledge** but eats the margin; raising prices **protects the profit goal** but strains the pledge. **Neither option is dishonest,** and both cost something the business cares about.

> **Dilemma vs. temptation — the key split:**
>
> - A scenario with a **clean right answer and a profitable wrong one** = a test of **ethical behavior.**
> - When **every option wounds something the business values** = an **ethical dilemma,** and the question asks how **leaders respond.**

---

## 6. Stakeholders (6:38)

Before a leader can weigh a dilemma, they need the **full list of people it touches** — split in two:

| | Internal stakeholders | External stakeholders |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | **Direct involvement** in the business's operations, decisions, outcomes | **Not directly employed** by the business, but hold a **vested interest** in its decisions |
| Examples | Owners, managers, employees | Customers, government agencies, community members |
| Snack box | The candy seller + his cousin | The families who buy, the league (permit), the park office (hosts games) — **and suppliers** (the wholesale parent holds a real stake without working a Saturday) |

> Every option lands somewhere on that map. **A decision that only counts the internal column misses half its consequences.**

---

## 7. How Leaders Respond (7:39)

Leaders use **two methods** to choose:

### Method 1 — weigh benefits and costs across stakeholders

Evaluate the **benefits and costs of each option for each stakeholder group**, then choose the action with the **greatest total benefit or the least total harm.**

| Option | Owner | Cousin | Families | Business |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sell concealed** | +\$40 | keeps share | overpay for damaged goods | risks a **trust collapse** worth far more than \$40 |
| **Disclose + discount** (melt bars at half price) | +~\$15 | — | **fair deal** + reason to trust | learns the rule by watching it cost something |

> **Total the columns → the discount wins**, because the **harm avoided outweighs the profit surrendered.**

### Method 2 — follow the vision

Choose the action **most consistent with the business's vision and goals.** His vision from 1.5 promises **game days families can trust** → the same answer falls out **without any arithmetic.**

> Here the two methods agree; when a scenario **splits them, apply whichever method the question names**, list the stakeholders, and **show the reasoning.**

**One more layer:** leaders also weigh **reputation** (how outsiders remember the business's choices) and **company culture** (what insiders learn to repeat — every employee who watches a leader resolve a dilemma learns what this business **actually rewards**). *Key moment:* the half-price sign — by Sunday, three parents have mentioned it in the group chat.

---

## 8. Two Tells Before the Practice Video

Scenarios **blur these on purpose:**

1. **A leader *acting* = modeling; a leader *punishing* = a repercussion.** Watch what the leader actually **does.**
2. **When a response question offers arithmetic, count *every* stakeholder group before totaling** — a half-counted table produces a **confident wrong answer.**

---

## 9. Exam Points the Video Emphasizes

- **Three forms of unethical behavior:** falsifying/concealing information, misusing company property, causing harm.
- **Incentives** drive behavior — design them for the behavior you want (e.g., tie bonuses to **satisfaction**, not units moved).
- **Four tools:** code of conduct, training, internal repercussions, **modeling** (the linchpin).
- **Ethics pays:** reputation attracts customers **and** employees; an ethical-failure response shapes relationships, perception, and profit.
- **Dilemma vs. temptation:** every option wounds a value = dilemma; clean right vs. profitable wrong = ethical-behavior test.
- **Two stakeholder groups:** internal vs. external — **count both** (suppliers are external).
- **Two response methods:** benefit-cost across stakeholders (greatest benefit / least harm), or consistency with vision/goals.
- **Weigh reputation and company culture** as part of the response.
