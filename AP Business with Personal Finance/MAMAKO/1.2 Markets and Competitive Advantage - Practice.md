## 2. The Stimulus: FreshCut (0:24)

> **Marcus Webb** owns **FreshCut**, one of **nine** stands selling strawberries at a large weekend farmers market. All nine stands buy from nearby farms and sell **nearly identical** strawberries. Last month, Marcus charged **$6** per basket while the other stands charged **$5**, and his sales fell sharply, so he returned to $5. Marcus is now considering two options: (1) investing in equipment that would **cut his costs below his rivals' costs**, or (2) converting his stand to sell **prepared strawberry desserts** which no other stand at the market offers.

The setup is a textbook identical-product (commodity) market with a prevailing price of **$5** that no single seller can hold above.

---

## 3. Q1–Q3 (shared FreshCut scenario)

### Q1 (1:18) — why did sales fall at $6?

> Which best explains why FreshCut sales fell when Marcus charged $6 per basket?
>
> A. Buyers switched to rival stands selling identical strawberries at the prevailing market price.  B. Marcus' strawberries were of lower quality than his rivals' strawberries.  C. The farmers market operates as a monopoly.  D. Raising prices **always** reduces a business's total sales.

**Answer: A.**
**Why:** The stands sell nearly identical products, so a buyer facing a higher price for the same basket simply **walks to the next stand**. Sellers push up, buyers push down; a competitive market settles at a **prevailing market price** ($5) no single seller can hold themselves above.
**Trap: D** — a pattern to memorize. Answer choices with **"always"** or **"never"** are almost never correct, because they convert a **market-specific** outcome into a **universal law**. (A differentiated business *can* raise prices without losing customers — the next questions show that.) **B** fails the stimulus test from the 1.1 practice video: nothing in the scenario mentions quality.

### Q2 (2:49) — the dessert option

> Which best describes Marcus' option of selling prepared strawberry desserts that no other stand offers?
>
> A. Seeking competitive advantage through product differentiation.  B. Strengthening a barrier to entry.  C. Charging the prevailing market price.  D. Operating in a commodity market.

**Answer: A.**
**Why:** Differentiation = building products with **distinguishing features**; a dessert no rival sells is a distinguishing feature. It moves FreshCut **out of the identical-products contest** — exactly why it appeals to a seller stuck at the market price.
**Trap: B.** "No other stand offers it" sounds like a barrier, but a **barrier to entry** is an obstacle that makes it difficult for **new firms** to compete — and **nothing stops the other eight stands from selling desserts next weekend.**

> **Key distinction the exam tests:** Uniqueness a rival **can copy tomorrow** = differentiation. An obstacle a rival **can't easily get past** = a barrier. Keep them separate.

### Q3 (4:06) — the cost-cutting option

> If Marcus instead invests in equipment that cuts his costs below rivals' costs, which best explains how this could produce competitive advantage?
>
> A. Lower costs would let him profitably charge the lowest price for an identical product.  B. Lower costs would differentiate his strawberries from his rivals' strawberries.  C. Lower costs would make the market less competitive for all nine stands.  D. Lower costs **guarantee** that his market share will increase.

**Answer: A.**
**Why:** In identical-product markets, businesses seek advantage by producing **as efficiently as possible** to charge the lowest price. Cutting costs below rivals makes the lowest price **survivable** — he can sell at a price the other stands **can't profitably match**.
**Trap: B.** **Cost lives inside the business; differentiation lives inside the product.** Cheaper-to-produce strawberries are still **identical strawberries to the buyer** — efficiency changes the price contest **without changing the product**. **D** repeats the absolutes pattern, with "guarantee" doing the same work as "always."

## 4. Q4–Q6 (standalone)

### Q4 (5:36) — reading a data table

The table shows market share for the three largest ride-hailing apps in one country:

| App | Year 1 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|
| SwiftRide | 38% | 52% |
| GoCab | 35% | 30% |
| UrbanLift | 27% | 18% |

> Which conclusion is **supported by the data** in the table?
>
> A. SwiftRide's market share grew while both rivals' shares declined.  B. SwiftRide gained market share because it charged the lowest prices.  C. UrbanLift's total revenue declined between year 1 and year 3.  D. GoCab served more customers in year 3 than in year 1.

**Answer: A.** SwiftRide 38→52, GoCab 35→30, UrbanLift 27→18. A states exactly what the numbers show and **adds nothing beyond them.**
**Traps:** Two, both teaching the same rule — **"supported by the data" means the figures show it, full stop.**

- **B** offers a **cause** (low prices) that appears nowhere in the table. **An explanation for a trend is never supported by a figure that only shows the trend** — the real exam runs this trap constantly.
- **C** confuses **market share with revenue**. Share is a *percentage* of market sales: if the whole market grew enough, UrbanLift's revenue could have **risen** while its share fell. Different measures → different conclusions.

### Q5 (7:36) — large-scale low prices

> A large beverage company produces at such a large scale that it can profitably charge prices new rivals cannot match. Which concept best describes the effect of the company's low prices?
>
> A. Voluntary exchange  B. A monopoly  C. A commodity market  D. A barrier to entry

**Answer: D.**
**Why:** Barriers to entry = obstacles making it difficult for new firms to compete; the listed barriers include **low prices made possible by operating at large scale**. A price entrants can't survive is an **obstacle at the front door.**
**Trap: B.** Scale pricing **deters new rivals**, but the company still **competes with existing ones**. A monopoly requires being the **only** business in the market with a unique product.

> **Barriers protect positions along the whole spectrum; monopoly is only the far end of it.**

### Q6 (8:37) — the utility

> HydroRegion is the only company licensed by the government to supply electricity in its region. Which best describes HydroRegion's market position?
>
> A. A commodity seller in a highly competitive market.  B. A competitive advantage achieved through differentiation.  C. A rival business seeking increased market share.  D. A monopoly protected by a legal barrier to entry.

**Answer: D.**
**Why:** One business, a unique service in its region, and **no competition** = monopoly. The **government license is the barrier** that maintains it — exactly how the course says monopolies protect their position.
**Trap: B.** Competitive advantage means **outperforming rivals in the same market** — but HydroRegion **has no rivals to outperform.**

> **Advantage is a concept about winning a contest; monopoly is the absence of the contest.**

## 5. Trap Summary (patterns across all six)

| # | Trap type | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Absolutes** ("always" / "never" / "guarantee") turn a market-specific outcome into a fake universal law | Q1, Q3 |
| 2 | Confusing **differentiation** (uniqueness a rival can copy) with a **barrier to entry** (obstacle a rival can't get past) | Q2 |
| 3 | Confusing **cost** (inside the business) with **differentiation** (inside the product) | Q3 |
| 4 | In data questions, inferring a **cause** or **revenue** that the figure doesn't show ("supported by the data" = figures only) | Q4 |
| 5 | Confusing **market share** (% of market) with **revenue** | Q4 |
| 6 | Treating any barrier as a **monopoly** (barriers span the spectrum; monopoly is only the far end) | Q5 |
| 7 | Confusing **competitive advantage** (winning a contest) with **monopoly** (no contest) | Q6 |

**Cross-question tips:**

- **Absolutes are a red flag** — "always / never / guarantee" is almost always the wrong answer.
- **Cost ≠ differentiation:** cost is internal to the business; differentiation is visible in the product to the buyer.
- **Data questions:** state only what the numbers show; never import a cause, and never equate share with revenue.
- **Barrier vs monopoly:** a barrier *protects* a position anywhere on the spectrum; monopoly is the position at the *far end* with no rivals at all.
