## 2. The Stimulus: ChargeShelf (0:18)

> **Leo Martin**, a college sophomore, notices students in the campus library constantly **hunt for open power outlets**. Over 2 weeks he **interviews 40 students**; **31** describe giving up a preferred seat to sit near an outlet. Leo **sketches** a rentable portable battery kiosk he calls **ChargeShelf**, builds a **cardboard mock-up** to work out its size and layout, then writes a **one-page description** of the service with a **\$2 daily rental** price. He shows the one-page description to **50 students** and collects **sign-ups** from those who said they would rent a battery.

Watch the artifacts: the **interviews** (problem validation), the **mock-up** (prototype), the **one-pager** (MVP), and the **sign-ups** (hypothesis evidence).

## 3. Q1–Q3 (shared ChargeShelf scenario)

### Q1 (1:02) — classify the 2 weeks of interviews

> Leo's 2 weeks of interviews are best described as which part of the entrepreneurial design-thinking process?
>
> A. Brainstorming potential solutions without evaluating them.  B. Conducting technical research to determine whether the kiosk can be built.  C. Gathering evidence to identify and validate a problem experienced by multiple potential customers.  D. Collecting customer feedback on a minimum viable product.

**Answer: C.**
**Why:** The process begins with observing, interviewing, and surveying; the goal of that first stage is to validate that a problem **exists, can be clearly defined, and is experienced by multiple customers.** 31 of 40 describing the same seat-vs-outlet trade-off is exactly that evidence.
**Traps:**

- **B** misreads the **target** of the research — technical research asks whether a solution *can be built and delivered*; Leo's interviews stay entirely on the students' problem, and **feasibility never comes up.**
- **D** runs the process **backward** — MVP feedback arrives **at the end**, after a product idea exists and is placed in front of customers. **During the interviews there was no product at all.**

### Q2 (2:26) — classify the mock-up and the one-pager

> Which correctly classifies the **cardboard mock-up** and then the **one-page service description**, in that order?
>
> A. A prototype then a minimum viable product.  B. A market gap then a business hypothesis.  C. A minimum viable product then a prototype.  D. A survey then a prototype.

**Answer: A.** A **prototype** is an early, simple version built to **explore** the idea (the mock-up works out size and layout). An **MVP** is the simplest version with only core features, placed **in front of customers** for validation feedback — and an MVP can be a sketch, description, or model, so the **one-pager qualifies.**
**Trap: C** — **swaps the pair.**

> **Classify by function:** the mock-up served **exploration** while Leo shaped the idea; the one-pager is the version he placed in front of 50 customers to **collect evidence** — the MVP's job, **even when the MVP is a page of text.**

### Q3 (3:43) — what do the sign-ups function as?

> The sign-ups Leo collects from the 50 students best function as which of the following?
>
> A. Proof that the kiosk business will be profitable.  B. A guarantee of enough demand to cover his startup costs.  C. Market research identifying a gap in an unrelated market.  D. Evidence validating his hypothesis that students will pay for the service.

**Answer: D.** The one-pager carries a **testable assumption** (students will rent batteries at \$2/day); the sign-ups are **collected evidence** bearing on exactly that hypothesis. Validation = gathering evidence, and evidence is what he now holds.
**Traps: A and B** fail the same way — **"proof" and "guarantee" are absolutes, and validation never delivers absolutes.** A student who signs a sheet is still **not a student who has paid.** Favorable evidence raises the odds and guarantees nothing.

> **The absolutes rule, flagged since Topic 1.1, is still in force here.**

## 4. Q4–Q6 (standalone)

### Q4 (5:00) — why accept the risk?

> Which best explains why an entrepreneur would bring a new product to market **despite the risk of losing invested resources**?
>
> A. Careful planning removes the possibility of financial loss.  B. The potential for future profits, the satisfaction of solving a problem, or the chance to pursue a passion can outweigh the risks involved.  C. New products succeed more often than they fail.  D. Physical and human resources have no cost until the product launches.

**Answer: B.** The three incentives justify bearing the risk: potential future profits, the satisfaction of solving a problem, and the ability to pursue a passion. **Any one** of them can justify the bet for a particular entrepreneur.
**Trap: A.** Research and planning **reduce** uncertainty, but **nothing removes it**, because customers are never obligated to buy.

> **Any choice that promises the removal of risk is wrong before you finish reading it.**

### Q5 (6:16) — survey data

An entrepreneur considering a **mobile bicycle repair service** surveyed **200 commuting cyclists.** Results:

| Finding | % reporting |
|---|---|
| Difficulty booking a repair **within the same week** | **62%** |
| Would **pay extra** for pickup-and-return service | **48%** |
| Difficulty **finding parts online** | **9%** |

> Based on the survey data, which conclusion is best supported?
>
> A. A same-week repair access problem exists and is experienced by a large share of potential customers.  B. Most surveyed cyclists will purchase the pickup-and-return service.  C. Cyclists in this market have little difficulty getting timely repairs.  D. An online parts business would be the entrepreneur's strongest opportunity.

**Answer: A.** 62% of 200 reporting the same clearly defined difficulty is **validation evidence**: the problem exists, is definable, and many share it. The conclusion **stays inside the data** and adds nothing beyond it.
**Traps:**

- **B overreaches twice:** 48% is **less than half**, so "most" is already wrong — and **survey willingness is weaker evidence than an actual purchase**, so "will purchase" outruns the data *even before the arithmetic.*
- **D inverts the table:** 9% is the **weakest signal**, and of the three findings it's the one the data supports the **least.**

### Q6 (7:58) — the MVP for a 20-feature app

> A startup is planning a study-scheduling app with **20 planned features**. Which best describes the MVP the startup should test first?
>
> A. A lower-quality version of the finished app offered at a discounted price.  B. A version withheld from customers until all 20 features work.  C. A version containing the five features that are **cheapest to build.**  D. A version containing only the **core features** needed to test whether the app solves the customer's main problem.

**Answer: D.** The MVP is the simplest version of the product idea with only its **core features**; its job is to test one question — **does this solve the customer's main problem?** Everything else waits until that answer comes back *yes.*
**Traps:**

- **A** is the **cheap-version misconception:** "minimum" refers to **feature count**, and the MVP still has to **work well enough** to test the core problem honestly.
- **C** filters features by **build cost**; the right filter is the **core problem**, because a cheap feature that ignores the main problem produces **feedback about nothing that actually matters.**

## 5. Trap Summary (patterns across all six)

| # | Trap type | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Misreading the **stage/target** of research (technical research vs. problem validation; MVP feedback comes at the **end**) | Q1 |
| 2 | **Paired-concept swap** (prototype vs. MVP) — classify by **function**, not appearance | Q2 |
| 3 | **Absolutes** ("proof" / "guarantee") — validation **never** delivers absolutes | Q3 |
| 4 | Promising the **removal of risk** — wrong before you finish reading | Q4 |
| 5 | **Data overreach:** "most" when <50%, and "will purchase" outruns survey **willingness** | Q5 |
| 6 | **Inverting the data:** building the conclusion on the **weakest** signal | Q5 |
| 7 | MVP **"minimum" = feature count**, not a cheap/cut-rate version; filter by **core problem**, not **build cost** | Q6 |

**Three cross-question tips:**

- **The absolutes rule (since 1.1):** validation **reduces uncertainty** and **raises the odds** — it never proves, guarantees, or removes risk.
- **Classify by function/role,** not surface form — an MVP can be a page of text; a prototype can be cardboard.
- **In data questions:** stay inside the figures; "most" needs >50%, and stated willingness ≠ a purchase.
