## 2. The Stimulus: Osei Printworks (0:21)

> **Marcus Osei** owns **Osei Printworks**, a small print shop with two employees. Posted **core values:** quality and honesty. A **rush order of 500 event programs** comes back with a **printing flaw visible only under bright light.** **Reprinting costs \$300 and misses the customer's deadline.** A **new employee suggests shipping the programs without mentioning the flaw.** Marcus is also drafting the shop's first **code of conduct** and is considering a **bonus that pays employees for each rush order completed on schedule.**

Watch for the artifacts: a **concealment** suggestion, an **incentive** (the bonus), and a potential **modeling** moment.

---

## 3. Q1–Q3 (shared Osei Printworks scenario)

### Q1 (0:59) — classify the employee's suggestion

> The employee's suggestion to ship the programs without mentioning the flaw is best described as which?
>
> A. Concealing information from a customer, a form of unethical behavior.  B. Misusing company property.  C. An ethical dilemma between two core values.  D. A legal factor affecting the print market.

**Answer: A.** Shipping a product with a known flaw and saying nothing means the customer pays believing something the seller knows is false — **concealing information** is a named form of unethical behavior.
**Trap: C.** Marcus *does* face a genuine decision with hard trade-offs, but **the employee's specific suggestion is silence for gain** — and **silence for gain is simply unethical behavior.**

> **Classify the suggestion *itself*.** A dilemma exists when a core value conflicts with another value or a business goal (honesty toward the customer vs. the deadline promise). The suggestion here is **concealment**, not a dilemma.

### Q2 (2:11) — classify the on-schedule bonus

> The proposed bonus for each rush order completed on schedule is best described as which?
>
> A. A code of conduct provision.  B. An internal repercussion for unethical behavior.  C. An incentive structure that could influence employees toward cutting corners.  D. Modeling ethical behavior for the staff.

**Answer: C.** An incentive is a reward that encourages a particular action; incentive structures can push people toward unethical choices that benefit themselves. A bonus tied **only to on-schedule completion** rewards speed and pays the same **whether the flaw is disclosed or hidden** — so it **quietly leans on employees to ship and stay silent.**
**Trap: B** — **points the concept backward.** A repercussion is a consequence imposed **after** unethical behavior to discourage it; the bonus arrives **before** any behavior and rewards output.

> **Sort these tools by direction:** **incentives pull *toward* behavior; repercussions push *back after* it.**

### Q3 (3:24) — Marcus reprints + explains + shows the customer note

> Marcus **reprints the order at the shop's expense**, tells both employees why, and shows them the note he sent the customer. This action best represents which?
>
> A. An external repercussion.  B. Modeling ethical behavior.  C. Outsourcing an ethical decision.  D. A conflict of interest.

**Answer: B.** Modeling means leaders **demonstrate** the ethical behavior themselves, so employees learn the standard by **watching it** — Marcus performed the standard in front of his staff **at real cost.**
**Trap: A.** A repercussion is a consequence aimed at someone who behaved unethically, and **nobody here is being punished.** Marcus **absorbed a cost to demonstrate a value** — demonstration is the modeling tool.

> **When a question shows a leader *acting* (rather than rewarding or punishing), look for modeling.**

## 4. Q4–Q6 (standalone)

### Q4 (4:25) — stakeholder classification

> Which best identifies a **correct** classification of stakeholders in a business?
>
> A. Customers are internal; owners are external.  B. Managers are external; community members are internal.  C. Owners are internal; employees are external.  D. Employees are internal; government agencies are external.

**Answer: D.** Internal = **direct involvement** in operations/decisions/outcomes (owners, managers, employees). External = **vested interest without** being employed or directly involved (customers, government agencies, community members).
**Trap: C** — gets owners right and then **exports the employees.**

> **The sorting rule is direct involvement — importance and seniority play no part.** An employee works inside daily operations → **internal** alongside owners/managers; **even the most loyal customer remains external.**

### Q5 (5:46) — benefit-and-cost table

A furniture maker must decide whether to **switch to a cheaper overseas supplier** whose delivery delays would reach customers. Estimated effects:

| Option | Internal stakeholders | External stakeholders |
|---|---|---|
| **Keep current** | **−\$90,000** (higher input prices) | **+\$70,000** (reliable deliveries) |
| **Switch** | **+\$90,000** (savings) | **−\$150,000** (late/canceled orders) |

> Using the approach of **greatest total benefit or least total harm across all stakeholders**, which decision does the table support?
>
> A. Switching — it produces a \$90,000 benefit for internal stakeholders.  B. Switching — customer losses are not costs to the company.  C. Keeping — its net cost of **\$20,000** is smaller than the **\$60,000** net cost of switching.  D. Keeping — reliable suppliers **always** increase long-run profits.

**Answer: C.** Work each option across **all** stakeholders:

- **Keep:** +\$70,000 − \$90,000 = **net cost of \$20,000**
- **Switch:** +\$90,000 − \$150,000 = **net cost of \$60,000**

Both options **hurt**, so the method picks the **least total harm** → \$20,000 is the smaller loss.
**Traps:**

- **A totals one column and stops.** The method exists **precisely to count every stakeholder group** — an option that **pays insiders by costing outsiders more** is exactly what the arithmetic is built to catch.
- **D reaches the *right decision with a broken reason*** — and **"always" is the tell.** No ethical choice carries a promised profit outcome; **a guarantee dressed as reasoning fails even when its verdict happens to match.**

### Q6 (7:58) — how a response to a lapse hits profitability

> Which best explains how a business's **response to an ethical lapse** can affect its **profitability**?
>
> A. Profitability is unaffected unless a law was broken.  B. The response shapes customer relationships, employee relationships, and public perception, which in turn affect revenues and costs.  C. A sincere apology **guarantees** customers will return.  D. Ethical responses matter only for large corporations with public brands.

**Answer: B.** The chain runs **response → relationships and perception → profitability.** Customers **reward trust with loyalty** and **punish deception by leaving**; employees weigh employers the same way; both movements land on the **revenue and cost** lines.
**Traps:**

- **A borrows legal thinking from Topic 1.3.** Legal consequences are one risk; **reputational consequences can arise whether or not any law is involved** — customers rarely wait for a court ruling before they stop buying.
- **C fails on "guarantees."** A good response **improves the odds and promises nothing.**

## 5. Trap Summary (patterns across all six)

| # | Trap type | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Classify the **specific action itself** — silence-for-gain = unethical behavior, not a dilemma | Q1 |
| 2 | **Incentives vs. repercussions by direction:** pull toward (before) vs. push back (after) | Q2 |
| 3 | **Leader acting = modeling; leader punishing = repercussion** — watch what the leader *does* | Q3 |
| 4 | Stakeholder sorting = **direct involvement**, not importance or seniority | Q4 |
| 5 | **Count every stakeholder column** before totaling; "pays insiders by costing outsiders" is what the arithmetic catches | Q5 |
| 6 | **Right decision + broken reason = still wrong**; "always/guarantee" as reasoning fails | Q5 |
| 7 | **Absolutes** ("guarantees") + borrowing **legal-factor** thinking from 1.3 (reputational harm needs no law) | Q6 |

**Three cross-question tips:**

- **The absolutes rule (since 1.1):** "always / never / guarantee" is almost always wrong — and a right verdict reached by a guaranteed-profit reason is *still* wrong.
- **Dilemma vs. unethical behavior:** classify the **action itself**; a clean profitable-wrong vs. right is an ethics test, not a dilemma.
- **Stakeholder arithmetic:** net **across every group** (internal + external), then pick **greatest benefit / least harm** — and remember reputation needs no law.
