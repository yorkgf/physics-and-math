## 2. The Stimulus: Riverbend Suds (0:23)

> **Dana Whitfield** owns **Riverbend Suds**, a small-batch soap business with a Saturday farmers market stall and a small online store. Stall shoppers who **join her email list** get a free sample bar. The store requires an **account**, and its software **logs what each account browses and buys**. A short **checkout survey** asks each customer's **age range, zip code, and what matters most** when choosing soap.
>
> Reading the results, Dana sees **two clusters**:
> - Shoppers **over 50** who buy single unscented bars and rank **sensitive-skin ingredients** first.
> - Shoppers in their **late 20s–30s** who buy **gift sets**, follow the shop on social media, and rank **plastic-free packaging** first.
>
> Dana decides her next product line will serve the **younger cluster**, and drafts a one-page description of an imaginary shopper: **Marisol, 31**, a renter who hosts friends often, follows zero-waste creators, and buys gifts locally.

Watch for the artifacts: **two data types** in the survey, a **target segment**, and a **customer profile** (Marisol).

## 3. Q1–Q3 (shared Riverbend Suds scenario)

### Q1 (1:32) — classify the survey data

> Which best describes the customer data the checkout survey collects?
>
> A. Only demographic data, because survey answers can be counted.  B. Both demographic data (age + zip code) **and** psychographic data (what matters most).  C. Only psychographic data, because surveys record opinions.  D. Neither type, because a survey is a traditional tool rather than a digital one.

**Answer: B.** Demographic = measurable qualities describing a population (age, income, location) → **age range + zip code.** Psychographic = interests, activities, values, lifestyles → **"what matters most."** One survey can collect **both at once.**
**Traps:**

- **A** — bait is the word **"measurable."** Dana *can* count how many ranked plastic-free packaging first, but **counting responses never changes what they describe.**
- **D** — **confuses the tool with the data type.** The type depends on the **question asked, never on the channel asking it.**

> **Traits of the person = demographic; contents of the mind = psychographic.**

### Q2 (2:57) — why target the younger cluster?

> Which best explains the advantage Dana gains by designing the next product line for the younger cluster?
>
> A. She can design branding, pricing, and advertising that appeal to **anyone who might ever buy** soap.  B. Serving one segment **guarantees** the new line outsells her current products.  C. She can stop collecting data from customers outside the chosen segment.  D. Focusing design, branding, pricing, and advertising on the buyers most likely to purchase is typically **more effective and less costly** than appealing to a wide audience.

**Answer: D.** A target customer is the buyer **most likely to purchase** because of their wants/needs/preferences; aiming product/price/ads at one defined group usually **converts better and costs less** than chasing the whole market.
**Traps:**

- **A** — the **opposite strategy.** Appealing to anyone who might ever buy is the expensive, unfocused path that targeting exists to replace. (A choice that quietly widens the audience to everyone has **abandoned the target.**)
- **B** — fails on **"guarantees."** Targeting **improves the odds and promises nothing.**

### Q3 (4:25) — identify the Marisol description

> The one-page description of Marisol is best identified as which?
>
> A. A **customer profile** — a fictional sample customer built from demographic and psychographic data.  B. A **market segment** because it represents many customers who share traits.  C. A record of Dana's single best **real** customer.  D. A **subscriber list** entry generated by the online store.

**Answer: A.** A customer profile **personifies the target customer** — the business invents one specific person and designs/markets as if she were the audience. Marisol's age + renter status = demographic layer; hosting, zero-waste creators, local gifting = psychographic layer.
**Traps:**

- **B** — **one zoom level too far out.** The segment is the **group**; the profile is **one imaginary member** drawn from it.
- **C** — misses the word **"fictional."** A profile is an invented composite; **no single real shopper is its subject.**

> **Segment = the group. Profile = the invented person who stands for it.**

## 4. Q4–Q6 (standalone)

### Q4 (5:36) — how a rewards program raises profits

> Which best explains how a rewards program for frequent buyers can increase a business's profits?
>
> A. It reduces the **cost of manufacturing** each unit sold.  B. It **guarantees** members will keep making repeat purchases.  C. It builds **brand loyalty and repeat purchasing**, which raises the **lifetime value** of each customer.  D. It removes any need to attract new customers.

**Answer: C.** Lifetime value = estimated total a customer will spend over time. Relationship tactics (rewards, personalized service, feedback surveys) exist to **raise it** because loyal customers make repeat purchases. Satisfied customers also **refer friends, which pushes CAC down** — so one program can move **both numbers.**
**Traps:**

- **B** — **"guarantees"** is the tell. A rewards program **raises the probability** of repeat purchases and promises nothing.
- **A** — **borrows from operations.** A punch card changes buying behavior and leaves the cost of making each unit **untouched.**

> **Relationship tactics work through revenue and acquisition cost — never through production cost.**

### Q5 (7:01) — CAC and lifetime value table

**Cinder Peak Cocoa** ran one marketing campaign in **spring** and one in **fall**:

| | Spring | Fall |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising costs | \$6,000 | \$6,000 |
| Other marketing & sales costs | \$2,000 | \$3,000 |
| New customers acquired | 400 | 300 |
| Average customer lifetime value | \$60 | \$90 |

> Which conclusion is supported by the table?
>
> A. Spring's CAC was **\$15** per customer.  B. Fall performed **worse** because its CAC was higher.  C. The campaigns had **equal** CAC because their advertising costs were equal.  D. Fall produced **more value per customer**, with LTV exceeding CAC by **\$60** vs **\$40** in spring.

**Answer: D.** CAC divides **total** marketing, advertising, and sales costs by customers acquired:

$$
\text{Spring CAC}=\frac{6000+2000}{400}=\frac{8000}{400}=\$20
\qquad
\text{Fall CAC}=\frac{6000+3000}{300}=\frac{9000}{300}=\$30
$$

Set each against LTV → **Spring:** \$60 − \$20 = **\$40** left · **Fall:** \$90 − \$30 = **\$60** left. **Fall wins per customer.**
**Traps:**

- **A** — divides **advertising alone** (6,000 ÷ 400 = \$15). The formula takes **every acquisition dollar** — marketing **+** advertising **+** sales together.
- **B** — **stops at the cost line.** A higher CAC can still be the better buy when LTV grows faster: fall paid **\$10 more** per customer to **gain \$30 more** in value.

> **Compute CAC from all costs, then judge it against LTV — never on its own.**

### Q6 (9:31) — the risk unsecured data creates for the business

> A neighborhood gym that advertises itself as a **trusted community space** stores members' payment card numbers and check-in histories in an **unsecured spreadsheet.** Which best explains the risk this creates **for the gym itself**?
>
> A. None — a breach harms only the customers whose information is exposed.  B. A breach could **drive members away, violate the gym's stated values, and damage its reputation.**  C. **Any** collection of customer data violates the law.  D. The risk ends once members accept the gym's terms of service.

**Answer: B.** Unsecured data invites a breach → exposes members to fraud and identity theft → consequences **rebound on the business.** Customers who no longer feel safe leave; the gym's stated values now read as broken promises; the story reaches people who never joined.

> **The three business-side risks: lost customers, violated values, damaged reputation.**

**Traps:**

- **A** — sends the harm in **only one direction.** Customers face identity theft; **the business faces the exits, the values violation, and the reputation damage.**
- **C** — hardens a real concern into a **false absolute.** Collecting customer data is **legal and routine**; the tested issue is **securing it and using it transparently.**

## 5. Trap Summary (patterns across all six)

| # | Trap type | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **"Measurable/countable" bait** — counting responses doesn't change what they describe; data type depends on the **question, not the tool/channel** | Q1 |
| 2 | **"Appeal to anyone"** = abandoning the target; **"guarantees"** (targeting improves odds, promises nothing) | Q2 |
| 3 | **Segment vs. profile** zoom level — segment is the *group*; profile is one *invented* person | Q3 |
| 4 | **Relationship tactics move revenue/LTV and CAC — never production cost**; "guarantees" repeat purchases | Q4 |
| 5 | **CAC uses ALL acquisition costs**, not just advertising; judge CAC **against LTV**, never on its own | Q5 |
| 6 | **Breach harm rebounds on the business** (3 business-side risks); "any data collection is illegal" = false absolute | Q6 |

**Three cross-question tips:**

- **Data type = the question asked**, never the tool: traits of the person → demographic; contents of the mind → psychographic.
- **Targeting vs. broad appeal:** targeted is more effective + less costly, but it **improves odds, never guarantees.**
- **CAC → then LTV:** $\text{CAC} = \frac{\text{ALL marketing + advertising + sales costs}}{\text{customers acquired}}$, then compare to lifetime value — a higher CAC can still be the better buy when LTV grows faster.
