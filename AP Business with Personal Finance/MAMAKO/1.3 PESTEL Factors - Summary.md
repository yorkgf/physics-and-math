## 1. The Memo (0:00)

Monday morning again — but this time the problem isn't a competitor. A memo goes up in the hallway: **"Starting next week, no food sales anywhere on campus, by order of the new principal."**

Two backpacks, one price war, one cold-drink sideline — and **none of it matters**, because a force **outside the market** just rewrote the rules for both kids at once. Neither seller did anything wrong; the **environment moved**.

---

## 2. The PESTEL Framework (1:00)

**PESTEL** is an acronym for the six kinds of **outside forces** that shape the business landscape: **P**olitical, **E**conomic, **S**ocial, **T**echnological, **E**nvironmental, **L**egal.

These factors decide which types of businesses are **viable** in a market.

> **Viability** = the ability to **survive and stay successful** in a market over time. The course uses this word constantly — learn it.

The two kids never thought about any of this while undercutting each other — and that's the point:

> **Rivals live inside the market. PESTEL factors press on it from the outside.**

The exam expects you to (1) **identify** each factor from a scenario, and (2) **reason** about what it does to a business.

---

## 3. Political, Economic, Social (1:49)

### P — Political

Policies and political dynamics that affect market activity: **trade policy, taxes and subsidies, mandates, bans, political stability.**

- A **subsidy** supports an activity; a **ban** limits one; a **tax** funds government while often discouraging whatever gets taxed (e.g., cigarette use).
- The principal's memo works exactly like a **ban** — one decision from an authority and an entire market closes.
- A country raising **tariffs** (taxes on imported goods) raises costs for every chocolate maker that buys abroad.
- A **mandate** works the other way: a rule requiring seat belts in every new car creates a market no automaker can skip.
- **Political stability** matters most where it is *missing* — businesses avoid markets where the rules might change with the next government.

### E — Economic

Conditions of the economy itself: **economic stability, household income, inflation, unemployment, interest rates.**

- When allowance money shrinks, fewer students have \$1.50 to spend, and the hallway market shrinks with it.
- When inflation raises the supermarket price of a bulk candy box, the seller's cost rises **before he sells a single bar.**
- Most businesses do better when the economy is strong and stable. (You'll meet interest rates properly in Unit 3.)

> **Exam tip:** income, inflation, unemployment, and interest rates all point to the same **source** — the condition of the economy itself — **never** to any single company's choices.

### S — Social

Trends in society and culture that affect consumers: **demographics, cultural norms, lifestyle trends, population growth.**

- A fitness trend sweeps the school → half the buyers switch to protein bars → demand for candy fades **without any rival lifting a finger.**
- A neighborhood filling with young families changes what sells there.
- **Demographics** = the measurable traits of a population (age, income bracket, family size). A town growing older buys differently than a town filling with students.

## 4. Technological, Environmental, Legal (4:17)

### T — Technological

Features of a market tied to the availability of technology: **internet access, automation of production, the pace of innovation.**

- The class group chat (from 1.2) is technology acting on the hallway market — inventory posted online reaches buyers a backpack cannot.
- A region without reliable internet **cannot host** an online store.
- A factory that automates can produce at costs a manual rival struggles to match.
- The **rate of innovation** matters as much as any single tool — markets that adopt technology quickly **punish businesses that move slowly.**
- Automation and AI are this decade's version, rewriting production and customer service at the same time.

### E — Environmental

External environmental conditions that promote or limit market activity: **geography, access to renewable/non-renewable resources, waste rules, how customers think about the environment.**

- The heat wave that made cold drinks valuable in 1.2 was an environmental factor **doing the selling.**
- A drought that ruins a strawberry harvest raises costs for **every** dessert stand.
- Customers who care about packaging will pay for the business that listens.
- **Geography counts:** a landlocked region cannot host a fishing fleet; a sunny one invites solar energy.

### L — Legal

Specific **laws, rules, and regulations** that impact market activity: **employment laws, consumer protection laws, health and safety rules, environmental regulations, intellectual property protection, antitrust laws.**

- A food-handling certificate requirement is a legal factor; so is the **patent** from 1.2 that protects a medicine.

> **The distinction the exam loves:** **Political** factors are broad policy *choices* (like a tax or a ban); **Legal** factors are specific *laws* a business must comply with (like a safety code). Keep these separate and one common trap disappears.

- **Antitrust laws** (limit monopolies, block anti-competitive behavior) connect this topic straight back to the end of 1.2.

### Overlap caution

Real scenarios **overlap** — a single new emissions law can carry political, environmental, *and* legal weight at once. The exam asks for the **best fit**, and the **source of the force decides it.**

## 5. Viability and Careers (6:58)

Put the six together and they answer one blunt question: **what kind of business can survive in this market at all?**

| Factor | What it decides |
|---|---|
| Political | Which activities are incentivized vs. disincentivized |
| Economic | How much customers, businesses, and government can spend |
| Social | What people **want** to buy |
| Technological | What can be produced, distributed, communicated |
| Environmental | What resources are even available |
| Legal | The cost of operating + the line around what's allowed |

> Most businesses need a strong economy, but **some thrive in a weak one** — like discount stores when incomes fall. A consumer-oriented business lives or dies on reading **social** factors correctly, so social factors sit at the center of its viability. **Government spending** belongs on the list too — public contracts and projects are customers.

**Careers:** jobs exist where viable businesses exist. When PESTEL factors make a market attractive, businesses enter and hire; when the factors turn, jobs move with it (layoffs cluster in downturns). **Choosing where to work is partly reading the same map a business reads.**

## 6. Applying the Framework (8:21)

This is the skill the exam actually tests — **using PESTEL to evaluate a market.** The method:

1. **Identify** which factors are relevant to your product.
2. **Assess** how each one affects the market's **attractiveness** *and* its **risks.**
3. A business enters where the factors **align** with its model, resources, and customers.

> A **tech company** locates where high-speed internet and electricity are cheap and reliable. A **farm-to-table restaurant** opens where customers prioritize locally grown food. **Same framework, opposite conclusions** — because the factor that matters depends on the business.

**The candy seller runs it on his next market** (hallway closed; he's considering the weekend soccer league):

| Factor | Reading for the soccer league |
|---|---|
| Political | No rule against food sales yet |
| Economic | Parents at games carry cash and buy freely |
| Social | A captive crowd shows up every Saturday |
| Technological | The group chat moves with him |
| Environmental | Games run outdoors; hot afternoons sell cold drinks |
| Legal | The park requires a simple vendor permit |

Five factors align; one costs paperwork → an **attractive market with known risk.**

> This factor-by-factor reasoning is exactly what a strong exam answer looks like: **name the factor, state its direction, and tie it to this specific business.** A factor that's good news for one model is bad news for another (cheap electricity attracts a data center, does nothing for a tutoring service).

---

## 7. When Factors Change (10:01)

PESTEL factors **move**, and when they move they can rewrite an existing market **overnight.** A change alters what customers need, what they can afford, what can be produced, and what resources are reachable.

- **Flu season** empties the school → every hallway sale disappears for two weeks.
- A new law, a downturn, a technology shift — each redraws the map for businesses **already operating.** That's why established companies keep watching the same six factors startups check before entering.
- The memo that opened this video was **one changed factor**, and it ended a market two competitors had spent weeks fighting over.

> **Exam signal:** when a question describes a market shifting after a new law, a downturn, or a technology jump, it's testing whether you can **name which factor moved** and **what it did to viability.**

---

## 8. Exam Points the Video Emphasizes

- **Rivals are inside the market; PESTEL factors press from outside.**
- **Viability** = survive and stay successful over time — used constantly.
- **Identify each factor from a scenario** and reason about its effect.
- **Political vs. Legal:** broad policy *choice* (tax/ban) vs. specific *law* to comply with (safety code) — the exam's favorite distinction here.
- **Overlap is real** — pick the **best fit** by the **source** of the force.
- **Economic factors** always point to the economy's condition, never one company's choices.
- **Evaluate a market** factor-by-factor: name it, state its direction, tie it to the specific business.
- **Factors move** — a shift tests whether you can name the factor and its effect on viability.
