## 1. The Line Theo Refused (0:00)

Saturday at the flea market, a father circles a **\$95 commuter bike** for the third time without reaching for his wallet. The vendor two racks over leans in and offers Theo the oldest trick in used sales: *"Tell him three other people are coming to see it this afternoon."* Nobody else is coming — and Theo knows it.

He flips up the **orange tag** zip-tied to the handlebars instead and reads the man all **21 points** of the inspection (brakes to bottom bracket), then taps the **30-day tune-up promise** printed at the bottom. **The father buys the bike.** This topic explains why he said yes — and why the honest version outsold the lie.

---

## 2. Why Consumers Buy (1:09)

Consumers buy goods and services to **meet basic needs and wants.** The father *needs* his kid transported to school and *wants* that on a bike the kid will actually ride.

Most needs have **rival goods** circling them — multiple products that can meet the same need. When rivals exist, **four things break the tie:**

| Tiebreaker | How it pulls the father |
|---|---|
| **Price** | a comparable new bike costs about **twice** Theo's price → pulls toward the stall |
| **Differentiated product features** | the signed inspection tag + the 30-day tune-up promise — no factory-fresh rival carries these |
| **Availability** | his bike rolls home **today**; an online order ships next week |
| **Advertising** | the listing that brought him to the stall — putting the product in front of the person whose need it fits |

---

## 3. Rational Decisions and Habits (2:07)

How carefully a consumer chooses depends on **what a mistake would cost.**

- **Consequential purchases** (a home, a car, a first bike for a kid riding to school in traffic): consumers use a **rational decision-making process** — list the alternatives, compare them **systematically** on the factors that matter, pick the best fit.
- **Small routine purchases** (the coffee on the way to work, the same gum at the same register): run on **habit** with no comparison at all.

The rational process identifies the product that best meets the need, and it charges for the search **mostly in time spent gathering information.**

### Worked comparison — the true cost of a cheaper rival

A parent spends three evenings on listings and finds an **untagged \$75 rival** to Theo's **\$95** commuter. The rival comes with **no inspection**, and a shop charges about **\$25** to check the brakes.

$$
\$75 + \$25 = \$100
$$

> The tagged bike **wins by \$5** — even though the sticker price pointed the other way. Three evenings of searching surfaced that reversal, which is a **sane trade for a bike and an absurd one for gum.**

**Match the depth of the process to the size of the decision.**

## 4. Four Families of Influence (3:24)

Four families of factors shape buying decisions — Theo's stall meets all four every Saturday.

### Personal factors — facts of the buyer's own life

**Age, sex, education, occupation, income level, budget, lifestyle.** They set what a consumer needs, wants, and can afford.

> Renee's profile card (from 2.1) is a stack of personal factors: 41 years old, a \$90 budget, a middle schooler who needs to reach campus by bell time. Her **income sets the ceiling**; her **kid's age sets the frame size.**

### Psychological factors — inside the buyer's head

**Values and beliefs, perceptions, learning, individual motivations** — shaping attitudes toward products and willingness to buy.

| Factor | Meaning | Theo's stall |
|---|---|---|
| **Values** | what matters most | Renee values **safety over speed** |
| **Perception** | how a product reads **before the facts arrive** | used bikes read as **risky** → the exact perception the orange tag exists to correct |
| **Learning** | associations built from **prior experience** | a parent whose last secondhand bike snapped a chain arrives carrying an association Theo must outweigh |
| **Motivation** | the **push** behind the purchase | hers is a **Saturday deadline** |

### Social and cultural factors — from other people

**Peers, family, social status, cultural norms, media.** They define what feels acceptable, desirable, or appropriate to buy.

> When three families at school pickup mention the same stall, buying there becomes the **normal** move. At the school bike rack, a clean rebuilt commuter carries a little **status** of its own. The pickup photos Theo posts (with written permission) are **media** doing the same work at scale.

### Situational influences — temporary conditions around the moment of purchase

**Store design (noise, lighting, organization), timing, product availability.**

> A flea market is loud and cluttered, so Theo's rack **fights the setting**: three tiers in a straight line, tags facing out, a clear lane for test rides. A sunny back-to-school Saturday encourages buying; a rainy one discourages it — **the bikes are identical both days.**

> ⚠️ **Exam point:** situational influences are **temporary conditions around the purchase — not features of the product.**

## 5. Patterns, Law, and Technology (5:49)

### Purchasing patterns

A **purchasing pattern** = a consumer's typical routine for buying, described by **timing, frequency, and quantity.** Where a person lives/works, income level, and the patterns of family/friends all help set it.

> Theo's ledger: local families buy **one bike at a time, every few years per kid, clustered in late August**, from a stall a short drive from home.

### Laws bend patterns

Laws regulate **what** consumers can buy and **when/where** they can buy it:

- Many places require **helmets** for young riders → a kids' bike sale tends to pull a helmet sale along behind it.
- **Consumer protection laws** help people avoid fraudulent or unsafe products → reach Theo directly: **reselling a recalled children's product can break the law**, so checking recall lists is the **first point** on his 21-point inspection.
- Legal factors also give rise to **substitutes** — when a product gets harder to obtain, consumers find another way to meet the need. Where cities restrict rental scooters, some riders substitute bicycles.

### Technology rewrites patterns — faster than law

Innovation keeps making new products accessible:

- The internet reaching homes in the **1990s** moved shopping, information, and entertainment online.
- **Smartphones** spreading in the **2010s** put all three in every pocket.
- **Trued Cycles only exists inside that shift** — Theo learned repairs from free videos, sells through a marketplace app, and watches parents check his price against new-bike prices on their phones.

> ⚠️ **Exam point:** technology changes patterns on **both sides of the sale — seller reach and buyer information.**

## 6. Seven Principles of Influence (7:47)

Psychologist **Robert Cialdini** cataloged the principles of influence — psychological factors that lead people to comply with a request. Marketers build sales tactics on them.

> **Every principle has an honest application and a manipulative one.**

| Principle | What it is | Theo's honest version | The manipulative version |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scarcity** | the rarer an item seems, the more people want it (limited-time offers, "only one left") | every used bike really **is** one of one — no invention needed | the vendor's *"three other buyers"* line is scarcity **faked** |
| **Authority** | people follow authority figures (expert spokespeople, credentials) | the **signed orange tag** — a documented 21-point inspection is a credential you can hold | (paid "expert" who isn't really expert) |
| **Consensus** (social proof) | people follow the norms of their group; promote positive feedback until a product feels widely liked | the **permission-based pickup photos** — a wall of local kids the next family can join | fake reviews |
| **Liking** | more easily influenced by someone they like/relate to | Theo is a **local teenager** who remembers each kid and explains brake pads in plain language | (manufactured false rapport) |
| **Reciprocity** | people feel obliged to give back after receiving value (free trials, samples, gifts) | he **airs up tires and lubes the chain** of whatever bike a browser rode in on — no charge | a "gift" that creates hidden obligation |
| **Consistency** | people repeat behavior that matches their **self-image**; appeal to identity | a listing that opens *"for parents who put safety first"* invites Renee to act like the person she believes she is | (getting a small commitment, then exploiting it) |
| **Unity** | when people feel part of a group, the group's influence grows; build community, invite co-creation | each kid **picks the handlebar tape color**; a fall **group ride for "Trued Families"** turns customers into members | (manufactured in-group vs. out-group pressure) |

> *Caption note: the video's auto-captions mis-render the name as "Hildini" — the psychologist is **Robert Cialdini** (author of Influence), and these seven are his principles.*

## 7. The Face Test (10:37)

The exam creators will ask you to **judge** a tactic and **build** one.

> **A tactic is worth keeping when the principle still works after full disclosure.**

Theo's test: **say the tactic to the customer's face and see if it survives.**

| Tactic | Face test |
|---|---|
| "This tag lists everything I checked" | ✅ **survives** |
| "Come back within 30 days and the tune-up is free" | ✅ **survives** — and reverses the risk that keeps used-bike buyers from saying yes |
| "Three other people are coming this afternoon" | ❌ **dies** the moment a buyer asks *"who?"* |

> One caught lie undoes the trust the signed checklist was built to earn — and **trust is what converts these buyers.**

**Assignment from this topic:** develop a sales tactic of your own, **name the principle** it draws on, and check that it **passes the face test.**

## 8. Exam Points the Video Emphasizes

- **Needs/wants + rival goods** broken by four tiebreakers: **price, differentiated features, availability, advertising.**
- **Rational vs. habitual:** match depth to stakes; the rational process "charges in time" — add the **true cost of a cheaper rival** (e.g., $75 + \$25 = \$100).
- **Four families:** personal, psychological, social/cultural, situational.
- ⚠️ **Situational = temporary conditions around the purchase, NOT product features.**
- **Purchasing pattern** = timing, frequency, quantity.
- **Laws** regulate what/when/where → create **substitutes**; **consumer protection laws** (e.g., recalled-product resale).
- ⚠️ **Technology reshapes both sides of the sale — seller reach and buyer information.**
- **Seven Cialdini principles:** scarcity, authority, consensus, liking, reciprocity, consistency, unity — each has an **honest and a manipulative** application.
- **Face test:** a tactic is worth keeping if the principle **still works after full disclosure.**
