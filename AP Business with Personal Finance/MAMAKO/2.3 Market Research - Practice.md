## 2. The Stimulus: Two Bucket Detail (0:22)

> **Tomas Herrera** and his sister **Bree** run **Two Bucket Detail**, a two-person mobile crew that details cars in customers' driveways. Their menu lists **eight separate services**, and bookings stall while callers ask what to pick — so the crew plans to replace the list with one flat **\$100 bundle.** They draft two candidates: **Inside Out** (interior vacuuming, seat cleaning, windows) and **Showroom** (exterior wash, wax, wheels).
>
> Tomas writes down what he suspects: **"customers booking a family vehicle will choose Inside Out over Showroom."** For **four weekends**, every booking inquiry is quoted **one of the two bundles, assigned at random**, and the crew logs which version **converts more inquiries into bookings.** Afterward, Bree calls **ten recent customers (five from each group)**, asking **open-ended** questions about how they decided, with **follow-ups** whenever an answer surprises her.

Watch for the artifacts: a **hypothesis** (Tomas's sentence), an **A/B test** (the four-weekend comparison), and **interviews** (Bree's follow-ups).

## 3. Q1–Q3 (shared Two Bucket scenario)

### Q1 (1:29) — identify Tomas's sentence

> The sentence Tomas writes down is best identified as which?
>
> A. A **finding** from primary-source research (it comes from the crew's own booking records).  B. A **market segment** (it names customers with family vehicles).  C. A **qualitative observation** (it is recorded in words).  D. A **business hypothesis** — an assumption about customer preferences stated so it can be tested before the crew commits.

**Answer: D.** A business hypothesis = an **assumption** about a customer, product, or market, written down so the business can **check whether it holds before committing** to a course of action. (Two Bucket is an established business doing what established businesses use research for: reading changing wants, finding an opening to innovate, holding/growing share.)
**Traps:**

- **A** — the tell is **timing**: a hypothesis goes **into** research; a finding comes **out.** Tomas holds a **hunch and zero results.**
- **B** — borrows last topic's vocabulary: a **segment** is a group of customers sharing traits; this sentence is a **testable claim** about what that group prefers.

> **The hypothesis is the assumption fed IN; the finding is what the test hands BACK.**

### Q2 (3:02) — classify the four-weekend comparison

> The four-weekend bundle comparison is best described as which?
>
> A. A **focus group** (customers react to two options).  B. An **A/B test** — an experiment measuring real customer behavior toward two competing versions.  C. An **observation** (the crew records decisions in a natural environment).  D. A **survey** (every caller answers a question).

**Answer: B.** An A/B test is a type of **experiment**: put two workable versions in front of real customers, **split them at random**, measure what each group **actually does.** Experiments and observations both capture **behavior** (not opinions); the difference is **control.** (Booking or declining is behavior, collected in a real-world setting where no caller knows a test is running.)
**Traps:**

- **C** — the tell is **intervention:** the crew **changed what each caller heard**; behavior measured after a deliberate change is an **experiment.** An observation watches a natural environment **without touching it.**
- **A** — swaps behavior for **opinions:** a focus group gathers a small group to **discuss** reactions in words; these callers discussed nothing — they booked or they didn't.

> **A deliberate change makes it an experiment; untouched watching is an observation.**

### Q3 (4:41) — why one-on-one interviews?

> Which best explains why Bree used **one-on-one interviews** for the follow-up calls?
>
> A. Interviews collect **in-depth qualitative data** from a small number of people, and **follow-up questions** can surface **why** each customer chose the way they did.  B. Interviews produce the **large quantitative sample** needed to represent the whole population.  C. Interviews record **customer behavior** in a natural environment.  D. Interviews **eliminate bias** because customers answer in their own words.

**Answer: A.** **Match the method to the data still MISSING.** The A/B test delivered **quantitative** data (how many/much/often — counts bookings, explains none). The calls collect **qualitative** data (why/how). Interviews and focus groups exist for that: **deep answers from a few people**, ideally highly engaged customers, with follow-up questions pulling out the full explanation.
**Traps:**

- **B** — describes a **survey** (large quantitative data reflecting a whole population); ten calls represent nobody and were never meant to.
- **D** — fails on **"eliminate"**: skew is **reduced** by neutral wording + a sample large/representative enough to mirror the population; **no method erases bias completely.**

> **Surveys and tests deliver counts; interviews and focus groups deliver reasons.**

## 4. Q4–Q6 (standalone)

### Q4 (6:19) — why start with secondary research?

> Which best explains why a business assessing a new market opportunity typically **begins with secondary-source research**?
>
> A. Secondary sources reveal the preferences of the business's **own customers**.  B. Secondary data is **always more current and accurate** than primary data.  C. Published sources already hold **market size, trends, segments, and rival information**, so the landscape can be assessed at **far lower cost** than running original research.  D. Secondary research is the **only way** to test a hypothesis.

**Answer: C.** Secondary research pulls **existing** info from external sources (government, commercial, academic). Those already measure **market size** (dollars + total customers), track **trends**, map **segments**, profile **rivals**, and cover **PESTEL** forces. Reading what someone else gathered costs a fraction of fielding your own study → size up the landscape secondhand, and **save primary research for questions only your own customers can answer.**
**Traps:**

- **A** — **reverses the division of labor**: your **own** customers' preferences live in **primary** research; no government database has interviewed them.
- **B** — the **absolutes** tell: published data can be **stale or aggregated**; its real advantages are **cost and coverage.**

> **Secondary research buys the landscape cheap; primary research buys answers about your own customers.**

### Q5 (8:15) — survey table

**Fernline Nursery** surveyed customers (neutrally worded questions) about a proposed **home-delivery** service:

| | Count |
|---|---|
| Customers surveyed | **200** |
| Would use home delivery | **120** |
| Would not | 80 |
| Of the 120 interested, willing to pay a **\$10** fee | **30** |

> Which conclusion is supported by the table?
>
> A. **25%** of all surveyed customers would pay the \$10 fee.  B. A **majority** expressed interest in delivery, and **15%** of all surveyed customers would **both use it and pay the fee**.  C. The service **will be profitable** because most customers want it.  D. The 80 who declined **would use** delivery if the fee were dropped.

**Answer: B.**

$$
\frac{120}{200}=60\% \text{ (a majority)} \qquad \frac{30}{200}=15\%
$$

Both halves stay inside what the 200 respondents actually said.
**Traps:**

- **A** — a correct division on the **wrong base:** $\frac{30}{120}=25\%$ is the share of **interested** customers; the claim says **all surveyed**, so the base is 200 and the number is **15%.**
- **C** — leaps from **wanting** to **profiting.** Interest = evidence of **desirability** (a product creating value by fitting a real problem). **Viability** (potential profit) needs revenue weighed against delivery costs the table never shows; **feasibility** (does the nursery have vans/staff/hours within its resources?) is a third test the table skips.

> Research informs the decision and predicts imperfectly; businesses often act on limited/unclear/contradictory data anyway. **Check the base under every percentage; keep conclusions inside what the table measured.**

### Q6 (10:32) — choose the visualization

> A pottery studio earns revenue from **classes, finished pieces, and kiln-firing services.** The owner wants **one chart** showing how **total revenue changed across three years** AND **how much each line contributed** to each year's total. Which visualization?
>
> A. A **pie chart** (each line's share of one year's revenue).  B. A **line graph** (revenue trend across three years).  C. A **bar chart** (comparing the three lines in the most recent year).  D. A **stacked bar chart** — one bar per year, divided into segments for the three lines.

**Answer: D.** The rule: choose the tool that shows the **exact insight** you need to communicate. This request carries **two layers:** totals compared across years, **and** the mix inside each total. A plain bar chart compares individual data points (three annual totals side by side) — **one layer.** Stacking adds the second: each bar's **height = that year's total**, and its **segments** show what classes/pieces/firing contributed.
**Traps:**

- **B** — carries the **trend** half and drops the **mix**: a line graph tracks **one value** over time and shows nothing inside each point.
- **A** — carries the **mix** half and drops the **trend**: a pie chart shows a part-to-whole split at **one moment**; three years of change would take **three separate pies** with no clean comparison of totals.

> **Totals over time PLUS the parts inside each total = stacked bars. One layer alone = bar, line, or pie.**

## 5. Trap Summary (patterns across all six)

| # | Trap type | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hypothesis vs. finding** (timing: fed in vs. handed back); hypothesis vs. segment (a claim, not a group) | Q1 |
| 2 | **A/B test vs. observation** (intervention/control); experiment vs. focus group (behavior vs. opinions) | Q2 |
| 3 | **Match the method to the data still MISSING**; interviews ≠ survey; no method **eliminates** bias | Q3 |
| 4 | **Secondary = the landscape, cheap** (cost + coverage); own customers = primary; "always more current/accurate" absolutes | Q4 |
| 5 | **Check the BASE under every percentage** (30/120 vs. 30/200); **desirability ≠ viability ≠ feasibility**; stay inside the data | Q5 |
| 6 | **Chart by layers:** totals-over-time + parts-inside = **stacked bar**; line/pie each carry only one layer | Q6 |

**Three cross-question tips:**

- **Hypothesis in → finding out;** and **experiment (deliberate change) vs. observation (untouched watching).**
- **Match the method to the missing data:** surveys/tests give **counts**, interviews/focus groups give **reasons**; secondary gives the **landscape cheap**, primary gives **your own customers.**
- **Data questions:** check the **base** of every percentage, keep conclusions **inside** the table, and don't confuse **desirability / viability / feasibility.**
