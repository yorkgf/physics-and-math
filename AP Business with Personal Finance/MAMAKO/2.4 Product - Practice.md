## 2. The Stimulus: Trued Cycles — First Year (0:18)

> **Theo**, 16, spent his first year building **Trued Cycles** at a flea-market stall. His **minimum viable product** was one \$35 mountain bike with a bent wheel, repaired using free videos and a borrowed floor pump, sold with a handwritten checklist. It sold for **\$90** — a **\$55 profit** on the first bike. Two weeks later a stranger he had never contacted asked for a checked bike because the first buyer had sent him.
>
> The orange tag on every commuter now reads: **"Half of retail. Inspected. Guaranteed 30 days."** That one line serves as the brand's **value proposition**. Theo's **product positioning** is built to undercut the bike shop's cheapest new model by half while offering **21 documented safety checks** that an unchecked driveway sale cannot match.
>
> When a Saturday rival appeared with **photocopied inspection sheets**, Theo added a **personal signature** on every tag and a **30-day guarantee** that costs a copycat real money to honor. A separate shopper on a **\$40 budget** passes Theo's rack each week for the cheapest untagged bike in the row.

Watch for the artifacts: an **MVP** (the \$35 bike), **product-market fit** (the referral), a **value proposition** (the tag's one-liner), **product positioning** (vs. two rivals), a **brand** (the name and the tag), and **trademark-worthy** differentiation (the signature).

## 3. Q1–Q3 (shared Trued Cycles scenario)

### Q1 (1:07) — what validated product-market fit?

> Which piece of evidence from the stimulus **best** demonstrates that Theo's MVP achieved product-market fit?
>
> A. The \$55 profit on the first bike, because revenue exceeded cost.  B. The handwritten checklist included with the bike, because it documented the repairs.  C. The unprompted referral two weeks later — a stranger asked for a checked bike because the first buyer sent him, delivering demand at a profitable price with zero marketing spent.  D. The \$90 sale price, because it was higher than the \$35 cost of the bike.

**Answer: C.** Product-market fit = customer demand sufficient to generate profit. The unprompted referral is the cleanest signal a small business gets: demand arrives **on its own** at a profitable price with **zero marketing spent.** The first sale hinted at fit; the referral confirmed it.
**Traps:**

- **A** — the \$55 profit shows the **first** sale was profitable, which *hints* at fit but doesn't **confirm** it. One sale can be luck; an unsolicited second customer from a referral is a pattern.
- **D** — restates A in different numbers. \$90 > \$35 is arithmetic, not evidence of sustained demand.

> **One profitable sale hints at fit. An unprompted referral confirms it.**

### Q2 (2:48) — the value proposition

> Theo's tag reads: **"Half of retail. Inspected. Guaranteed 30 days."** This one line is best described as which?
>
> A. A **product position**, because it compares Theo's bike to rival products.  B. A **brand trademark**, because it appears on every tag Theo produces.  C. A **value proposition** — a statement that names the target customer, the problem addressed, and why this product beats the alternatives.  D. A **marketing mix**, because it covers product, price, and promotion in one sentence.

**Answer: C.** A value proposition is built from market research and answers three questions in one statement: **who** the product serves (parents buying a commuter bike — "half of retail" signals affordability), **what problem** it addresses (a safe bike without the new-bike price — "inspected"), and **why it beats** the alternatives ("guaranteed 30 days" — no rival table offers that).
**Traps:**

- **A** — product positioning is the **strategy** that shapes how customers perceive the product relative to rivals; it runs **behind** the value proposition. The tag **delivers** the proposition; the positioning is the thinking that wrote it.
- **D** — a marketing mix is the full set of tactical decisions across product, price, place, and promotion. One line on a tag is not a mix.

> **Value proposition = who + problem + why us. Positioning is the strategy behind it.**

### Q3 (4:36) — the signature and guarantee

> When a rival appeared with photocopied inspection sheets, Theo responded by adding a personal signature and a 30-day guarantee. This is best understood as which?
>
> A. **Raising prices** to signal higher quality than the rival.  B. **Expanding distribution** to reach more customers than the rival.  C. **Differentiation through a brand identity** — adding elements that are costly for a copycat to replicate, strengthening the product's distinctiveness.  D. **Cost leadership** — reducing Theo's costs so he can undercut the rival on price.

**Answer: C.** Differentiation means making the product **distinct** from rivals in ways customers value. A personal signature ties the tag to a **named individual** (accountability), and a guarantee that costs real money to honor creates a **financial commitment** no copycat can cheaply match. Both are brand-identity elements that **raise the cost of imitation.**
**Traps:**

- **A** — Theo didn't raise his price; the sticker stayed at \$95. The quality signal comes from the signature and guarantee, not a higher number.
- **B** — nothing in the stimulus mentions new channels or reaching more customers. This is about **what** Theo sells, not **where** he sells it.

> **Differentiation ≠ higher price. Differentiation = making the product harder to copy.**

## 4. Q4–Q6 (standalone)

### Q4 (6:12) — generic products

> **Libra Coffee** roasts and bags its own beans, selling branded 250 g bags for \$18 through its café and online store. A regional grocery chain asks Libra to supply **unbranded bags** for the chain's store-brand coffee line, priced at \$9. Libra agrees. Which concept **best** describes Libra's decision?
>
> A. **Brand extension** — launching a new product line under the existing brand name.  B. **Generic product** — offering an unbranded version for customers who prioritize cost savings over brand recognition.  C. **Product positioning** — changing how consumers perceive Libra relative to grocery competitors.  D. **Price skimming** — charging a higher price to early adopters before lowering it.

**Answer: B.** A generic product is an **unbranded** good aimed at customers who want to **save money** and do not care about brand names. Libra strips its label so the grocery chain can sell the beans under its own store brand — same beans, no Libra identity, lower price. That is the generic-product strategy.
**Traps:**

- **A** — a brand extension keeps the **brand name** (Libra-branded cold brew, Libra-branded espresso capsules). An unbranded bag is the **opposite** of a brand extension — it removes the name.
- **C** — product positioning is about **customer perception** of a branded product relative to rivals. An unbranded bag has no Libra perception to shape.

> **Generic = no brand, lower price. Brand extension = same brand, new product.**

### Q5 (8:00) — PLC stage identification

> **Pace Bottles** makes reusable water bottles. In its second year, total sales are **40 % higher** than year one. Three new competitors have launched reusable-bottle lines in the same period. Pace's profits are positive and growing. Which stage of the product life cycle is Pace most likely in?
>
> A. Introduction — sales are rising because the product is new.  B. Growth — sales are climbing rapidly, profits are positive, and visible demand is pulling new competitors into the market.  C. Maturity — sales have peaked and the market is saturated.  D. Decline — sales are falling as customers switch to substitutes.

**Answer: B.** Growth-stage signals: sales rising at an **increasing rate** (40 % year-on-year), profits turning positive, and **new competitors entering** the market because they can see the demand. Every signal in the stimulus matches growth.
**Traps:**

- **A** — in introduction, almost nobody knows the product exists and sales are **low.** 40 % growth and three new competitors indicate the market already **knows** the product.
- **C** — maturity is **peak sales, flattening growth, saturated market.** Pace is still accelerating, not plateauing.

> **"Sales rising fast + competitors appearing" = growth. "Flat + saturated" = maturity.**

### Q6 (9:41) — marketing focus by PLC stage

> **Volt Cycles** launches an electric commuter bike. Sales volume is low in the first three months because almost nobody in the city has heard of the product. Which marketing strategy is **most** appropriate for this stage?
>
> A. Heavy investment in promotion to build **brand awareness** among the target segment.  B. Reducing the price and offering loyalty programs to retain existing customers.  C. Harvesting remaining profit and preparing to **discontinue** the product.  D. Switching to a **subscription model** to generate recurring revenue.

**Answer: A.** Low sales because "almost nobody knows it exists" = **introduction stage.** The marketing focus in introduction is building **awareness**: advertising, demonstrations, free trials — anything that teaches the target segment the product exists and is worth trying.
**Traps:**

- **B** — loyalty programs and lower prices are **maturity-stage** tactics. In introduction there are too few customers to retain; the challenge is **finding** them, not keeping them.
- **C** — harvesting and discontinuing are **decline-stage** actions. Introduction is the beginning, not the end.

> **Introduction = awareness. Maturity = loyalty. Decline = harvest or reinvent.**

## 5. Trap Summary (patterns across all six)

| # | Trap type | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **One sale ≠ product-market fit** — a hint, not confirmation; an unprompted referral is the cleanest signal | Q1 |
| 2 | **Value proposition ≠ positioning** — the proposition is the statement; the positioning is the strategy behind it | Q2 |
| 3 | **Differentiation ≠ price change** — making a product harder to copy, not charging more for it | Q3 |
| 4 | **Generic product vs. brand extension** — unbranded (generic) removes the name; a brand extension keeps it | Q4 |
| 5 | **PLC stage signals:** rising fast + competitors entering = **growth**; flat + saturated = **maturity**; low + unknown = **introduction** | Q5 |
| 6 | **Match marketing to PLC stage** — awareness for introduction, loyalty for maturity, harvest for decline | Q6 |

**Three cross-question tips:**

- **Product-market fit is confirmed by unprompted demand**, not by one profitable sale. The MVP earns the first dollar; the referral earns the second.
- **Value proposition = who + what problem + why us.** Product positioning is the **strategy** that makes the proposition land. Generic products skip branding entirely for cost savings.
- **PLC stage is determined by sales data and competitive dynamics**, not by how new the product is. Match the marketing focus to the stage: awareness → differentiation → loyalty → harvest or reinvent.
