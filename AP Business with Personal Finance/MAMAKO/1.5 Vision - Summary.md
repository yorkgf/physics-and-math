## 1. The Two Offers (0:00)

The snack box is three weeks old and already has regulars — then one week brings **two offers:**

1. A wholesale-business parent offers to **bankroll a candy-only mega cart**: double the inventory, double the margin — but **the fruit the families asked for would have to go.**
2. The league asks him to take over the **far field's snack table** — a bigger job that **pays less at first** and runs entirely on trust.

One pair of hands, one Saturday, two doors. **Nothing in his cash box answers this**, because it's a decision about **what the business is *for*** and **what it is *best at*** — not about the arithmetic.

---

## 2. Core Values (1:04)

**Core values** = the **defining beliefs and principles** that guide an individual's or a business's actions. Examples: creativity, excellence, transparency, empathy, reliability.

They sound abstract until a decision makes them concrete — he writes three on the **lid of his cooler**: *show up on time · listen to the families · keep prices fair.*

**Businesses communicate core values for two reasons:**

1. **Align employees** to a shared purpose.
2. **Guide decision makers** toward choices consistent with what the business believes.

> His cousin helps on Saturdays, and **the cooler lid is how the cousin makes the right call when nobody's watching.** That's the job values do: **turn one person's judgment into the whole team's default.**

**Run the mega cart past the lid:** double margin, but the fruit goes — and the families asked for that fruit two weeks ago, so the cart **fails "listen to the families" before the math even starts.**

> **A value the business abandons the first time money appears was a decoration.** Test any stated value by asking: **what did it cost the business at the last real decision?** (Transparency → what goes on the label; empathy → how a refund is handled; excellence → whether a flawed batch ships.)

---

## 3. Core Competencies (2:36)

**Core competencies** = the **capabilities, skills, and expertise** that help an individual or a business **outperform rivals and achieve competitive advantage.** Examples: innovation, customer service, communication, ethical behavior, efficiency.

His list: the **pre-order system** in the group chat (nobody else takes orders before game day), the **halftime handoff** (one minute per team), and a **reputation for showing up** (carried from the hallway in 1.1).

> **The pair the exam loves to swap — pin the difference now:**

| | Core **value** | Core **competency** |
|---|---|---|
| It is… | what the business **believes** | what the business **is good at** |
| Example | Reliability on the cooler lid (a belief he enforces) | The 60-second handoff (a skill he built) |

**Competitive advantage** (from 1.2) = the edge that lets one business win customers from a rival; **core competencies feed that edge.** A differentiated product, a lower cost, a faster service — trace an advantage backward and you usually find **a capability somebody developed.**

> **Rule of thumb:** pursue the opportunities your **strengths can win**, and spend resources where those strengths **compound.** The far-field table leans on his logistics + reputation; the mega cart leans mostly on **someone else's money.** (A caterer whose strength is on-time setup grows by booking more events — not by selling kitchen equipment.)

## 4. Your Own Decisions (4:31)

The same tool points at **you.** Individuals consider their own core values and core competencies when choosing **educational paths, careers, and jobs.**

- A student who values **independence** and is skilled with **systems** reads one set of options; one who values **service** and is skilled with **people** reads another.
- **Two job offers:** one pays **20% more** but demands weekend hours that collide with something you refuse to give up; the other pays **less** but builds a skill already on your strengths list.
- The bigger number wins **only if money outranks every value on your list.**

> **Knowing your list before the offer arrives is the entire advantage.** (The candy seller is learning he enjoys **building systems** more than selling candy.) Write your own two lists — values and strengths — and check every opportunity against them the way the cooler lid checked the mega cart.

## 5. Vision and Mission (5:30)

Two documents students constantly confuse:

| | **Vision statement** | **Mission statement** |
|---|---|---|
| Describes | the **future** the business is trying to create | **what the business does** and **how** it will achieve its long-term goals |
| In one word | aspiration | the operation |

- His vision: **"game days where no family scrambles."**
- His mission: **"deliver pre-ordered snack boxes to every team by halftime, reliably, at prices families can trust."**

> **Test any statement with one question:** does it describe a **future worth wanting**, or does it describe the **work**? **Future = vision. Work = mission.**

These statements earn their space **twice:**

- **Internally** — communicate long-term goals + core values, guide decision making, give everyone a shared purpose (the cooler lid grown up into a document).
- **Externally** — inform potential customers and investors of goals + values *before a single transaction.* (When the wholesale parent reads the mission, **he learns the fruit stays.**)

> **The labels are unreliable:** the words "vision" and "mission" rarely appear inside the statements themselves, so **classify by content.**
>
> - "A city where every student graduates ready" → no operations, all aspiration → **vision.**
> - "We provide afterschool math tutoring in three neighborhoods, priced by family income" → what it does + how → **mission.**

## 6. Three Kinds of Organizations (7:21)

### Businesses

Seek to **achieve and increase profits**, fulfill their stated missions/goals, and **remain competitive and viable** long-term. Profit improves in exactly **two ways:** **increase revenues** or **decrease costs** (many work both). That's the **revenue − cost** arithmetic from 1.1, now running a whole organization.

### Social enterprise

Seeks **profit while also, by design, achieving social objectives.** Impact travels through **three channels:**

| Channel | Example |
|---|---|
| The **products** themselves | A company whose product is a low-cost water filter |
| **Operations** | A moving company that hires workers other employers overlook |
| The **financial model** | A brand that routes a share of every sale to a local scholarship fund |

### Nonprofit organization

Serves the **public good** rather than generating profit for owners.

> **Mark the precision — the exam tests the edges:** a nonprofit **can** sell goods and services, and its revenues **can** exceed its costs. The law draws **one hard line: any surplus must be reinvested in the organization, and none of it may flow to owners.**

- Nonprofits often add **grants** and **donations** to revenue (the mission usually costs more than sales alone can carry).
- **Grant** = funding awarded for a stated purpose, usually by a government or foundation; earned **by application, not by sale.**
- **Donations** arrive with **no product attached.**
- **Volunteers** lower the **labor** side of the cost ledger — how many nonprofits deliver services no business could price.

**All three at the park on Saturday:**

| Who | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| His snack box | **Business** | Profit is his to keep or reinvest |
| The league itself | **Nonprofit** | Registration fees + bake-sale table fund uniforms and goals; surplus buys nets, never a payout |
| Parent floating a drink stand, donating half its profit to field-repair | **Social enterprise** | Profit-seeking + a built-in social objective |

> Same park, three different answers to **whom the gains ultimately serve.**

## 7. The Classification Tells (exam habit)

This is a **classification topic** — collect the tells:

| Signal in the scenario | Points to |
|---|---|
| "Believes" | Core **value** |
| "Good at" | Core **competency** |
| "Aspires" (a future worth wanting) | **Vision** |
| "Does and how" (the work) | **Mission** |

**For organization type, ask two questions in order:**

1. **Can owners keep the profit at all?** If **no** (every surplus feeds the mission) → **nonprofit.**
2. If **yes** → look for a **social objective** built into the products, operations, or financial model: **find one → social enterprise; find none → business.**

---

## 8. Exam Points the Video Emphasizes

- **Core value = believes; core competency = good at** — the swap the exam loves.
- **Test a stated value** by what it cost at the last real decision.
- **Competencies feed competitive advantage** (1.2); pursue opportunities your strengths can win.
- **Vision = future/aspiration; mission = does + how** — classify by **content**, not the label.
- **Profit improves only two ways:** raise revenue or cut cost.
- **Social enterprise impact:** through products, operations, or financial model.
- **Nonprofit edge-case precision:** can sell goods, can run a surplus — but **no surplus to owners**, ever.
- **Organization-type decision tree:** owners keep profit? → no = nonprofit; yes → has a built-in social objective = social enterprise, else = business.
