## 2. The Stimulus: GreenBox (0:19)

> **Elena Reyes** owns **GreenBox**, a meal-prep delivery service in a mid-size city. Three developments have her attention:
>
> 1. The city council is **debating a proposal to tax sugary packaged foods**.
> 2. A **fitness lifestyle trend** is growing among adults in the city, and **gym memberships have risen for three straight years**.
> 3. A new **state law** will require every food delivery business to hold a **certified food-handler license** within one year.
>
> Elena is deciding whether to **expand GreenBox into two nearby towns**.

Note the three factors hiding in plain sight: a proposed **tax** (policy choice), a **lifestyle trend** (social), and a **licensing law** (legal).

---

## 3. Q1–Q3 (shared GreenBox scenario)

### Q1 (1:03) — classify the fitness trend

> The growing fitness lifestyle trend and rising gym memberships are best classified as which type of PESTEL factor?
>
> A. Economic  B. Social  C. Technological  D. Political

**Answer: B.**
**Why:** Social factors are trends in society and culture that affect consumers, including **lifestyle trends**, cultural norms, and demographics. A fitness trend that changes what people want to eat is a lifestyle trend **acting on consumer demand.**
**Trap: A.** Rising gym memberships involve people **spending money**, and spending sounds economic. But economic factors describe the **condition of the economy itself** (income, inflation, unemployment, stability).

> **Master rule:** **Classify by the source of the change.** A change in what people choose to value is a **social** trend, *even when the choice costs money* — here the source is culture.

### Q2 (2:09) — the paired-concept swap

> The proposed tax on sugary packaged foods and the new food-handler license requirement are best classified, **respectively**, as which two PESTEL factors?
>
> A. Legal, then political  B. Political, then environmental  C. Economic, then legal  D. Political, then legal

**Answer: D.** A proposed **tax** is a policy choice — taxes, subsidies, mandates, and bans are **political** factors. A specific **law** requiring a license to operate is a **legal** factor (concrete laws/regulations a business must comply with).
**Trap: A** — **swaps the pair.** This is the paired-concept swap for this topic; the sorting rule from the content video settles it: **political** = broad policy choice (tax/ban); **legal** = specific rule to follow (license/safety code). **C** fails on the first half: a tax affects money, but **its source is a government policy decision**, and source decides the classification.

### Q3 (3:29) — how to actually use the framework

> Based on the scenario, which best describes how Elena should use the PESTEL framework in her expansion decision?
>
> A. Adopt the framework only after choosing which town to enter.  B. Weigh only the factors that favor expansion.  C. Identify the relevant factors and assess how each affects the attractiveness and risks of each town.  D. Treat all six factors as equally important in every market.

**Answer: C.**
**Why:** The framework works by identifying the factors **relevant to the specific product and market**, then assessing how each one impacts **attractiveness and risk.** The license law is a cost in every town; the fitness trend may be **stronger in one town than another**; the comparison is the decision.
**Trap: D.** A scenario will rarely make all six matter **equally**, because relevance depends on the business: a delivery service cares intensely about a licensing law, while a candy stand doesn't care about internet speeds.

> **"Treat every factor as equally weighted" sounds thorough and reads as a wrong answer.**

## 4. Q4–Q6 (standalone)

### Q4 (4:49) — reading a data table

The table shows two indicators for two regions:

| Region | Median household income | Unemployment |
|---|---|---|
| North | **+8%** over 3 years | 7% → **4%** |
| South | **−3%** over the same period | 5% → **9%** |

> A business selling **premium home fitness equipment** is comparing the two regions. Based on the economic factors shown, which conclusion is **supported by the table**?
>
> A. Region North currently offers the more attractive economic conditions for a premium product.  B. Region South's population is declining.  C. Region North's residents value fitness more than Region South's residents.  D. The business is **guaranteed** higher profits in Region North.

**Answer: A.** Rising incomes and falling unemployment mean more households can **afford a premium product** — exactly what the numbers show. The conclusion **stays inside the data** and adds nothing beyond it.
**Traps:**

- **C** **invents a social factor** — the table shows income and unemployment and says **nothing about what anyone values**. A fitness preference is never supported by economic data alone.
- **D** uses **"guarantee"** — the absolutes rule from earlier topics still applies: favorable conditions **raise the odds** and guarantee nothing.

### Q5 (6:35) — solar-panel subsidies

> A country's government begins offering large **subsidies** to businesses that install solar panels on their facilities. Which statement best describes this development in PESTEL terms?
>
> A. A legal factor, because subsidies are written into law.  B. An environmental factor, because solar panels affect the environment.  C. A political factor that incentivizes a specific business activity.  D. A technological factor, because solar panels are technology.

**Answer: C.** Subsidies are **policy tools** (alongside taxes, mandates, bans); their purpose here is to **incentivize** an activity the government wants more of — the definition of a political factor.
**Trap: B** — teaches the sorting rule one more time:

> **Classify by the source of the force, never by the subject matter it touches.** The subsidy *concerns* solar panels, but the *force acting on the market* is a government policy choice. A scenario about environmental technology can still be a **political** factor — and the exam builds wrong answers out of exactly that confusion.

### Q6 (7:53) — fiber internet reaches rural areas

> A homework-help app operates in a region where most households have high-speed internet. A new **fiber network** then brings fast internet to the region's **rural areas for the first time**. Which statement best describes the effect of this change?
>
> A. It raises a barrier to entry that protects the app from rivals.  B. A technological factor changed, expanding the app's viable market.  C. It converts the app's service from a want into a need.  D. It is a social factor, because more people can now join online communities.

**Answer: B.** Internet access is a **technological** factor, and when it spreads, the set of customers a technology-dependent business can serve **grows with it**. A market that was out of reach becomes viable **without the app changing anything about its product.**
**Trap: A** — **borrows Topic 1.2 vocabulary.** A barrier to entry is an obstacle that keeps **new firms out**, and wider internet access is the **opposite** of an obstacle: it opens the market to the app **and to future rivals alike.**

> **Recognizing a term from a neighboring topic is never enough — the scenario has to match what the term means.**

## 5. Trap Summary (patterns across all six)

| # | Trap type | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Spending **sounds economic**, but classify by **source** — a lifestyle choice is **social** even when it costs money | Q1 |
| 2 | **Paired-concept swap** (political vs. legal) and "affects money" ≠ economic — **source** decides | Q2 |
| 3 | **"All six equally important"** sounds thorough but is wrong — relevance depends on the business | Q3 |
| 4 | In data questions, **don't invent a factor** the table doesn't show (no "values" from income data) | Q4 |
| 5 | **Absolutes** ("guarantee") — favorable conditions raise odds, not certainty | Q4 |
| 6 | Classify by the **source of the force**, not the **subject matter** it touches (solar subsidy = political) | Q5 |
| 7 | **Borrowing neighboring-topic vocabulary** (barrier to entry from 1.2) — wider access ≠ an obstacle | Q6 |

**Three cross-question tips:**

- **The master rule of this topic:** classify by the **source** of the change — not by what it costs, what it touches, or its subject matter.
- **Political vs. legal:** broad policy *choice* (tax/ban/subsidy) vs. specific *rule* to comply with (license/safety code).
- **Data + absolutes:** a data question only supports what the figures show; and "always/never/guarantee" is almost always the wrong answer.
