Unit 1: Business Fundamentals

1.1 What Is a Business

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Business An organization or entity that produces and distributes products (goods and/or services); can be any size, face-to-face or virtual.
2 Product A good, a service, or both — what a business produces and distributes.
3 Goods Tangible items (a candy bar, a phone, a desk).
4 Services Work performed for compensation (a haircut, a tutoring session, a flight).
5 Revenue Money earned from selling, before expenses.
6 Profit What remains after all expenses are subtracted. (Profit = Revenue − Total Cost)
7 Customer The individual or business that purchases a good or service.
8 Consumer The individual who uses a good or service, whether or not they bought it.
9 Need Something essential for survival (food, water, shelter, basic healthcare).
10 Want Improves life without being essential (a Netflix subscription, new sneakers).
11 Market opportunity A customer problem, need, or want that a business might address.
12 Problem-solution fit The match between a customer problem and a product built to solve it.
13 Value The worth or benefit of a product to customers (measured by the highest price they’d willingly pay).
14 Value creation When a business provides a product that responds to customers’ problems, needs, or wants.
15 Value capture When a business charges a higher price than it cost to produce. (Value captured = Price − Cost)
16 Value proposition A clear statement of how a product solves a customer problem and why choose it over alternatives.

1.2 Markets and Competitive Advantage

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Market Any physical or virtual space where sellers interact with their buyers; can be local, regional, or global.
2 Voluntary exchange A transaction in which both sides walk away better off — sellers earn revenue, buyers receive value.
3 Prevailing market price The going price for a good or service in a competitive market that no individual seller can hold themselves above.
4 Competitive advantage The ability to outperform rivals, leading to increased market share and potentially increased profits.
5 Market share The portion of a market’s total sales that one business controls.
6 Rival businesses Businesses competing for the same customers in the same market.
7 Differentiation Building products with distinguishing features that set them apart from competitors’ offerings.
8 Barrier to entry An obstacle that makes it difficult for new firms to compete in a market.
9 Monopoly A market without competition, in which only one business operates and produces a unique good or service.
10 Cost leadership A strategy of achieving competitive advantage by producing as efficiently as possible to charge the lowest price — common in commodity and agricultural markets where products are undifferentiated.

1.3 PESTEL Factors

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 PESTEL An acronym for the six kinds of outside forces that shape the business landscape: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal.
2 Viability The ability to survive and stay successful in a market over time.
3 Political factors Policies and political dynamics that affect market activity: trade policy, taxes, subsidies, mandates, bans, political stability.
4 Economic factors Conditions of the economy itself: economic stability, household income, inflation, unemployment, interest rates.
5 Social factors Trends in society and culture that affect consumers: demographics, cultural norms, lifestyle trends, population growth.
6 Technological factors Features of a market tied to the availability of technology: internet access, automation, pace of innovation.
7 Environmental factors External environmental conditions that promote or limit market activity: geography, access to resources, waste rules, customer attitudes toward the environment.
8 Legal factors Specific laws, rules, and regulations that impact market activity: employment laws, consumer protection, health and safety rules, IP protection, antitrust laws.

1.4 How Business Ideas Originate

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Gap A need or want that is not currently being met — an unmet need in a market. Can exist even when competitors are present, if a specific need goes unserved.
2 Opportunity A new or better way to do something — often triggered by a change in technology, society, rules, or resources.
3 Existing business What is already being sold in a market — a source of ideas through replication, adaptation, or improvement.
4 Replication Copying a business model that works in one market and applying it in another — one of the most common sources of business ideas worldwide.
5 Observation The skill of watching what people struggle with, complain about, or can’t find — the primary way entrepreneurs discover gaps.
6 Risk The chance that things will not go as planned — the possibility of unwanted outcomes like losing money, time, or effort. Can be managed but never fully eliminated.
7 Return The gain (usually money) that an entrepreneur hopes to earn from a business. Never guaranteed.
8 Risk–return tradeoff A situation where taking more risk gives the chance of a higher return, but also a higher chance of losing everything.
9 Viability The ability to survive and stay successful in a market over time (carried forward from 1.3).
10 Entrepreneur A person who takes on the risk of starting and running a business.
11 Entrepreneurial motivation The reasons entrepreneurs bear risk, including potential profits, satisfaction of solving a problem, and pursuing a passion — not all motivation is financial.

1.5 Vision, Mission, and Values

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Core values The defining beliefs and principles that guide an individual’s or business’s actions. Examples: creativity, excellence, transparency, empathy, reliability.
2 Core competencies The capabilities, skills, and expertise that help an individual or business outperform rivals and achieve competitive advantage. Examples: innovation, customer service, communication, ethical behavior, efficiency.
3 Vision statement A concise description of the future the business is trying to create — its aspirations and core values. Describes a future worth wanting, not the work.
4 Mission statement Describes what the business does and how it will achieve its long-term goals. Describes the work — the operation, not the aspiration.
5 Business An organization that seeks to achieve and increase profits, fulfill its mission/goals, and remain competitive and viable. Owners keep or reinvest profit.
6 Social enterprise An organization that seeks profit while also, by design, achieving social objectives. Impact travels through products, operations, or financial model. Owners keep profit.
7 Nonprofit organization An organization that serves the public good rather than generating profit for owners. Any surplus must be reinvested; no payment to owners.
8 Grant Funding awarded for a stated purpose, usually by a government or foundation; earned by application, not by sale.
9 Donation Money or resources given to an organization with no product attached in return.
10 Competitive advantage The ability to outperform rivals in the same market, leading to increased market share and potentially profits. Core competencies feed competitive advantage.

1.6 Business Ethics

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Unethical behavior Actions that violate moral or professional standards
2 Code of conduct A written document outlining expected ethical behavior and standards
3 Training Teaching employees to follow ethical guidelines through ongoing instruction and practice scenarios
4 Internal repercussion A consequence imposed after an ethical transgression (warning, suspension, termination)
5 Modeling (ethical behavior) A leader demonstrates ethical behavior in front of employees, so the standard is learned by observation
6 Incentive A reward that encourages a particular action, offered before the behavior
7 Ethical dilemma A situation where a core value conflicts with another core value or with a business goal or practice
8 Internal stakeholder Individuals or groups with direct involvement in a business’s operations, decisions, and outcomes
9 External stakeholder Individuals or groups not directly employed or involved but with a vested interest in the business’s decisions and outcomes
10 Brand loyalty Ongoing customer preference for a business’s products based on trust and positive experience

Trap Pairs

# Term A Term B Key Difference
1 Ethical dilemma Unethical behavior Both involve ethics — Dilemma = two sides with real trade-offs; unethical behavior = a specific wrong act (lying, hiding, taking)
2 Incentive Internal repercussion Both influence employee behavior — Incentive = reward BEFORE behavior (pulls toward); repercussion = consequence AFTER behavior (pushes back)
3 Modeling Internal repercussion Both involve a leader taking action — Modeling = leader DEMONSTRATES the standard; repercussion = leader PUNISHES a transgression
4 Internal stakeholder External stakeholder Both affected by business decisions — Internal = direct involvement in operations; external = vested interest only
5 Greatest total benefit One-column total Both involve adding numbers — Must sum across EVERY stakeholder group (internal + external); stopping at one column is the trap
6 Right decision + broken reason Right decision + valid reason Both reach the same verdict — A conclusion supported by “always” or “guarantee” is wrong reasoning even if the verdict is correct

1.7 Organization, Roles, and Responsibilities

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Sole proprietorship A business with one owner who has full control, keeps all profits, and is personally liable for all debts
2 Partnership A business with two or more owners who share control and profits and are personally liable for all debts
3 Limited liability company (LLC) A business structure where owners retain control and profits but are shielded from personal liability
4 Corporation A business structure owned by shareholders who cede control to an elected board of directors in exchange for greater access to funding
5 Personal liability The owner’s personal assets are at risk for business debts and obligations
6 Limited liability (liability shield) Business owners are not personally responsible for business debts — only the business’s assets are at risk
7 Organizational structure The arrangement of roles, responsibilities, authority, and communication within a business
8 Department A specialized functional unit within a larger business (e.g., accounting, marketing, operations)
9 Accounting The department that tracks expenditures and earnings and prepares financial statements to monitor financial health
10 Finance The department that secures and manages funds and uses financial data to recommend strategies for improving performance
11 Outsourcing Hiring another business to handle a function instead of doing it internally

Trap Pairs

# Term A Term B Key Difference
1 Sole proprietorship LLC Both have one or few owners who retain control — Sole prop = personal liability; LLC = liability shield
2 Partnership Corporation Both have multiple people in ownership — Partnership = owners retain control, personal liability; Corporation = owners cede control, liability shielded
3 Accounting Finance Both deal with money and financial data — Accounting = backward (records, statements); Finance = forward (funds, strategies)
4 Personal liability Limited liability Both describe who pays business debts — Personal = owners pay from pocket; Limited = only business assets at risk
5 Roles Departments Both describe who does what — Roles = how small teams divide work; Departments = how large businesses organize functions
6 Outsourcing Core competency Both involve a business function — Outsourcing = moving function out (cost-driven); Core competency = developing function in-house (competitive advantage)

1.8 Supply Chains

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Artisan production A production process that relies on skilled labor and close attention to detail, producing goods in smaller quantities
2 Mass production A production process that relies on technology, assembly lines, and machinery to produce goods in far larger quantities
3 Supply chain All of the individuals and businesses involved at each stage of producing and distributing a product — from raw materials to final customer delivery
4 Raw materials The basic natural resources or inputs used to manufacture a product
5 Manufacturing facility The location where workers and equipment turn raw materials into finished goods
6 Warehouse A facility that emphasizes storage of finished goods
7 Distribution center A facility that stores goods and moves them onward to stores and customers
8 Supplier A business that buys materials/goods from one business and sells them to another
9 Distributor A business that buys finished products from manufacturers and sells them onward to retail stores
10 Scaling Building higher-capacity, more efficient supply chains so that revenue increases outrun cost increases
11 Service supply chain Acquiring the employees, resources, and delivery systems needed to provide a service (in person or virtually)

Trap Pairs

# Term A Term B Key Difference
1 Artisan production Mass production Both produce physical goods — Artisan = skilled labor, small quantities; Mass = technology, large quantities
2 Warehouse Distribution center Both store goods — Warehouse = storage only; Distribution center = stores and moves onward
3 Supplier Distributor Both move goods between businesses — Supplier = materials into production; Distributor = finished goods out to retail
4 Buying inputs Outsourcing Both involve paying another business — Buying inputs = purchasing raw materials; Outsourcing = handing entire function to another business
5 Service supply chain No supply chain Services have no visible factory or warehouse — Services DO have supply chains made of people, resources, delivery systems
6 Cost increase Scaling failure Both show rising total costs — Scaling succeeds when revenue growth > cost growth; question is whether revenue rose faster

Formulas

Formula Calculation

Warehouse vs. distribution center Warehouse = storage; Distribution center = storage + forwarding

Unit 2: Marketing and Customers

2.1 Marketing to Customers

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Marketing All activities a business undertakes to identify customers’ problems, needs, and wants, and to promote, sell, and deliver products that meet them
2 Customer data Information about customers that helps marketers decide which customers to serve, which products to produce, and how to market effectively and profitably
3 Demographic characteristics Measurable qualities used to describe a population, such as age, sex, race, ethnicity, income, and location
4 Psychographic characteristics Cognitive and behavioral factors within a population, including individuals’ interests, activities, values, and lifestyles
5 Market segmentation Aggregating potential customers into groups (market segments) based on shared demographic and psychographic characteristics
6 Market segment A group of potential customers who share demographic and psychographic characteristics
7 Target customers The buyers most likely to purchase a specific product because of their wants, needs, and preferences
8 Customer profile A fictional description of a specific sample customer, incorporating demographic and psychographic data as well as wants, needs, and preferences
9 Personalized service A relationship tactic that treats a customer as a known individual
10 Customer acquisition cost (CAC) Total marketing, advertising, and sales costs used to acquire customers ÷ number of customers acquired
11 Lifetime value of a customer (LTV) The estimated amount of money a customer will spend on the business’s products over time
12 Data breach An incident that exposes stored customer information to outsiders

Additional Terms

# Term Definition
1 Subscriber list A digital tool that records who asked to hear from the business
2 Click-tracking software A digital tool that records what visitors tap or click on a website
3 Social media monitoring Reading what people say publicly about a product on social platforms
4 Rewards program A relationship tactic that gives repeat buyers a reason to return
5 Customer feedback opportunities A relationship tactic (e.g., satisfaction surveys) that asks buyers what to fix
6 Identity theft Using stolen personal details to impersonate a person
7 Fraud Using deception, often powered by stolen data, to take money or property
8 Targeting efficiency The cost and effectiveness advantage of designing products and marketing for a specific segment rather than appealing to a broad audience — targeting is more effective and less costly

Trap Pairs

# Term A Term B Key Difference
1 Demographic Psychographic Both describe customers — Demographic = measurable/census traits (who the person is) · Psychographic = cognitive/behavioral (what the person cares about)
2 Market segment Customer profile Both describe target customers — Segment = the group sharing traits · Profile = one fictional person who stands for the group
3 CAC LTV Both are per-customer numbers — CAC = what you spend to get a customer · LTV = what that customer spends on you over time
4 Advertising costs Acquisition costs Both involve spending to get customers — Advertising is one component; acquisition costs = marketing + advertising + sales (all of them)
5 Brand loyalty Repeat purchase Both describe coming back — Loyalty = emotional attachment to the brand · Repeat = buying again (may be convenience, not loyalty)
6 Privacy violation Data breach Both involve customer data problems — Privacy = collecting/storing without awareness or disclosure · Breach = data exposed to outsiders

2.2 Consumer Behavior

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Rational decision-making process A deliberate, step-by-step approach in which a consumer identifies a need, gathers information, evaluates alternatives, makes a purchase, and reviews the outcome
2 Habitual buying behavior Low-involvement, routine purchasing in which a consumer repeats a familiar choice with little conscious evaluation
3 Personal factors Individual characteristics—such as age, income, lifestyle, and personality—that shape what a consumer wants and can afford
4 Psychological factors Internal mental processes—motivation, perception, learning, and beliefs—that influence how a consumer interprets and reacts to marketing
5 Social and cultural factors External forces—family, reference groups, culture, and social class—that set norms and expectations for what a consumer considers acceptable or desirable
6 Situational influences Temporary, context-specific conditions—such as time pressure, store atmosphere, or mood—that affect a purchase decision at the moment
7 Purchasing pattern The observable regularity in a consumer’s buying, defined by timing, frequency, and quantity of purchases
8 Consumer protection laws Government regulations that safeguard buyers against unfair, deceptive, or unsafe business practices
9 Substitute products Alternative goods or services that satisfy the same underlying need, giving consumers a choice when one option is restricted or unavailable
10 Scarcity The perception that a product is limited in availability, which increases its psychological appeal and urgency to buy
11 Authority The tendency to defer to people perceived as experts or credible sources when making purchase decisions
12 Consensus/social proof The tendency to follow the actions of others, assuming that if many people buy something, it must be a good choice
13 Reciprocity The felt obligation to give something back after receiving a favor, gift, or concession from a seller
14 Consistency The desire to act in ways that are aligned with commitments one has already made, even small ones

Additional Terms

# Term Definition
1 Liking The tendency to be persuaded by people or brands we find attractive, similar to us, or complimentary toward us
2 Unity The feeling of shared identity—“we are the same kind of person”—that deepens influence beyond mere similarity
3 Face test A personal ethics check: “Would I be comfortable if the tactic I’m using were fully disclosed to the customer?”
4 Consequential purchase A high-stakes buying decision (large money, long commitment, high risk) that is more likely to trigger the rational decision-making process
5 Technology-driven purchasing shift Changes in consumer purchasing patterns caused by technology and innovation — e.g., the internet and smartphones enabled online shopping, price comparison, peer reviews, and new information/entertainment channels

Trap Pairs

# Term A Term B Key Difference
1 Rational decision-making Habitual buying Both describe how consumers buy — Rational = deliberate, step-by-step evaluation · Habitual = automatic, low-involvement routine
2 Personal factors Psychological factors Both operate inside the individual — Personal = who you are (age, income, lifestyle) · Psychological = what you think and feel (motivation, perception, beliefs)
3 Social/cultural factors Situational influences Both are external forces on a decision — Social/cultural = permanent norms and group expectations · Situational = temporary, moment-specific conditions (time, mood, store atmosphere)
4 Consensus/social proof Authority Both rely on others’ behavior or status — Consensus = “many people bought this” (quantity of buyers) · Authority = “an expert endorses this” (credibility of one source)
5 Reciprocity Liking Both involve a positive connection to the seller — Reciprocity = obligation from a favor received (give-back) · Liking = rapport from attraction or similarity (warmth)
6 Consistency Unity Both deepen commitment over time — Consistency = alignment with your own past actions (self-image) · Unity = alignment with a group’s identity (belonging)

Formulas

Formula Calculation
Purchasing pattern = timing + frequency + quantity Three observable dimensions of how a consumer buys
Four tiebreakers when choosing between similar products Price, features, availability, advertising — the criteria consumers use when two options seem equal

2.3 Market Research

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Market research The systematic process of gathering, analyzing, and interpreting information about a market, including potential customers and competitors, to guide business decisions.
2 Quantitative data Numerical information that can be measured and expressed using numbers, such as sales figures, percentages, or survey ratings.
3 Qualitative data Non-numerical information expressed in words, images, or observations, such as customer opinions, motivations, or feelings.
4 Desirability Whether a product or service solves a real customer problem or fulfills a genuine need (problem-solution fit).
5 Feasibility Whether the business has or can acquire the resources, technology, and capabilities needed to deliver the product or service.
6 Viability Whether the product or service can generate sustainable profit and long-term financial success.
7 Secondary-source research Analysis of data that already exists, such as government reports, industry publications, or competitor websites, to understand the broader market landscape.
8 Primary-source research Collection of new, original data directly from target customers through methods like surveys, interviews, experiments, and observations.
9 Business hypothesis A specific, testable assumption about a business idea (e.g., “Customers prefer Feature A over Feature B”) that is fed INTO a research method for validation.
10 A/B testing A controlled experiment that compares two versions (A and B) of a single variable—such as a webpage headline or pricing—by randomly assigning real customers to each version and measuring which performs better.
11 Survey A structured set of questions distributed to a large number of respondents to collect primarily quantitative data on preferences, behaviors, or demographics.
12 Data visualization The graphical representation of data through charts, graphs, or maps to make patterns, trends, and comparisons easier to understand.

Additional Terms

# Term Definition
1 Focus group A moderated discussion with a small group of target customers (typically 6-10) to explore attitudes, perceptions, and reactions to a product or concept.
2 One-on-one interview A direct, in-depth conversation with a single respondent to gather detailed qualitative insights, often including follow-up questions.
3 Experiment A controlled test in which a researcher deliberately changes one variable while holding others constant to observe the effect on behavior or outcomes.
4 Observation A research method in which a researcher watches and records customer behavior in natural settings without altering the environment or interacting with subjects.
5 Bar chart A chart that uses rectangular bars of varying heights to compare discrete categories or data points.
6 Stacked bar chart A bar chart in which each bar is divided into segments representing sub-categories, showing both the total and the composition of each category.
7 Line graph A graph that connects data points with lines to display trends or changes over a continuous period.
8 Pie chart A circular chart divided into slices that represent the proportion or percentage share of each category within a whole.
9 Sample bias A systematic error that occurs when the sample of respondents is not representative of the broader target population, leading to skewed or unreliable results.
10 Research method selection The process of choosing which primary research method (survey, interview, focus group, experiment, A/B test, observation) to use based on the types of data needed to test a specific business hypothesis.
11 Research limitations The reality that businesses cannot always accurately predict customer or market responses and must sometimes innovate with limited, unclear, or contradictory data — no research method eliminates all uncertainty.

Trap Pairs

# Term A Term B Key Difference
1 Quantitative Qualitative Both are types of data collected during research. — Quantitative = numbers you can count and graph (e.g., “72% prefer X”). Qualitative = words and images you interpret (e.g., “I like X because…”).
2 Secondary Primary Both aim to understand the market. — Secondary = data that already exists (government stats, industry reports). Primary = data you collect yourself (surveys, interviews).
3 Hypothesis Finding Both relate to the research cycle. — Hypothesis = the assumption you feed IN before testing (e.g., “Feature A is preferred”). Finding = the result handed BACK after analysis (e.g., “Feature B was preferred 70%-30%”).
4 A/B Test Observation Both study customer behavior. — A/B test = you deliberately change something (Version A vs. B) and compare outcomes. Observation = you watch behavior without changing anything.
5 Survey Interview Both collect customer input. — Survey = large sample, primarily quantitative, fixed questions, little or no follow-up. Interview = small sample, primarily qualitative, open-ended, with follow-up questions.
6 Desirability Viability Both assess whether to pursue a business idea. — Desirability = “Do customers want this?” (problem-solution fit). Viability = “Can we make money doing this?” (profitable path). A product can be desirable but not viable.

2.4 Product

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Idea Generation The first stage of product development where businesses brainstorm new concepts or gather suggestions from customers.
2 Idea Screening Evaluating potential product ideas by analyzing market size, competition, costs, and regulatory barriers before moving forward.
3 Prototype A preliminary model of a product that can range from a simple visual mockup to a functional working version.
4 Concept Testing Sharing a product idea with potential customers to confirm it solves a real problem they actually have.
5 Minimum Viable Product (MVP) The most basic version of a product that includes only core features and that customers are willing to pay for.
6 Value Proposition A statement that explains why a target customer should choose your product instead of a competitor’s.
7 Manufacturing Planning Deciding how and where a product will be produced, which begins after the MVP is validated through customer feedback.
8 Market Research The process of gathering and analyzing data about customers, competitors, and market conditions to guide product decisions.
9 Brand A name, term, design, symbol, or combination that identifies and differentiates a company’s products from those of competitors.
10 Branding The ongoing process of building a brand’s identity so that customers develop trust and loyalty over time.
11 Brand Equity The added financial and competitive value a product earns because customers recognize, trust, and prefer the brand.
12 Product Life Cycle (PLC) A model describing the four stages every product passes through: introduction, growth, maturity, and decline.
13 Introduction Stage The first PLC stage, marked by low sales, high costs, and a strategic focus on building customer awareness.
14 Maturity Stage The PLC stage where sales peak, growth slows, the market is saturated, and competition is heaviest.
15 Product Portfolio Management The practice of managing multiple products at different life cycle stages to balance risk and maintain steady revenue.
16 Product Messaging The stage of product development focused on crafting the value proposition and product-positioning strategy to communicate the product’s unique value to target customers
17 Generic Product A product sold without branding or a distinctive brand identity, targeting customers who prioritize cost savings over brand recognition

Additional Terms

# Term Definition
1 Growth Stage The PLC stage where sales rise rapidly, profits begin to appear, and new competitors enter the market.
2 Decline Stage The final PLC stage where sales drop and businesses must decide whether to reduce investment, discontinue, or reinvent the product.
3 Product Features The specific attributes or functions of a product that deliver value to the customer.
4 Customer Feedback Information collected from users about their experience with a product, used to guide refinements and improvements.
5 Target Market The specific group of consumers a product is designed and marketed to serve (cross-topic: 2.1).
6 Market Saturation A condition in which most potential customers already own or use a product, making further sales growth difficult.
7 Price Wars Aggressive rounds of price cutting among competitors, most common during the maturity stage of the PLC.

2.5 Price

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Price The amount of money a customer pays in exchange for a good or service.
2 Pricing Strategy A deliberate method a business uses to set the price of its products in order to meet revenue and profit goals.
3 Value-Based Pricing Setting a price based on the customer’s perceived value — what they are willing to pay — rather than on costs alone.
4 Competitive Pricing Setting a price by looking at what competitors charge for similar products and positioning accordingly.
5 Cost-Based Pricing Calculating the total cost per unit and then adding a markup to determine the selling price (cost + desired profit = price).
6 Penetration Pricing Setting a low initial price to attract customers quickly and gain market share, then gradually raising the price.
7 Dynamic Pricing Adjusting the price of a product in real time based on current demand, supply, or other market conditions.
8 Loss-Leader Pricing Selling a specific product below cost to draw customers into the store or platform, expecting they will also buy profitable items.
9 Pricing Power The ability of a business to raise its prices without losing customers to competitors.
10 Price Elasticity of Demand A measure of how sensitive customers are to a change in price — whether demand rises or falls significantly when price shifts.
11 Price Fixing An illegal agreement between competitors to set prices at a certain level, eliminating genuine competition.
12 Price Gouging Raising prices to unfair levels during a crisis or emergency, which is illegal in many states and countries.
13 Price Discrimination Charging different prices to different customer segments for the same product; illegal when based on protected characteristics.

Additional Terms

# Term Definition
1 Elastic Demand Demand that changes significantly when price changes — customers are highly price-sensitive.
2 Inelastic Demand Demand that stays relatively stable even when price changes — customers are less price-sensitive.
3 Markup The amount added to the cost of a product to determine its selling price and generate profit.
4 Switching Costs The time, money, or effort a customer must spend to change from one product or brand to another.
5 Price War A situation where competing businesses continuously lower prices to undercut each other, often hurting profits industry-wide.
6 Perceived Value The customer’s belief about what a product is worth to them, influenced by brand, quality, and experience.

2.6 Place and Channels

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Place Where and how customers access a business’s products, whether through physical locations, online platforms, or membership-based models
2 Marketing channel All the individuals and businesses involved in moving a finished product from the producer to the final customer
3 Distribution channel A synonym for marketing channel — the path a product follows from production to the end user
4 Supply chain The full network of people and businesses that produce and distribute a product, from raw materials through final delivery; the marketing channel is the final stage of this chain
5 Direct channel A marketing path that connects the business straight to its customers with no intermediaries in between
6 Indirect channel A marketing path that includes intermediaries such as wholesalers or retailers between the business and its customers
7 Intermediary A third party — such as a wholesaler, retailer, or distributor — that sits between the producer and the final customer in a channel
8 Wholesaler A business that buys products in bulk from manufacturers and resells them in smaller quantities to retailers or other businesses
9 Retailer A business that sells products directly to the final consumer, either in physical stores or online
10 B2C channel A marketing channel designed to deliver products from businesses directly to individual consumers, such as websites or retail stores
11 B2B channel A marketing channel designed to deliver products from one business to another, often using industrial distributors
12 Legally mandated channel A distribution path required by law for certain products — such as prescription medications or health-and-safety goods — that restricts how and where the product can be sold

Additional Terms

# Term Definition
1 Industrial distributor A middleman that sells products to other businesses rather than to individual consumers; the standard B2B channel
2 Company-owned store A retail location operated directly by the brand, giving the business full control over pricing, merchandising, and customer experience
3 Club membership model A retail format (e.g., Costco, Sam’s Club) where customers pay a fee to access products, often at lower prices in bulk
4 Channel partner Any external business — wholesaler, retailer, or distributor — that helps move a product to the final customer through an indirect channel
5 Channel conflict Tension that arises when two or more channel partners compete for the same customers, such as a brand’s website competing with its own retail partners

2.7 Promotion and Marketing Communications

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Marketing campaign A coordinated effort that uses multiple promotional tools to promote a product or brand to customers
2 Promotional mix The combination of tools a business uses to communicate its message to customers
3 Media advertising Using mass-media channels (TV, radio, newspapers, billboards) to deliver the same message to a large audience at the same time
4 Personal selling Direct, one-on-one interaction with a customer that provides detailed product information or a demonstration
5 Sales pitch A short verbal presentation that conveys a product’s value proposition to a potential buyer

2 Competitive rivalry The intensity of competition among existing businesses in a market, determined by the number of competitors, product differentiation, and pricing power
3 Threat of new entrants The risk that new businesses will enter the market and increase competition; determined by barriers to entry
4 Barriers to entry Obstacles that make it difficult for new businesses to enter a market, such as high capital requirements, brand loyalty, regulations, and economies of scale
5 Threat of substitute products The risk that customers will switch to alternative products that meet the same need but are not direct competitors
6 Customer (buyer) power The ability of customers to influence pricing and terms, shaped by the number of customers, acquisition costs, and switching costs
7 Supplier power The ability of resource providers to raise input costs, shaped by the number of suppliers and the cost of switching to alternatives
8 Switching costs The monetary and psychological costs a customer incurs when changing from one product or brand to another
9 SWOT analysis A framework evaluating internal (Strengths, Weaknesses) and external (Opportunities, Threats) factors that influence a business’s ability to accomplish its goals
10 Strengths Internal advantages a business controls, such as core competencies, brand recognition, skilled employees, and financial resources
11 Weaknesses Internal disadvantages a business faces, such as missing competencies, limited funds, outdated technology, and poor customer service
12 Opportunities External factors beyond a business’s control that may contribute to success, such as market growth, technology advancements, and favorable regulations
13 Threats External factors beyond a business’s control that may negatively impact the business, such as rising costs, natural disasters, and disruptive innovation
14 PESTEL A framework analyzing external macro-environmental factors: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, and Legal

Additional Terms

# Term Definition
1 Competitive environment The overall market conditions in which businesses compete, including the number and strength of competitors, customers, and suppliers
2 Product differentiation The degree to which a product is perceived as unique or distinct from competitors’ products
3 Pricing power A business’s ability to raise prices without losing customers to competitors
4 Economies of scale Cost advantages that businesses obtain due to their scale of operation, where cost per unit decreases as output increases
5 Intangible assets Non-physical assets such as brand recognition, reputation, intellectual property, and company culture
6 Intellectual property Creations of the mind — inventions, designs, brand names, and trade secrets — legally protected from unauthorized use
7 Disruptive innovation An innovation that fundamentally changes the way customers meet their needs, often displacing established businesses
8 Input costs The prices paid for raw materials, component parts, and resources needed to produce goods or services
9 Resource market The market in which businesses purchase the inputs they need to produce their products (e.g., grain market, labor market)

Trap Pairs

# Term A Term B Key Difference
1 Strengths Opportunities Strengths = internal factors the business CONTROLS (e.g., skilled employees). Opportunities = external factors the business CANNOT control (e.g., market growth). The key test: can the business directly change it?
2 Weaknesses Threats Weaknesses = internal disadvantages (e.g., limited budget). Threats = external risks (e.g., new competitor enters). Weaknesses can be fixed by the business; threats must be responded to.
3 Competitive rivalry Threat of new entrants Rivalry = competition from EXISTING businesses. New entrants = competition from businesses that DON’T EXIST YET but could enter the market.
4 Customer power Supplier power Customer power = buyers’ ability to push prices DOWN. Supplier power = sellers’ ability to push input costs UP. Both squeeze the business’s margins from different sides.
5 Substitute products Direct competitors Substitutes = different products that meet the same need (e.g., food truck vs. restaurant). Direct competitors = same type of product (e.g., restaurant vs. restaurant).
6 Porter’s Five Forces SWOT analysis Five Forces = evaluates ONLY the external competitive environment (industry attractiveness). SWOT = evaluates BOTH internal (S, W) and external (O, T) factors. Five Forces is an input to the “T” in SWOT.
7 Barriers to entry Switching costs Barriers to entry = obstacles for NEW BUSINESSES entering the market. Switching costs = costs for CUSTOMERS changing brands. Both protect existing businesses but from different threats.

Unit 5: Personal Finance

5.1 Taxes, Net Income, and Budgeting

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Income tax A percentage of income paid to federal, state, and/or local governments; for employees, a portion is withheld from each paycheck by the employer
2 Capital gains tax A tax paid on the profit earned from selling an asset (e.g., stocks, property) at a higher price than the purchase price; typically taxed at a lower rate than income tax
3 Payroll tax Taxes withheld from employee paychecks that fund specific government insurance programs, including Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, and unemployment benefits
4 Property tax A tax based on the value of an individual’s property (houses, land, cars), paid to local or state government
5 Sales tax A tax based on the sales price of an item, collected by the business at the point of sale and submitted to the government
6 Progressive tax A tax system in which individuals earning higher incomes pay higher tax rates; the U.S. federal income tax is progressive
7 Tax deduction An expense that reduces an individual’s taxable income, thereby reducing the taxes owed (e.g., mortgage interest, retirement contributions, charitable donations)
8 Tax credit A direct reduction in the amount of taxes owed (not taxable income); more valuable than a deduction of the same amount
9 Gross income The total amount of income earned during a pay period before any deductions
10 Net income (net pay) The amount of money an individual receives after all deductions (taxes, benefits, retirement) are subtracted from gross income
11 Pay stub A document provided by an employer showing gross income, mandatory and voluntary deductions, and net income for a pay period
12 Mandatory deduction Amounts required by law to be withheld from a paycheck, including federal/state income taxes and payroll taxes
13 Voluntary deduction Amounts an employee chooses to have withheld for benefits such as health insurance, retirement savings, and life insurance

Additional Terms

# Term Definition
1 Taxable income Gross income minus tax deductions; the amount of income on which taxes are actually calculated
2 Tax refund Money returned to a taxpayer when the amount withheld from paychecks exceeds the actual tax owed for the year
3 Withholding The practice of an employer deducting estimated taxes from each paycheck and sending them directly to the government
4 Pretax deduction A deduction taken from gross income before taxes are calculated, reducing taxable income (e.g., 401(k) contributions, HSA contributions)
5 Social Security A federal program providing retirement, disability, and survivor benefits, funded by payroll taxes shared between employer and employee
6 Medicare A federal health insurance program for people 65+ and certain younger individuals with disabilities, funded by payroll taxes
7 Self-employment tax The full payroll tax (both employer and employee portions) that self-employed individuals and contractors must pay themselves
8 Filing status A classification (single, married filing jointly, head of household, etc.) that determines tax brackets and standard deduction amounts
9 Budget A plan for how an individual or household will allocate income toward expenses, savings, and debt repayment over a specific period

Trap Pairs

# Term A Term B Key Difference
1 Tax deduction Tax credit Deduction reduces TAXABLE INCOME (you save your tax rate × the deduction). Credit reduces TAXES OWED directly (a $1,000 credit saves you exactly $1,000). Credits are more valuable.
2 Gross income Net income Gross = total earnings BEFORE deductions. Net = what you actually TAKE HOME after all deductions. Net can be significantly lower than gross.
3 Mandatory deduction Voluntary deduction Mandatory = required by law (income tax, payroll tax). Voluntary = chosen by employee (health insurance, 401(k), union dues). You can change voluntary deductions; you cannot opt out of mandatory ones.
4 Income tax Payroll tax Income tax = general tax on earnings funding government operations. Payroll tax = specific tax funding Social Security and Medicare. Both are withheld, but they fund different things.
5 Progressive tax Flat tax Progressive = higher income → higher tax rate (U.S. federal system). Flat = same rate for all income levels. The U.S. uses progressive; some states use flat tax.
6 Pretax deduction Post-tax deduction Pretax = deducted BEFORE taxes calculated → reduces taxable income (e.g., 401(k)). Post-tax = deducted AFTER taxes calculated → does not reduce taxable income (e.g., Roth 401(k)).

5.2 Managing Personal Risk

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Insurable risk A risk that involves a potential loss due to chance, is quantifiable, and is statistically predictable, allowing an insurer to estimate cost and likelihood
2 Personal risk An insurable risk involving the health and well-being of the insured individual, such as injury from an accident or suffering from illness
3 Property risk An insurable risk involving loss or damage to the insured individual’s property, such as their home or car
4 Liability risk An insurable risk involving damage caused to another person’s property or health, such as damage from reckless driving
5 Health insurance Insurance that reimburses policyholders for medically necessary healthcare and sometimes preventive care; may be provided as an employee benefit
6 Auto insurance Insurance covering financial losses from car accidents, damage to the insured’s vehicle, and liability for damage caused to others
7 Homeowner’s insurance Insurance covering financial losses to a home and its contents, as well as liability for injuries that occur on the property
8 Renter’s insurance Insurance covering a renter’s personal property and liability, but not the building itself (which is the landlord’s responsibility)
9 Life insurance Insurance that provides funds to designated beneficiaries in the event of the insured person’s death, intended to replace lost income or fund dependents’ needs
10 Premium The amount an insurance buyer pays (annual, semi-annual, or monthly) for an insurance policy
11 Claim A formal request filed by the policyholder to the insurance company for reimbursement after a covered financial loss
12 Risk tolerance An individual’s willingness and ability to accept uncertainty or potential financial loss; affects insurance and investment decisions
13 Insurance fraud A crime involving misrepresentation or falsified claims by policyholders, or misrepresentation of policies/benefits by insurance sellers
14 Predatory lending Deceptive or aggressive lending practices that exploit borrowers, often through high fees, hidden terms, or pressure tactics
15 Personal financial risk The risk of financial loss due to events such as car accidents, illness, property damage, or liability — individuals face both financial and physical risks in daily life
16 Legal insurance requirement Insurance coverage mandated by law, such as auto liability insurance required by most U.S. states and property insurance required by mortgage lenders
17 Dependent coverage Additional insurance needs that arise when an individual has dependents (spouse, children), including family health insurance, multiple vehicle policies, and higher life insurance amounts
18 Risk reduction Actions taken to lower insurance premiums by reducing risky behavior — e.g., maintaining a good driving record (auto insurance) or not smoking (life insurance)

Additional Terms

# Term Definition
1 Deductible The amount the policyholder must pay out of pocket before insurance begins covering costs; higher deductibles typically mean lower premiums
2 Coverage The total amount of protection provided by an insurance policy; the maximum the insurer will pay for a covered loss
3 Beneficiary The person designated to receive funds from a life insurance policy upon the insured person’s death
4 Extended warranty A service contract on an expensive purchase (car, appliance) that extends the manufacturer’s warranty and covers repairs beyond the original terms
5 Identity theft The fraudulent acquisition and use of someone’s personal information (SSN, credit card numbers) for financial gain
6 Phishing A fraudulent attempt to obtain personal or financial information by disguising as a trustworthy entity in electronic communications
7 Credit freeze A tool that restricts access to an individual’s credit report, preventing identity thieves from opening new accounts in the person’s name
8 Nonprofit credit counselor A professional from a nonprofit organization who provides free or low-cost advice on managing debt, budgeting, and avoiding predatory loans

Trap Pairs

# Term A Term B Key Difference
1 Premium Deductible Premium = what you PAY regularly to have insurance. Deductible = what you PAY out of pocket when you FILE a claim. Higher deductible → lower premium (and vice versa).
2 Personal risk Property risk Personal risk = harm to YOUR BODY (injury, illness). Property risk = harm to YOUR THINGS (home, car). Both are insurable, but different policies cover each.
3 Liability risk Property risk Liability = damage YOU cause to OTHERS or THEIR property. Property = damage to YOUR OWN property. Auto insurance covers both: collision (your car) + liability (the other car).
4 Tax credit Insurance claim Tax credit = reduces taxes you owe to the government. Insurance claim = request for reimbursement from your insurance company after a loss. Both give you money back, but from different sources.
5 Predatory lending Insurance fraud Predatory lending = the LENDER exploits the borrower. Insurance fraud = the POLICYHOLDER or INSURER commits a crime. Both are illegal and harmful to consumers.
6 Homeowner’s insurance Renter’s insurance Homeowner’s = covers the BUILDING + contents + liability. Renter’s = covers CONTENTS + liability only (not the building). The landlord’s policy covers the building for renters.

5.3 Saving and Investing for Education, Housing, and Retirement

Core Terms

# Term Definition
1 Financial asset A resource with monetary value that an individual owns, including savings accounts, CDs, stocks, bonds, and mutual funds
2 Savings account A deposit account at a bank or credit union that earns interest; federally insured (FDIC/NCUA), low risk, low return
3 Certificate of deposit (CD) A savings instrument with a fixed term and fixed interest rate; penalties for early withdrawal; federally insured
4 Stock A share of ownership in a company; value depends on the company’s performance; higher risk, higher expected return
5 Bond A loan made by an investor to a borrower (government or corporation); pays fixed interest; generally lower risk than stocks
6 Mutual fund An investment that pools money from many investors to buy a diversified portfolio of stocks and/or bonds
7 Compounding The process by which investment returns generate their own returns over time; starting early leads to significantly greater wealth
8 Risk-return tradeoff The principle that financial assets with higher potential returns also carry higher potential risk of loss
9 Inflation The rate at which the general level of prices rises over time, decreasing the purchasing power of money
10 Diversification A strategy of allocating funds across a variety of financial assets with different risk levels to seek higher returns without excessive risk
11 Time horizon The length of time an investor expects to hold an investment before needing the money; affects asset allocation choices
12 Social Security A U.S. federal program providing retirement income funded by payroll taxes; one source of retirement funding
13 Mortgage A loan used to purchase real estate, where the property serves as collateral; repayment terms include principal, interest, and duration
14 Student loan A loan used to finance postsecondary education; federal loans typically have lower interest rates and more favorable terms than private loans

Additional Terms

# Term Definition
1 Nominal return The rate of return on an investment before adjusting for inflation
2 Real return The rate of return on an investment after adjusting for inflation; reflects actual purchasing power gained
3 Capital gain The profit earned when an investment is sold for more than its purchase price
4 Dividend A distribution of a portion of a company’s earnings to its shareholders
5 Interest The cost of borrowing money (paid by borrower) or the return earned on savings/investments (paid to saver)
6 Transaction fee A cost charged each time an investment is bought or sold
7 Management fee An ongoing fee charged by a mutual fund or financial advisor for managing investments
8 Discount brokerage A firm that charges lower fees for buying/selling investments but provides less personal advice than a full-service firm
9 Behavioral bias Psychological tendencies (overconfidence, loss aversion) that cause investors to make irrational decisions
10 Overconfidence bias A behavioral bias causing investors to overestimate their ability to predict market movements, leading to excessive risk-taking
11 Loss aversion bias A behavioral bias causing investors to feel losses more acutely than gains, leading to premature selling at a loss
12 Down payment An upfront cash payment representing a percentage of a home’s purchase price; the rest is financed with a mortgage
13 Fixed-rate mortgage A mortgage with an interest rate that stays the same for the entire loan term
14 Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) A mortgage with an interest rate that can change over time based on market conditions
15 Scholarship Financial aid awarded based on merit (academic, athletic, artistic) that does not need to be repaid
16 Grant Financial aid awarded based on financial need that does not need to be repaid
17 Work-study program A federal financial aid program providing part-time employment to students to help pay for education expenses
18 401(k) An employer-sponsored retirement savings plan allowing employees to contribute pretax dollars; employers may match contributions
19 IRA (Individual Retirement Account) A personal retirement savings account with tax advantages; traditional IRA (tax-deferred) or Roth IRA (tax-free growth)
20 Charitable giving Donations to nonprofit organizations; may provide tax deductions and align with personal values and mission
21 Long-term financial goals Major financial objectives that individuals or households plan for over years or decades, including paying for postsecondary education, buying a home, and saving for retirement
22 Family financial planning The process of discussing and sharing long-term financial goals between partners or family members to reduce potential financial conflict and align priorities
23 Automated savings technology Financial technology tools — such as automated savings transfers, payroll deductions for retirement accounts, and round-up savings apps — that help individuals overcome barriers to saving
24 Financial advisor A professional who helps individuals evaluate saving and investment options; selection criteria include licensing, certifications, education, experience, and cost
25 Investment benchmarking The practice of comparing the performance of financial assets against a standard index (e.g., S&P 500 for stocks, Bloomberg Aggregate Bond Index for bonds) to assess returns

Trap Pairs

# Term A Term B Key Difference
1 Stock Bond Stock = OWNERSHIP in a company (higher risk, higher return). Bond = a LOAN to a company/government (lower risk, lower return). Stockholders own; bondholders lend.
2 Nominal return Real return Nominal = return BEFORE inflation adjustment. Real = return AFTER inflation adjustment. A 7% nominal return with 3% inflation = 4% real return.
3 Tax deduction Tax credit Deduction reduces TAXABLE INCOME. Credit reduces TAXES OWED directly. A $1,000 deduction at 22% tax rate saves $220; a $1,000 credit saves $1,000.
4 Scholarship Grant Scholarship = based on MERIT (academic, athletic, artistic achievement). Grant = based on NEED (financial situation). Neither needs to be repaid.
5 Fixed-rate mortgage Adjustable-rate mortgage Fixed = interest rate NEVER changes → predictable payments. Adjustable = interest rate CAN change → payments may increase or decrease.
6 401(k) IRA 401(k) = employer-sponsored (higher contribution limits, possible employer match). IRA = individual account (more investment choices, lower limits). Both offer tax advantages for retirement.
7 Overconfidence bias Loss aversion bias Overconfidence = thinking you’re BETTER at investing than you are → takes too much risk. Loss aversion = feeling losses TOO ACUTELY → sells too early to avoid further loss. Both lead to poor returns.
8 Transaction fee Management fee Transaction fee = one-time cost PER TRADE. Management fee = ongoing annual cost for fund management (often as % of assets). Both reduce your return.
9 Savings account CD Savings account = withdraw ANYTIME, lower interest. CD = locked for a FIXED TERM, higher interest, penalty for early withdrawal. Both are federally insured.
10 Capital gain Dividend Capital gain = profit from SELLING an asset for more than you paid. Dividend = regular CASH PAYMENT from company earnings to shareholders. Both are forms of investment return but earned differently.
