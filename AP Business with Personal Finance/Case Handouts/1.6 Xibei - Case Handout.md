## Background

Xibei Youmian Village (西贝莜面村) is a Chinese casual-dining chain founded in 1988 by Jia Guolong (贾国龙) in Inner Mongolia. It serves northwestern Chinese cuisine, built around oat noodle (莜面) dishes. By 2025 it operated roughly **370 direct-operated stores** with about **18,000 employees**, and reported revenue of **¥6.2 billion** in 2023. Xibei positions itself as a premium family restaurant — its brand promise centers on **safe ingredients and kid-friendliness** (食材安全、亲子友好). That positioning let it charge above-average prices: customers understood the premium as a **trust premium** — paying more because they believed the food was freshly and carefully made.

Like most large Chinese restaurant chains, Xibei relies on a **central kitchen model**: dishes are pre-processed — cut, marinated, partially cooked — at large central kitchens, delivered cold-chain to stores, and finished in-store (heated, assembled, seasoned, plated). The model is legal, industry-standard, and has real advantages: consistent taste, food-safety control, lower cost. Xibei's stores feature "open kitchens" (明厨亮灶) that show the final preparation step. What customers did not clearly see was how much of each dish had already been made before it reached the store.

The dispute turned on a **definition**. In 2024, six government departments issued a document defining 预制菜 (pre-made dishes) in a way that **excludes a restaurant chain's own central-kitchen preparation**. Jia Guolong therefore insisted Xibei used "no pre-made dishes at all." Critics, including internet personality Luo Yonghao (罗永浩), used the broader everyday meaning: if it wasn't cooked from scratch in the store, it's 预制菜. A national standard for pre-made dishes was still pending as of early 2026.

On **September 10, 2025**, Luo Yonghao posted on Weibo that Xibei's dishes were "almost all pre-made" while prices were high, and called for legislation requiring restaurants to disclose pre-made dish use. Jia Guolong responded within a day — denying the claim, invoking the official definition, and announcing a lawsuit for defamation. The exchange triggered a collapse in customer traffic: daily revenue fell by roughly **¥1 million per day** in the first days, business declined **40–60%** over the following four months, and in **January 2026** Xibei announced it would close **102 stores — about 30% of its locations** — affecting roughly **4,000 employees**. On September 14, 2025, Jia publicly admitted "my way of responding was wrong," pledged to build "a completely transparent Xibei," and said the company would "learn thoroughly from Pangdonglai (胖东来)."

## Questions

**1. Respond to parts A, B, and C.**

Some customers felt deceived: they paid restaurant prices believing dishes were freshly made, while much of the preparation happened in a central kitchen. Xibei pointed out that its model is legal, common in the industry, and passes food-safety checks.

(A) Identify the **core value** and the **business practice** that conflict in this situation.

(B) Explain why both sides of this conflict have legitimate claims — that is, why this is an **ethical dilemma** rather than a case of clearly unethical behavior.

(C) Recommend one specific disclosure practice Xibei could adopt that respects the customer's core value without abandoning the central-kitchen model.

---

**2. Respond to parts A, B, and C.**

Using a central kitchen is a legal business practice. Some consumers nonetheless argued that Xibei's marketing presented centrally processed dishes as "freshly made in store."

(A) Identify the form of unethical behavior that **deliberately hiding** central-kitchen processing from customers would represent.

(B) Explain the difference between *using* a central kitchen (a business practice) and *concealing* that you use one (unethical behavior), using the sorting rule "if one side is clearly wrong, it's unethical behavior; if both sides have legitimate claims, it's a dilemma."

(C) Explain how Xibei's initial response — denial and a defamation lawsuit — affected customer relationships, employee relationships, and public perception, and how those effects reached the **revenue line**.

---

**3. Respond to parts A, B, and C.**

Xibei's brand promise centers on safe ingredients and family-friendliness. After the controversy, Jia Guolong pledged to build "a completely transparent Xibei" and to "learn thoroughly from Pangdonglai."

(A) Identify what Xibei's brand positioning promised customers, and how that promise supported its premium pricing.

(B) Explain how relying on central-kitchen processing **without clear disclosure** conflicts with this promise.

(C) Recommend one specific action consistent with the "transparent Xibei" pledge that could rebuild the trust premium. Justify using **Lens 2** (consistency with vision and goals).

---

**4. Respond to parts A, B, and C.**

Jia Guolong repeatedly argued that under the six-department definition, Xibei's central-kitchen model is **not** 预制菜 — so Xibei operated legally. The public debate continued anyway.

(A) Identify the type of PESTEL factor that this regulatory definition represents.

(B) Explain why "operating legally" did not settle the public debate — that is, why reputational consequences arose **whether or not any law was broken**.

(C) Predict what would happen to restaurant chains like Xibei if a national standard required clear on-menu labeling of central-kitchen processing. Consider both chains and customers.

---

**5. Respond to parts A, B, and C.**

By January 2026, Xibei had closed 102 stores, affecting roughly 4,000 employees. During the controversy, frontline staff reported being insulted by customers; the company paid subsidies and later raised wages. Luo Yonghao argued that "no company fails just because it was criticized — failure comes from its own decisions."

(A) Classify the following as **internal** or **external** stakeholders: Xibei's employees; Xibei's customers; the government departments that issued the 预制菜 definition; Xibei's vegetable suppliers.

(B) Using **Lens 1** (stakeholder arithmetic), evaluate Xibei's September 14 response: who benefited and who was harmed by admitting error, apologizing, and switching nine dishes to in-store cooking?

(C) Evaluate Luo Yonghao's claim using evidence from this case. Was it the criticism that closed the 102 stores — or the response to it?
