# 2.4 Dyson — Case Handout

**Topic:** 2.4 Product

---

## Background

Dyson Ltd. is a British technology company founded by James Dyson in 1993 (as Dyson Appliances Limited). The company is known for its premium vacuum cleaners, hand dryers, hair care products, and air treatment devices. As of 2024, Dyson generates over $7 billion in annual revenue and sells products in more than 80 countries. James Dyson himself ranks 60th on the Bloomberg Billionaires Index with a net worth of $26.2 billion.

James Dyson was born in 1947 in Cromer, Norfolk, England. He studied at the Byam Shaw School of Art and then the Royal College of Art (RCA), where his fascination with engineering was ignited. He collaborated with entrepreneur Jeremy Fry on projects like the Dyson Sea Truck, an amphibious landing craft, which honed his skills as a product designer. Before pursuing the vacuum cleaner, he invented the **Ballbarrow** — a wheelbarrow with a molded plastic trough and a ball instead of a wheel — demonstrating his pattern of identifying everyday problems and engineering better solutions. Despite initial UK success, the Ballbarrow design was replicated by US competitors, teaching Dyson an early lesson about protecting innovation.

## Key Events

| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
| ~1975 | Invents the **Ballbarrow** (ball-wheelbarrow) while renovating his farmhouse; achieves UK success but loses to US copycats — learns the importance of protecting innovation |
| 1978 | Disassembles his Hoover vacuum cleaner; identifies root cause: **clogged pores and reduced suction** from accumulated dust in the bag |
| 1978 | Observes a sawmill's **30-foot conical centrifuge** (dust removal system); recognizes the potential to miniaturize cyclone technology for a vacuum |
| 1979 | Begins building prototypes of a bagless vacuum in a barn behind his house |
| 1979–1984 | Builds **5,127 prototypes** over five years, enduring financial strain and skepticism from others; wife supports the family by teaching art |
| 1984 | Perfects the **Dual Cyclone** technology (DC1 prototype) but **every domestic manufacturer refuses to license it** — they profit from selling replacement bags |
| ~1985 | Settles patent disputes with Amway over Dual Cyclone technology; learns to defend intellectual property |
| 1986 | Secures a licensing deal with **Apex**, a Japanese manufacturer; launches the **G-Force** vacuum in Japan at ~$2,000 |
| 1986–1993 | G-Force wins design awards in Japan and Europe; validates that customers will pay a premium for bagless technology |
| 1993 | Founds **Dyson Appliances Limited** in the UK, using his own savings and collateral; launches the **DC01** with the campaign **"Say goodbye to the bag"** |
| 1995 | DC01 becomes the **#1 selling vacuum** in the UK, outselling established brands despite costing twice as much |
| 1996–2001 | Licenses technology to **Phantom Technologies** in North America; later takes direct control of US operations |
| ~2000 | Appoints **Martin McCord** as CEO so Dyson can refocus on design and engineering; McCord secures deals with **Best Buy, Target, Home Depot** |
| ~2000 | Launches the **Contra Rotator** washing machine (£1,000) — two drums rotating in opposite directions; positive reviews but commercial failure; discontinued after a few years |
| 2002 | Relocates manufacturing from Malmesbury, UK to **Malaysia** — frees up resources for R&D despite controversy over UK job losses |
| 2006 | Launches the **Airblade** hand dryer — enters a completely new product category |
| 2016 | Launches the **Supersonic** hair dryer at $400 — disrupts a stagnant market through engineering innovation |
| 2018 | Launches the **Airwrap** multi-styler — becomes a viral social media phenomenon |
| 2019 | Attempts an **electric car project** — invests $2.5 billion but ultimately cancels; demonstrates willingness to take bold risks |
| 2020 | Collaborates with **TTP** to develop ventilators during COVID-19 pandemic — showcases agility and social responsibility |
| 2023 | Launches the **Airstrait** wet-to-dry straightener; continues expanding the hair care portfolio |
| Ongoing | Dyson invests **15–20% of annual revenue** in R&D, with research labs in the UK, Singapore, and the US; the company holds approximately **6,000 granted and pending patents** |

## Questions

**1. Prototype vs. MVP**
Dyson built 5,127 prototypes before launching the G-Force in Japan. Why was the G-Force considered an MVP and not just another prototype? What specific characteristic makes an MVP different from a prototype?

**2. Idea Screening**
When Dyson approached vacuum manufacturers with his working prototype, every major brand refused to license it. Why? What does this tell us about the importance of screening for **business model conflicts**, not just technical feasibility?

**3. Refinement**
After the G-Force launched in Japan, Dyson spent seven years refining the product before launching the DC01 in the UK. What specific changes might he have made based on customer feedback? Why is this refinement stage critical?

**4. Brand Equity**
Dyson vacuums sell for 2–3x the price of competitors. Some customers say, "I know I'm paying for the brand." Is this a criticism or a compliment? What does it reveal about brand equity?

**5. Portfolio Management**
Dyson's vacuum line is in the maturity stage while its hair care products are in growth. How does managing products at different life cycle stages benefit the company? What would happen if all Dyson products were in the same stage?

**6. Life Cycle Strategy**
The Dyson Airwrap launched in 2018 and became a viral social media phenomenon. Meanwhile, Dyson's vacuum line has been on the market since 1993 and faces established competitors. Using the facts from the case, what PLC stage is each product likely in today? Given that Dyson invests 15–20% of annual revenue in R&D, what strategy should the company pursue for each product line?
