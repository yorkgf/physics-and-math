# Bombas — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.1 What Is a Business** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

---

## 1. Company Introduction

**Bombas** is an American apparel company that sells premium socks and underwear with a **buy-one-give-one** mission: for every item purchased, Bombas donates one to someone in need, primarily through homeless shelters.

- **Founded:** 2013 by **David Heath** and **Randy Goldberg** in New York City
- **Product:** Premium socks, underwear, and T-shirts — engineered for comfort (seamless toe, arch support, blister tab)
- **Mission:** Buy one, give one — every purchase donates an item to a homeless shelter
- **Industry:** Apparel / E-commerce

The founders were inspired by a statistic: **socks are the most requested clothing item in homeless shelters.** They built a company where every product sold generates a direct donation. Bombas appeared on **Shark Tank** in 2014 and secured a deal with Daymond John. As of 2024, Bombas has donated over **100 million items.**

---

## 2. Key Events

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **2013** | David Heath and Randy Goldberg found Bombas after learning socks are the #1 requested item in homeless shelters |
| **2013** | Launches Kickstarter campaign — raises $140,000 in 30 days; validates the buy-one-give-one model with real customers |
| **2014** | Appears on **Shark Tank Season 6**; secures a deal with **Daymond John** ($200,000 for 17.5% equity) |
| **2014–2018** | Rapid growth through online sales; expands product line from socks to underwear and T-shirts |
| **2020** | Reaches **50 million items donated**; expands retail partnerships (Nordstrom, Dick's Sporting Goods) |
| **2023** | Surpasses **100 million items donated**; becomes one of the most recognized mission-driven apparel brands in the U.S. |

---

## Questions

**Q1.** Bombas solves two problems at once: consumers want better socks, and shelters need socks. Using the framework from Topic 1.1, explain how Bombas **creates value** for its customers and how it **captures value** for itself. What is the relationship between the buy-one-give-one mission and the premium price?

**Q2.** Bombas sells socks (a good) and also offers customer support and easy returns (a service). Does Bombas sell **goods, services, or both**? Explain using the definitions from class.

**Q3.** The founders tested their idea with a Kickstarter campaign before manufacturing at scale. How does this relate to the concept of **testing a hypothesis** about a business idea ? What was the hypothesis, and what did the $140,000 in Kickstarter funding tell them?

**Q4.** For every pair of socks Bombas sells, one pair is donated to a homeless shelter. In this transaction, who is the **customer** and who is the **consumer** of the donated pair? Explain the difference between the two terms using this example.

**Q5.** Compare Bombas's path to **VANCL's (凡客)** path (see the VANCL handout). Both are apparel companies. What is **one key difference** in how each company created and captured value? Why did one grow sustainably while the other collapsed?

# VANCL (凡客诚品) — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.1 What Is a Business** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

---

## 1. Company Introduction

**VANCL (凡客诚品)** was a Chinese e-commerce company that sold affordable apparel and lifestyle products directly to consumers online. At its peak, VANCL was one of China's most recognized internet brands — a symbol of "people's fast fashion" (人民快时尚).

- **Founded:** 2007 by **Chen Nian (陈年)** in Beijing, China
- **Product:** Affordable T-shirts, casual clothing, shoes, and eventually a wide range of lifestyle products — all sold online at low prices
- **Founder background:** Chen Nian was a co-founder of Joyo.com (卓越网), one of China's early e-commerce platforms, which was later acquired by Amazon. He launched VANCL with the vision of building a direct-to-consumer apparel brand for young, price-sensitive Chinese consumers.
- **Industry:** E-commerce / Fast fashion / Apparel

VANCL grew explosively between 2008 and 2011, fueled by aggressive online advertising and a simple value proposition: decent-quality clothing at the lowest possible price, delivered to your door. At its peak, VANCL sold **tens of millions of items per year** and was valued at over $3 billion.

---

## 2. Key Events

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **2007** | Chen Nian founds VANCL in Beijing; launches with a small selection of men's shirts and casual wear sold exclusively online |
| **2008–2010** | Explosive growth: aggressive internet advertising (banner ads, search engine marketing) drives massive traffic; revenue grows rapidly; VANPL becomes one of China's most recognized e-commerce brands |
| **2010** | Peak valuation exceeds **$3 billion**; VANCL is seen as a challenger to established retail brands |
| **2011** | Company begins **expanding product lines** far beyond apparel — into electronics, home goods, cosmetics, and even kitchenware; inventory management becomes chaotic |
| **2011–2012** | **Over-expansion** leads to massive unsold inventory; product quality declines as the brand tries to cover too many categories; the brand identity becomes diluted — customers no longer know what VANCL stands for |
| **2013–2014** | Revenue collapses; the company lays off thousands of employees; attempts to refocus on core apparel products |
| **2014–present** | VANCL shrinks dramatically; brand fades from public consciousness; the company survives at a fraction of its former scale |

---

## Questions

**Q1.** VANCL created value by offering affordable clothing to price-sensitive consumers. Explain how VANCL **created value** for its customers and how it **captured value** for itself. What was the relationship between low price and high volume in VANCL's business model?

**Q2.** VANCL sold apparel (goods) through its online store. Does VANCL also sell **services**? Think about what else customers receive when they buy from an online retailer — customer support, delivery, easy returns. Explain using the definitions from class.

**Q3.** VANCL expanded from T-shirts into dozens of unrelated product categories (electronics, home goods, cosmetics). Explain why this over-expansion **eroded the company's value creation and capture.** What happened to product quality, inventory management, and brand identity?

**Q4.** Compare VANCL's target customer strategy with **Bombas's** target customer strategy (see the Bombas handout). VANCL targeted **price-sensitive mass-market buyers**; Bombas targeted **premium, mission-minded buyers.** How did each choice of target customer affect the company's ability to create and capture value?

**Q5.** VANCL's original value proposition was "人民快时尚" — affordable, decent-quality fashion for the masses. Using the concept of **competitive advantage** from Topic 1.2, explain what VANCL's advantage was in 2008 and why that advantage disappeared by 2013. What would VANCL have needed to do to maintain its advantage?

# ofo (小黄车) — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.1 What Is a Business** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

---

## 1. Company Introduction

**ofo** was a Chinese bike-sharing company — one of the first and largest dockless共享单车 (shared bicycle) platforms in the world. Users could unlock and ride yellow bicycles anywhere using a smartphone app, then leave them at their destination for the next rider.

- **Founded:** 2014 by **Dai Wei (戴威)** at Peking University, Beijing
- **Product:** Dockless shared bicycle service — users unlock bikes via a mobile app, ride, and park anywhere
- **Founder background:** Dai Wei was a Peking University student who started by running a campus bike-sharing program for students. He expanded the concept to cities across China and eventually internationally.
- **Industry:** Shared mobility / Transportation / Gig economy

At its peak in 2017, ofo operated over **10 million bicycles** in more than 200 cities across 20 countries. The company raised over **$2 billion** in venture capital and was valued at approximately **$3 billion.** It was one of the most visible symbols of China's startup boom.

---

## 2. Key Events

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **2014** | Dai Wei founds ofo at Peking University as a **campus bike-sharing** project — students share bikes within the university |
| **2015** | Receives angel funding; expands to other university campuses across Beijing |
| **2016** | Raises significant VC funding; expands to **cities** across China; dockless model (no fixed stations) attracts millions of users |
| **2016–2017** | Intense competition with **Mobike (摩拜)** — both companies engage in a subsidy war, offering rides for as little as 1 yuan or even free |
| **2017** | Peak: over **10 million bicycles** deployed in 200+ cities in 20 countries; raises $2 billion+ in total funding; valued at ~$3 billion |
| **2018** | Cash flow crisis: too many bicycles, too few profitable rides; maintenance costs explode; subsidy war burns through cash; users report millions of broken or abandoned bikes |
| **2018–2019** | Reports of ofo unable to pay suppliers; **user deposit refund crisis** — millions of users queue online to withdraw their deposits; Dai Wei reportedly borrows money personally to keep the company alive |
| **2019–2020** | Company effectively ceases major operations; most remaining bikes are abandoned or scrapped; legal disputes with suppliers and users continue |
| **2020–present** | ofo survives at a tiny fraction of its former scale; Dai Wei is reportedly restricted from high-speed rail and flights due to unpaid debts (被列入失信被执行人名单) |

---

## Questions

**Q1.** ofo started as a campus bike-sharing project and expanded to cities across China. Using the concept of **hypothesis testing** from Topic 1.4, explain why the business model that worked on a university campus might **not** automatically work in a city. What changed when ofo scaled up?

**Q2.** ofo offered rides at subsidized prices (sometimes free) to attract users and beat competitors. Apply the **risk-return framework** from Topic 1.4 to evaluate this strategy. What risk did ofo accept? What return was it pursuing? Why did the strategy ultimately fail?

**Q3.** ofo created value for users (cheap, convenient rides) but **could not capture enough value** to cover its costs. Explain the **value creation vs. value capture** distinction using ofo as an example. Why is creating value for customers not enough to build a sustainable business?

**Q4.** ofo raised over $2 billion in venture capital. Identify the **PESTEL factors** (from Topic 1.3) that enabled ofo's rapid growth and the factors that contributed to its collapse. Which factors were within ofo's control, and which were not?

**Q5.** Compare ofo's path to **VANCL's (凡客)** path (see the VANCL handout). Both companies grew explosively and then collapsed. What is **one similarity** in the reasons for their collapse? What lesson does this teach about the relationship between **growth and sustainability?**

# Coca-Cola vs. Chinese Soda Brands — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.2 Markets and Competitive Advantage** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

---

## 1. Case Introduction

This case compares **Coca-Cola** — the world's largest beverage company — with **Chinese domestic soda brands** (such as 天府可乐 Tianfu Cola, 崂山可乐 Laoshan Cola, 北冰洋 Beipingyang, and 冰峰 Bingfeng) to explain how **cost structures, pricing strategies, and competitive advantage** determine which companies survive in a competitive market.

**Key observation:** Coca-Cola sells a bottle of cola for about **3 yuan (~$0.40)** in China and still makes a profit. Chinese domestic soda brands sell their cola for **7 yuan (~$1.00)** or more — and many still lose money. Why?

---

## 2. Key Context

| Factor | Coca-Cola | Chinese Soda Brands |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale** | Global operations in 200+ countries; billions of bottles/year | Regional operations; millions of bottles/year |
| **Supply chain** | Massive, optimized global supply chain; negotiates the lowest prices for sugar, packaging, and logistics | Smaller supply chains; higher per-unit costs for ingredients and packaging |
| **Distribution** | Owns or controls bottling plants and distribution networks worldwide | Relies on regional distributors; limited national reach |
| **Brand recognition** | Among the most recognized brands on Earth | Strong regional loyalty (e.g., 北冰洋 in Beijing, 冰峰 in Xi'an) but limited national awareness |
| **Price per bottle (China)** | ~3 yuan | ~7 yuan or more |
| **Profitability** | Profitable at 3 yuan | Many brands struggle to break even at 7 yuan |

> **Market structure:** The Chinese cola market is an oligopoly dominated by two global giants: Coca-Cola and Pepsi. Domestic brands survive through regional loyalty built on nostalgia and local taste preferences — for example, 北冰洋 is preferred in Beijing and 冰峰 in Xi'an.

---

## Questions

**Q1.** Coca-Cola sells cola for 3 yuan in China and profits. Chinese soda brands sell for 7 yuan and struggle. Using the concept of **cost advantage** from Topic 1.2, explain **why** Coca-Cola's costs are lower per bottle. Identify at least **two specific factors** that contribute to Coca-Cola's cost advantage.

**Q2.** Chinese soda brands like 北冰洋 and 冰峰 have strong **regional loyalty** — consumers in Beijing and Xi'an prefer them over Coca-Cola. Is regional loyalty a form of **competitive advantage**? If so, what type (cost advantage or differentiation)? Explain why this advantage works locally but not nationally.

**Q3.** The Chinese cola market is dominated by a few large firms (Coca-Cola, Pepsi). What **market structure** does this describe? How does this market structure affect the pricing power of smaller domestic brands?

**Q4.** Some Chinese soda brands have tried to compete by emphasizing **nostalgia, local identity, and unique flavors** rather than price. Using the concept of **differentiation** from Topic 1.2, explain how this strategy works. What customer segment is it targeting, and why might these customers be willing to pay more?

**Q5.** Coca-Cola's cost advantage comes from scale — producing billions of bottles globally. If a Chinese soda brand wanted to **build a similar cost advantage**, what would it need to do? Is this realistic? What alternative strategies might be more achievable?

# Dewu / Poizon (得物) — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.2 Markets and Competitive Advantage** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

---

## 1. Company Introduction

**Dewu (得物)**, known internationally as **Poizon**, is a Chinese e-commerce platform specializing in **authenticated sneakers, streetwear, and luxury goods.** It is the largest sneaker resale and authentication platform in China.

- **Founded:** 2015 by **Yang Bing (杨冰)** as a feature within **Hupu (虎扑)**, China's largest sports fan community; became an independent app in 2017
- **Product:** A C2C2C platform where sellers list sneakers and streetwear, Dewu **authenticates** every item before shipping to the buyer, ensuring genuineness
- **Founder background:** Yang Bing was a sneaker enthusiast and Hupu user who noticed that sneaker trading was booming in Hupu's forums but plagued by counterfeit products. He built an authentication service to solve the trust problem.
- **Industry:** E-commerce / Sneaker resale / Luxury goods authentication

As of 2024, Dewu has over **300 million registered users**, primarily Gen Z consumers. The platform processes millions of transactions annually and has expanded beyond sneakers into streetwear, luxury bags, watches, cosmetics, and electronics.

**Revenue model:** Dewu generates revenue through two sources: (1) a **commission fee** on each transaction between buyers and sellers on the platform, and (2) an **authentication fee** charged for verifying the authenticity of sneakers and luxury goods before they are shipped to the buyer.

---

## 2. Key Events

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **2015** | Sneaker trading feature launches within **Hupu (虎扑)** — users buy, sell, and discuss sneakers in dedicated forums |
| **2017** | Spun off as an independent app called **"毒" (Poison/Du)**; introduces **authentication service** — every sneaker is verified by experts before shipping to the buyer |
| **2018–2019** | Rapid growth among Gen Z sneaker collectors; app gains millions of users; becomes the go-to platform for authentic sneakers in China |
| **2020** | Rebrands from "毒" to **"得物" (Dewu)** — meaning "get good stuff" — to appeal to a broader audience beyond sneakerheads |
| **2020–2022** | Expands product categories: streetwear, luxury bags, watches, cosmetics, electronics, and collectible toys; reaches **300 million registered users** |
| **2023–2024** | Becomes one of China's most popular e-commerce platforms among Gen Z; challenges established players like Taobao and JD.com in specific categories |

---

## Questions

**Q1.** Dewu authenticates every sneaker before it reaches the buyer. How does this create a **competitive advantage** over platforms like Taobao or eBay, where counterfeits are a known risk? What **type** of competitive advantage is this — cost advantage or differentiation?

**Q2.** Dewu targets Gen Z sneaker collectors specifically, not the mass market. Using the concept of **target customer selection** from Topic 1.2, explain why focusing on a specific customer segment can be a more effective strategy than trying to serve everyone.

**Q3.** Dewu is a platform — it does not own the products sold on it. Explain how Dewu **creates value** for both buyers and sellers. How does the platform **capture value** for itself?

**Q4.** Dewu rebranded from "毒" (Poison) to "得物" (Get Good Stuff) to appeal to a broader audience. What does this tell you about the relationship between **brand identity** and **target customers**? How might the name change affect Dewu's competitive position?

**Q5.** Compare Dewu's **differentiation strategy** with Coca-Cola's **cost advantage strategy** (see the Cola vs. Chinese Soda handout). Both are forms of competitive advantage. In what situations is differentiation more effective than cost advantage? Give an example from each case.

# Genki Forest (元气森林) — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.2 Markets and Competitive Advantage** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

## 1. Company Introduction

**Genki Forest (元气森林)** is a Chinese beverage company that disrupted the soft drink market by launching **zero-sugar, zero-calorie sparkling water** in a market dominated by sugary sodas and traditional tea drinks. The zero-sugar formula uses erythritol and stevia (natural sweeteners) instead of sugar. The brand became one of China's fastest-growing consumer companies.

- **Founded:** 2016 by **Tang Binsen (唐彬森)** in Beijing, China
- **Product:** Zero-sugar sparkling water, zero-sugar tea drinks, and other health-oriented beverages — sold in distinctive minimalist packaging with Japanese-inspired branding
- **Founder background:** Tang Binsen was a tech entrepreneur (founder of a mobile gaming company) who applied a data-driven, rapid-testing approach to the beverage industry. He identified the growing health-consciousness trend among Chinese consumers and designed products to match.
- **Industry:** Beverage / Consumer packaged goods (CPG)

Genki Forest grew from zero to over **$1.5 billion in annual revenue** within five years. The brand is now sold in convenience stores, supermarkets, and online platforms across China, and has expanded internationally to Southeast Asia, Japan, and North America.

**Branding:** Genki Forest uses the Japanese kanji character **"気"** (ki, meaning "spirit" or "energy") in its branding instead of the simplified Chinese character "气." The product packaging and visual identity are designed to evoke a Japanese aesthetic, positioning the brand as a premium imported product in the minds of Chinese consumers — even though Genki Forest is a Chinese company.

---

## 2. Key Events

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **2016** | Tang Binsen founds Genki Forest; launches first product — zero-sugar sparkling water in minimalist, Japanese-inspired packaging |
| **2017–2018** | Early growth through **convenience store distribution** — the brand places products in FamilyMart, 7-Eleven, and other chains where young urban consumers shop |
| **2019** | Product goes **viral** on social media — health-conscious consumers embrace zero-sugar drinks; sales grow 300%+ year-over-year |
| **2020** | Annual revenue reportedly exceeds **3 billion yuan (~$450M)**; brand becomes a household name among young Chinese consumers; raises significant VC funding |
| **2021** | Annual revenue reportedly exceeds **7 billion yuan (~$1 billion)**; expands product line to include milk tea, juice, and sports drinks — all zero-sugar or low-sugar |
| **2022–2024** | Competition intensifies — Coca-Cola launches "AH!HA!" sparkling water, Pepsi launches "Bubly," and numerous Chinese brands enter the zero-sugar market; Genki Forest faces pressure to maintain market share; continues international expansion |

---

## Questions

**Q1.** Genki Forest differentiated itself through **zero-sugar positioning, Japanese-inspired packaging, and targeting health-conscious young consumers.** Using the concept of **differentiation** from Topic 1.2, explain which of these three factors is most important for sustaining competitive advantage. Which factor is easiest for competitors to copy?

**Q2.** Tang Binsen was a tech entrepreneur, not a beverage expert. He applied a **data-driven, rapid-testing approach** to developing drinks. How does this relate to the concept of **testing and refining a hypothesis** from Topic 1.4 ? What was the hypothesis, and how did Genki Forest test it?

**Q3.** Genki Forest uses the Japanese kanji "気" in its brand name and Japanese-inspired packaging — even though it is a Chinese company. # Zhong Xue Gao (钟薛高) — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.2 Markets and Competitive Advantage** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

---

## 1. Company Introduction

**Zhong Xue Gao (钟薛高)** was a Chinese premium ice cream brand that positioned itself as a luxury alternative to mass-market ice cream. The name is a play on words — it sounds like a traditional Chinese name but is also a homophone for "中雪糕" (Chinese ice cream).

- **Founded:** 2018 by **Lin Sheng (林盛)** in Shanghai, China

# 2.3 New Coke — Market Research

> **Topic:** 2.3 Market Research
> **Company:** Coca-Cola

---

## Background

In the 1980s, **Coca-Cola** was losing the "Cola Wars" to Pepsi. After World War II, Coca-Cola had owned **60% of the cola market**; by 1983, that share had fallen to **less than 24%**. Pepsi intensified the pressure by launching the **"Pepsi Challenge"** — blindfolded taste tests in which people overwhelmingly chose Pepsi over Coca-Cola. The public results were humiliating for a brand that had dominated the cola category for nearly a century.

Coca-Cola CEO **Roberto Goizueta** led the company's response. Born in Cuba and trained as a chemical engineer, Goizueta became president of Coca-Cola in 1979. Earlier in his career, he had successfully tweaked Coke's flavor for the Bahamas market — a past success that later created **confirmation bias** when he championed a much larger reformulation. Under his leadership, Coca-Cola launched **"Project Kansas"** — a secret plan to create a sweeter version of classic Coca-Cola that could defeat Pepsi in head-to-head taste tests.

---

## Key Events

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **1983** | Coca-Cola's market share falls below 24%, down from 60% after WWII. Pepsi's "Pepsi Challenge" campaign gains national attention |
| **1983–1985** | Project Kansas begins — Coca-Cola secretly develops a sweeter reformulation of classic Coca-Cola |
| **1984** | Coca-Cola spends approximately **$12 million** (in today's dollars) conducting market research on New Coke, enrolling **200,000 participants** in taste tests and focus groups. The taste test methodology gave participants only a small sip of each product — not a full can. This is significant because sip tests favor sweeter drinks (New Coke was sweeter), while drinking a full can favors less sweet, more familiar flavors (original Coca-Cola) |
| **April 23, 1985 — "Black Tuesday"** | Coca-Cola announces that New Coke will replace classic Coca-Cola. Pepsi had prepped **200+ reporters** with tough questions before the press conference. Media immediately frames it as a "massive misstep" |
| **April–July 1985** | Coca-Cola's Atlanta headquarters receives **8,000+ angry calls per day**. Demonstrations and letter-writing campaigns erupt across the country |
| **July 1985 — 3 months later** | Coca-Cola brings back the original formula as **"Coca-Cola Classic"** |

### Six Features of Penetration Pricing

| # | Feature | Definition |

| 6 | **Long-term profitability** | Sacrificing short-term profits to achieve long-term gains through scale and retention |

| # | Limitation | Risk |

| 2 | **Quality perception and brand value** | Consumers may associate low price with low quality, damaging the brand |

# 3.6 Hershey — The Income Statement

> **Topic:** 3.6 The Income Statement
> **Company:** Hershey

---

## Background

**The Hershey Company** is one of the largest chocolate and confectionery manufacturers in the world, headquartered in Hershey, Pennsylvania. Founded in 1894 by **Milton S. Hershey**, the company produces iconic brands including Hershey's Kisses, Reese's Peanut Butter Cups, Kit Kat (under license in the US), Twizzlers, and Ice Breakers. As of 2024, Hershey generates approximately **$11.2 billion in annual revenue**, employs over **19,000 people**, and sells products in more than **80 countries**.

Hershey's business model is straightforward: the company **manufactures and sells confectionery products** (chocolate, candy, mints, and snacks) through retail channels including grocery stores, convenience stores, vending machines, and increasingly through e-commerce. Approximately **90% of revenue** comes from North America.

In 2024, Hershey faces a **major financial challenge**: the global price of **cocoa beans** — the primary raw material for chocolate — surged to record highs. Cocoa prices reached over **$10,000 per metric ton** in early 2024, more than **tripling** from $3,000 in late 2022. This is the most severe commodity cost increase in the chocolate industry in decades, driven by poor harvests in West Africa (which produces ~70% of the world's cocoa) caused by climate change, disease, and aging trees.

This cocoa price surge directly impacts Hershey's **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)** and compresses the company's **profit margins**. Hershey's management must decide how to respond: absorb the cost increase (reducing profits), raise prices (risking lower sales volume), reformulate products (reducing cocoa content), or some combination of all three.

---

## Key Events

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **1894** | Milton S. Hershey founds the Hershey Chocolate Company in Lancaster, Pennsylvania |

## Background

## Background

**Yardley Manufacturing, Inc.** is a mid-sized manufacturer of office furniture (desks, filing cabinets, and ergonomic chairs) headquartered in Grand Rapids, Michigan. Founded in 1988 by **Richard Yardley**, the company has grown into a profitable business generating approximately **$48 million in annual revenue**, employing **210 people**, and selling products to office supply retailers, corporate buyers, and government agencies across the United States.

Yardley went public in 2005 and trades on the NASDAQ under the ticker **YARD**. As a publicly traded corporation, Yardley is required to file quarterly and annual financial reports with the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** and to have its financial statements audited by an independent accounting firm. The company's stock price is closely watched by investors, and executive compensation is tied to meeting quarterly earnings targets.

In early 2024, **Lisa Morales**, Yardley's Director of Finance, faces an ethical dilemma. The company is about to report Q4 2023 earnings, and the numbers are below the **earnings target** that analysts expect. Missing the target would likely cause Yardley's stock price to drop, reduce executive bonuses, and potentially trigger a review by the Board of Directors. Lisa's supervisor, **CFO David Park**, suggests "adjusting" the numbers to meet the target — specifically, by recording $1.2 million in revenue from a large order that has not yet been shipped to the customer.

Lisa knows that recording revenue before the product is shipped violates **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** and SEC regulations. But she also knows that the order is confirmed and the shipment will go out in early January — just a few weeks later. David tells her: "We're not lying — the sale is real. We're just moving it forward by a few weeks."

## The Ethical Dilemma — A Detailed View

### What Happened

| Step | Action | Date | Ethical Issue |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Corporate client places a $1.2M order for 400 ergonomic desks | Dec 5, 2023 | Order is real — no issue yet |
| 2 | Production is scheduled; desks will be ready to ship by Jan 8, 2024 | Dec 10, 2023 | Standard production timeline |
| 3 | CFO David Park tells Lisa: "Record the revenue now — the order is confirmed" | Dec 15, 2023 | **Request to violate GAAP revenue recognition rules** |
| 4 | Lisa records $1.2M in Q4 2023 revenue, increasing accounts receivable | Dec 20, 2023 | **Revenue recorded before product shipped — violates GAAP** |
| 5 | Yardley reports Q4 and FY2023 earnings that meet analyst targets | Jan 25, 2024 | **Financial statements are misleading** |
| 6 | Desks ship to the customer on January 12, 2024 | Jan 12, 2024 | Revenue should have been recognized here, in Q1 2024 |
| 7 | External auditor discovers the discrepancy during annual audit | Feb 2024 | **Fraud detected** |

### Why It Matters

The $1.2 million adjustment changed Yardley's financial picture:

| Metric | With Fraud (Reported) | Without Fraud (Actual) | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q4 2023 Revenue | $13.2M | $12.0M | +$1.2M |
| FY2023 Revenue | $48.5M | $47.3M | +$1.2M |
| FY2023 Net Income | $3.8M | $2.9M | +$0.9M |
| Earnings Per Share (EPS) | $1.52 | $1.16 | +$0.36 |
| Met analyst target? | ✅ Yes ($1.50) | ❌ No ($1.16) | — |

> **The fraud was not about the money — it was about the target.** Yardley did not invent a fake sale. The order was real. But by recording it in the wrong quarter, Lisa and David made the company appear more profitable than it actually was in FY2023, which directly affected stock price, executive bonuses, and investor decisions.

## Background

**ExpressionMed** is a small business founded in **2018** by **Meghan Sharkus** in **Chicago, Illinois**, when she was just 17 years old. The company designs and manufactures **decorative adhesive patches and accessories** for people who wear **continuous glucose monitors (CGMs)** — medical devices used by people with diabetes to track their blood sugar levels 24/7.

CGMs like the Dexcom G6/G7 and Abbott FreeStyle Libre are small sensors worn on the abdomen or arm that continuously measure glucose levels. While these devices are medically essential, many users — especially children and teenagers — feel self-conscious about wearing them. The devices look clinical and can draw unwanted questions or stares. Meghan, who was diagnosed with Type 1 diabetes at age 4, experienced this firsthand and created ExpressionMed to solve the problem.

ExpressionMed's products are **thin, colorful, waterproof adhesive overlays** that stick over the CGM sensor, allowing users to express their personality while wearing their medical device. The patches come in hundreds of designs — from sports teams and florals to abstract art and holiday themes. They also serve a practical function: the adhesive helps the CGM stay attached to the skin longer, reducing the frequency of sensor reattachments.

As of **2024**, ExpressionMed:
