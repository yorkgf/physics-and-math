# Bombas — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.1 What Is a Business** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

## Background

Bombas is an American apparel company founded in 2013 by David Heath and Randy Goldberg in New York City. The company sells socks, underwear, and T-shirts through its website and retail partners such as Nordstrom and Dick's Sporting Goods. Bombas operates a buy-one-give-one model: for every item purchased, one is donated to a homeless shelter.

The founders were inspired by a statistic: socks are the most requested clothing item in homeless shelters. They launched a Kickstarter campaign in 2013 and raised $140,000 in 30 days. In 2014, Bombas appeared on Shark Tank Season 6 and secured a deal with Daymond John ($200,000 for 17.5% equity). As of 2024, Bombas has donated over 100 million items. The company sells premium socks at higher prices than mass-market brands, citing features such as seamless toe construction, arch support, and blister tabs.

# VANCL (凡客诚品) — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.1 What Is a Business** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

---

## Background

VANCL (凡客诚品) was a Chinese e-commerce company founded in 2007 by Chen Nian (陈年) in Beijing. The company sold apparel and lifestyle products directly to consumers online at low prices. Chen Nian was a co-founder of Joyo.com (卓越网), one of China's early e-commerce platforms, which was later acquired by Amazon.

VANCL grew rapidly between 2008 and 2011 through aggressive internet advertising (banner ads, search engine marketing). The company's value proposition was decent-quality clothing at the lowest possible price, delivered to the customer's door. At its peak in 2010, VANCL's valuation exceeded $3 billion, and it sold tens of millions of items per year. The brand marketed itself as "人民快时尚" (people's fast fashion).

In 2011, VANCL began expanding product lines far beyond apparel — into electronics, home goods, cosmetics, and kitchenware. Inventory management became difficult, product quality declined, and the brand identity became diluted. Revenue collapsed in 2013–2014, and the company laid off thousands of employees. VANCL has since shrunk to a fraction of its former scale.

# ofo (小黄车) — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.1 What Is a Business** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

---

## Background

ofo was a Chinese bike-sharing company founded in 2014 by Dai Wei (戴威) at Peking University, Beijing. The service allowed users to unlock and ride yellow bicycles using a smartphone app, then park them at their destination. ofo operated a dockless model — no fixed stations were required.

Dai Wei started by running a campus bike-sharing program for Peking University students. He expanded the concept to other university campuses, then to cities across China and internationally. At its peak in 2017, ofo operated over 10 million bicycles in more than 200 cities across 20 countries. The company raised over $2 billion in venture capital and was valued at approximately $3 billion.

ofo's primary revenue came from ride fees paid by users through the app. During 2016–2017, ofo engaged in a subsidy war with competitor Mobike (摩拜), offering rides for as little as 1 yuan or free. By 2018, the company faced a cash flow crisis: maintenance costs for millions of bicycles exceeded revenue, and the subsidy war had burned through much of its funding. Millions of users queued online to withdraw their deposits. The company effectively ceased major operations by 2019–2020, and Dai Wei was reportedly restricted from high-speed rail and flights due to unpaid debts.

> **Topic 1.2 Markets and Competitive Advantage** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

## Background

Genki Forest (元气森林) is a Chinese beverage company founded in 2016 by Tang Binsen (唐彬森) in Beijing. The company sells zero-sugar, zero-calorie sparkling water and tea drinks in a market dominated by sugary sodas and traditional tea beverages. The zero-sugar formula uses erythritol and stevia (natural sweeteners) instead of sugar.

Tang Binsen was a tech entrepreneur — founder of a mobile gaming company — who applied a data-driven, rapid-testing approach to the beverage industry. He identified a growing health-consciousness trend among Chinese consumers and designed products to match. Genki Forest uses the Japanese kanji character "気" (ki) in its branding instead of the simplified Chinese character "气." The packaging and visual identity are designed to evoke a Japanese aesthetic, positioning the brand as a premium product — even though Genki Forest is a Chinese company.

Genki Forest grew from zero to over $1.5 billion in annual revenue within five years. The brand is sold in convenience stores (FamilyMart, 7-Eleven), supermarkets, and online platforms across China, and has expanded to Southeast Asia, Japan, and North America.

# Coca-Cola vs. Chinese Soda Brands — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.2 Markets and Competitive Advantage** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

## Background

This case compares Coca-Cola — the world's largest beverage company — with Chinese domestic soda brands such as Tianfu Cola (天府可乐), Laoshan Cola (崂山可乐), Beipingyang (北冰洋), and Bingfeng (冰峰).

Coca-Cola sells a bottle of cola for about 3 yuan (~$0.40) in China and earns a profit. Chinese domestic soda brands sell their cola for 7 yuan (~$1.00) or more, and many struggle to break even.

| Factor | Coca-Cola | Chinese Soda Brands |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale** | Global operations in 200+ countries; billions of bottles/year | Regional operations; millions of bottles/year |
| **Supply chain** | Global supply chain; negotiates lowest prices for sugar, packaging, logistics | Smaller supply chains; higher per-unit costs for ingredients and packaging |

| **Price per bottle (China)** | ~3 yuan | ~7 yuan or more |
| **Profitability** | Profitable at 3 yuan | Many brands struggle to break even at 7 yuan |

The Chinese cola market is dominated by two global firms: Coca-Cola and Pepsi. Domestic brands survive through regional loyalty built on local taste preferences and nostalgia — for example, Beipingyang is preferred in Beijing and Bingfeng in Xi'an.

# Zhong Xue Gao (钟薛高) — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.2 Markets and Competitive Advantage** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

---

## Background

Zhong Xue Gao (钟薛高) was a Chinese premium ice cream brand founded in 2018 by Lin Sheng (林盛) in Shanghai. The name sounds like a traditional Chinese name and is also a homophone for "中雪糕" (Chinese ice cream). The company sold ice cream bars priced at 13–66 yuan (~$2–$9 USD) per bar — 3 to 10 times the price of standard ice cream. The products were marketed as made with natural ingredients and featured a distinctive curved wafer-tile shape.

Lin Sheng was a marketing veteran who previously worked on branding for other food companies. The brand grew rapidly in 2019–2021 through social media marketing, and annual sales reportedly exceeded 1 billion yuan. The company raised venture capital funding and expanded distribution to convenience stores, supermarkets, and online platforms.

In 2022, a series of controversies led to a collapse. Consumers discovered that some products were sold at inflated prices in convenience stores and called Zhong Xue Gao an "雪糕刺客" (ice cream assassin) — products that look affordable but cost far more than expected at the register. Reports also emerged that some products did not melt even when left at room temperature for extended periods, raising questions about ingredient quality. By 2024, the brand had largely disappeared from the market, and the company faced lawsuits from suppliers and partners.

# Haidilao (海底捞) — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.2 Markets and Competitive Advantage** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

---

## Background

Haidilao International Holding Ltd. is a Chinese hot pot restaurant chain founded in 1994 by Zhang Yong in Jianyang, Sichuan Province. Customers cook their own ingredients in a shared pot of broth at the table. As of 2024, Haidilao operates over 1,400 restaurants in China and internationally (U.S., U.K., Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Australia), with annual revenue exceeding $5 billion. The company went public on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange in 2018.

Haidilao's hot pot is similar to what hundreds of other Chinese hot pot chains serve. What distinguishes Haidilao is its service model: while waiting for a table, customers receive free manicures, snacks, and shoe-shining services. During the meal, staff perform noodle-pulling demonstrations and sing birthday songs. Staff are trained to anticipate customer needs — offering phone cases, charging cables, and lap blankets for children — and are empowered to resolve problems on the spot without manager approval. Employees who receive positive customer feedback earn bonuses and promotion opportunities. Zhang Yong's stated philosophy is: "Take care of your employees first; they will take care of your customers."

Haidilao's employees go through an in-house training program focused on customer service skills, not culinary technique. The company does not recruit from culinary schools. Its training costs per employee are among the highest in the Chinese restaurant industry.

# Dewu / Poizon (得物) — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.2 Markets and Competitive Advantage** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

## Background

Dewu (得物), known internationally as Poizon, is a Chinese e-commerce platform specializing in sneakers, streetwear, and luxury goods. It was founded in 2015 by Yang Bing (杨冰) as a feature within Hupu (虎扑), China's largest sports fan community. The platform became an independent app in 2017.

Dewu operates as a C2C2C platform: sellers list sneakers and streetwear, Dewu authenticates every item before shipping to the buyer, and charges both a commission fee and an authentication fee. Yang Bing noticed that sneaker trading was booming in Hupu's forums but plagued by counterfeits. He built an authentication service to address this problem.

As of 2024, Dewu has over 300 million registered users, primarily Gen Z consumers. The platform has expanded beyond sneakers into streetwear, luxury bags, watches, cosmetics, and electronics. In 2020, the company rebranded from "毒" (Poison) to "得物" (meaning "get good stuff") to appeal to a broader audience.

# China's "Double Reduction" Policy & New Oriental (新东方) — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.3 PESTEL Factors** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

## Background

New Oriental Education & Technology Group (新东方) was China's largest private education company, founded in 1993 by Yu Minhong (俞敏洪) in Beijing. Yu Minhong was a former Peking University English professor who started by teaching English to students preparing to study abroad. The company expanded to offer K-12 after-school tutoring, English language training, overseas test preparation (TOEFL, IELTS, SAT), and education technology.

New Oriental went public on the New York Stock Exchange in 2006 — the first Chinese education company listed in the U.S. By 2020, the company operated over 100 learning centers across China with more than 50,000 teachers, served millions of students annually, and was valued at over $20 billion.

In July 2021, the Chinese government announced the "Double Reduction" (双减) policy, which banned for-profit K-12 academic tutoring. New Oriental's stock crashed over 90% in days. The company laid off 60,000+ employees and closed most learning centers. In 2022, Yu Minhong launched Oriental Selection (东方甄选), a live-stream e-commerce platform where former teachers sell agricultural products and consumer goods on camera. Former teachers brought transferable skills from the classroom: audience engagement, clear communication, on-camera presentation, and storytelling.

# Patagonia — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.5 Vision** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

---

## Background

Patagonia is an American outdoor clothing and gear company founded in 1973 by Yvon Chouinard in Ventura, California. Chouinard was a lifelong climber and environmentalist who started by forging his own climbing pitons. The company sells outdoor clothing, climbing gear, camping equipment, and food products.

Patagonia's stated vision is: "We're in business to save our home planet." The company has made several decisions that prioritized environmental goals over short-term profit:

- In 1996, Patagonia switched 100% of its cotton line to organic cotton. This increased raw material costs by an estimated 50%, and the company had to discontinue one-third of its product line because organic cotton suppliers could not produce certain weaves.
- In 2011, Patagonia ran a full-page Black Friday ad in the New York Times: "Don't Buy This Jacket" — telling consumers to buy less and reduce waste. Despite the anti-consumption message, revenue grew approximately 30% the following year (from ~$540M to ~$700M).
- In 2012, the company launched the Worn Wear program, encouraging customers to repair and reuse clothing instead of buying new. Patagonia offers free lifetime repairs and operates the largest garment repair facility in North America.
- In 2013, Patagonia filed as a Benefit Corporation, legally committing to consider environmental and social impact alongside profit.

In September 2022, Chouinard transferred ownership: 98% of shares to Holdfast Collective (a nonprofit fighting climate change), 2% to a trust ensuring mission enforcement. All future profit goes to environmental causes. Chouinard's public statement: "Earth is now our only shareholder."

# Seesaw Coffee — Case Handout

> **Topic 1.5 Vision** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

## Background

Seesaw Coffee was a Chinese specialty coffee brand founded in 2012 in Shanghai by Zuo Zuojun (左佐君). The company positioned itself as a pioneer of China's "third wave" coffee movement — emphasizing craft, skilled baristas, and curated café environments. Seesaw attracted a loyal following among urban professionals and received venture capital funding beginning in 2017.

Seesaw's stated purpose was: "Bring specialty coffee into everyone's daily life." The brand expanded aggressively into multiple Chinese cities between 2017 and 2021. However, in 2023, a price war erupted in China's coffee market — Luckin Coffee and Cotti Coffee launched 9.9-yuan coupons (~$1.35 USD per cup). Seesaw responded by launching its own 9.9-yuan promotions to retain customers. Because specialty beans and trained baristas cost far more than 9.9 yuan per cup, margins collapsed.

By 2024, reports surfaced of delayed supplier payments, employee salary arrears, and store closures. The company entered bankruptcy/liquidation in 2024–2025.

# 2.5 Costco (开市客) — Case Handout

> **Topic 2.5 Price** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

## Background

Costco Wholesale Corporation is the world's third-largest retailer, operating over 890 warehouse clubs across 14 countries. Founded in 1983 in Seattle by **Jim Sinegal** and **Jeff Brotman**, Costco operates on a membership warehouse model — customers pay an annual fee for the privilege of shopping at Costco's no-frills warehouses, where they find a limited selection of products at low prices.

Costco's business model is different from traditional retailers. While most stores aim to maximize profit on each product sold, Costco **caps its markup at 14%** on most items and **15% on Kirkland Signature** (its private label). Costco intentionally limits how much money it makes on products and instead generates almost all of its profit from **membership fees**.

As of 2025, Costco has over **135 million cardholders** worldwide, with a **membership renewal rate above 90%**. The company generates approximately **$4.8 billion annually** from membership fees alone — a figure that represents nearly all of its operating profit.

## Costco's Loss-Leader Strategy — Key Facts

- Rotisserie chicken is priced at **$4.99** — this price has been in place since **2009** and has never been raised.
- The chicken is placed at the **very back of the store** — shoppers must walk through the entire warehouse to reach it, passing hundreds of products along the way.
- In 2019, Costco built a **$450 million poultry processing plant** in Fremont, Nebraska to control costs and protect the $4.99 price point.
- The Fremont plant ships **1 million chickens per week**.
- Costco loses an estimated **$30-40 million per year** selling rotisserie chicken at $4.99 — intentionally, to drive store traffic.
- Costco's **gross margin on products is approximately 12%**, compared to Walmart's approximately 24%. Costco compensates for lower margins through high sales volume and membership fees, which generate nearly all of its operating profit.

# 2.7 Bilibili & YouTube — Case Handout

> **Topic 2.7 Promotion and Marketing Communications** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

---

## Background

**Bilibili (B站)** is a Chinese video-sharing platform founded in 2009, originally focused on anime, comics, and games (ACG) content. By 2025, Bilibili had grown to **366 million monthly active users (MAU)** and **113 million daily active users (DAU)**, with an average user age of **26 years old** — one of the youngest large-platform audiences in China. Users spend an average of **99 minutes per day** on the platform. Unlike most video platforms, Bilibili's culture centers on **danmu (弹幕)**, a real-time bullet-comment system where viewer comments scroll across the video screen, creating a shared viewing experience.

Bilibili's advertising model is fundamentally **creator-driven**. Instead of serving ads before or during videos (as most platforms do), Bilibili connects brands with **UP主 (Uploaders)** — the platform's content creators — who integrate products into their original videos. The official brand-creator marketplace is called **Huahuo (花火)**. Bilibili does not insert pre-roll or mid-roll ads into creator videos; nearly all advertising revenue comes from brand-creator collaborations. In 2025, Bilibili generated **RMB 10.06 billion (~$1.44 billion) in advertising revenue**, a 23% increase year over year. Approximately **3 million creators** earned income on the platform, with average creator income growing **21% year over year**.

**YouTube** is a global video-sharing platform owned by Google/Alphabet, with over **2.7 billion monthly logged-in users** across all age groups and regions. YouTube's primary advertising model is **platform-served ads**: the platform automatically inserts video ads before (pre-roll), during (mid-roll), or after (post-roll) creator content. Advertisers bid through **Google Ads** in a real-time auction system. When a viewer loads a video, the auction runs in milliseconds, and the winning ad is served. YouTube shares **55% of Watch Page ad revenue** with creators (45% for Shorts), paying through AdSense. YouTube's parent company Alphabet generated over **$350 billion in total ad revenue** in 2025.

---

## How Each Advertising Model Works

### Bilibili: Creator-Embedded Model (UP主带货)

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Official marketplace** | **Huahuo (花火)**: brands browse UP主 profiles, negotiate collaborations, and pay directly. Bilibili charges only a **5% platform commission**. |
| **Open marketplace** | **Xuanshang (悬赏)**: brands upload products; UP主 voluntarily choose which to promote. UP主 earn **CPS (Cost Per Sale) commissions of 20–50%**. Requires 10,000+ followers. |
| **Ad formats** | **Interstitial**: UP主 briefly mentions or demonstrates the product within their regular content. **Brand creative collaboration**: UP主 creates a fully customized original video around the brand. |
| **Cost formula** | ~**10% of follower count in RMB**. A UP主 with 100,000 followers charges approximately **RMB 10,000 (~$1,500 USD)** per video. |
| **Budget allocation** | Recommended: **70% to creators**, 30% to Qifei (paid amplification to boost reach). |
| **Content lifespan** | Videos remain on the platform indefinitely; conversion half-life is up to **1 month** — videos keep generating sales weeks after posting. |

### YouTube: Platform-Served Model (贴片广告)

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Skippable ads (TrueView)** | Viewer can **skip after 5 seconds**. Advertiser pays only when viewer watches **30+ seconds** or interacts. CPV: **$0.10–$0.30**. |
| **Non-skippable ads** | Viewer must watch the entire ad (**7–15 seconds**). Billed on **CPM (cost per 1,000 impressions)**: **$5–$30+**. |
| **Bumper ads** | **6 seconds or less**, non-skippable. CPM: **$5–$20**. |
| **Auction system** | Advertisers bid through Google Ads. Winning bid = bid amount + ad quality/relevance score. The winner never pays more than their submitted bid. |
| **Revenue share** | Creators receive **55% of net ad revenue** on Watch Page ads; YouTube keeps **45%**. For Shorts, creators receive 45%. |
| **Content lifespan** | Ads are **one-time impressions** — they do not create a lasting content asset for the brand. |

---

## Key Metrics Comparison

| Metric | Bilibili UP主 | YouTube Pre-Roll |
|--------|--------------|------------------|
| **CPM / cost** | ~10% of follower count per video (mid-tier: $1,500–$5,000) | CPM $3–$30+; CPV $0.10–$0.30 |
| **Conversion rate** | **4.1%** (Bilibili links to JD.com — 2× the Douyin rate) | **1.5–3.0%** benchmark |
| **Viewer skip rate** | N/A — no forced ads; viewers chose the video | **75–85%** of viewers skip skippable ads within 5 seconds |
| **Watch completion** | **62%** average (for engaged viewers who chose to watch) | **40–60%** for 30-second skippable ads |
| **Trust mechanism** | Parasocial relationship: fans trust UP主 recommendations | No trust mechanism; ad is from an unknown brand |
| **Branded search lift** | **340%** increase in Baidu searches after UP主 video (case study) | **5–20%** brand lift (industry benchmark) |
| **Content longevity** | Videos stay searchable and watchable for months; ongoing passive conversion | One-time impression; no lasting content asset |
| **Platform commission** | Huahuo: **5%** | YouTube: **45%** of ad revenue |

---

In 2024, an ergonomic chair brand ran a campaign on Bilibili using **3 mid-tier UP主** (80,000–220,000 followers in the tech/gaming category). Each UP主 created a **15-minute product review video** integrating the chair into their regular content.

By comparison, a YouTube pre-roll campaign for a similar product category would typically achieve a **1.5–3.0% conversion rate** at a CPA (cost per acquisition) of **$20–$100**, with most viewers skipping the ad before the message is delivered.

# 3.6 Hershey — The Income Statement

> **Topic 3.6 The Income Statement** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

## Background

**The Hershey Company** is one of the largest chocolate and confectionery manufacturers in the world, headquartered in Hershey, Pennsylvania. Founded in 1894 by **Milton S. Hershey**, the company produces brands including Hershey's Kisses, Reese's Peanut Butter Cups, Kit Kat (under license in the US), Twizzlers, and Ice Breakers. As of 2024, Hershey generates approximately **$11.2 billion in annual revenue**, employs over **19,000 people**, and sells products in more than **80 countries**.

Hershey's business model: the company **manufactures and sells confectionery products** (chocolate, candy, mints, and snacks) through retail channels including grocery stores, convenience stores, vending machines, and e-commerce. Approximately **90% of revenue** comes from North America.

In 2024, Hershey faces a financial challenge: the global price of **cocoa beans** — the primary raw material for chocolate — surged to record highs. Cocoa prices reached over **$10,000 per metric ton** in early 2024, more than **tripling** from $3,000 in late 2022. This increase is driven by poor harvests in West Africa (which produces ~70% of the world's cocoa) caused by climate change, disease, and aging trees.

This cocoa price surge directly impacts Hershey's **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)** and compresses the company's **profit margins**. Hershey's management must decide how to respond: absorb the cost increase (reducing profits), raise prices (risking lower sales volume), reformulate products (reducing cocoa content), or some combination.

Industry analysts project U.S. confectionery market growth of **2–3%** in 2025 (down from 5% in 2024) as consumers reduce discretionary spending. Hershey employs approximately **19,500 people** globally at an average total compensation (salary + benefits) of **$85,000 per employee per year**.

# 3.9 Yardley — Ethics and Financial Reporting

> **Topic 3.9 Ethics and Financial Reporting** · Read before class. Be ready to discuss.

## Background

**Yardley Manufacturing, Inc.** is a mid-sized manufacturer of office furniture (desks, filing cabinets, and ergonomic chairs) headquartered in Grand Rapids, Michigan. Founded in 1988 by **Richard Yardley**, the company generates approximately **$48 million in annual revenue**, employs **210 people**, and sells products to office supply retailers, corporate buyers, and government agencies across the United States.

Yardley went public in 2005 and trades on the NASDAQ under the ticker **YARD**. As a publicly traded corporation, Yardley is required to file quarterly and annual financial reports with the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** and to have its financial statements audited by an independent accounting firm. The company's stock price is closely watched by investors, and executive compensation is tied to meeting quarterly earnings targets.

In early 2024, **Lisa Morales**, Yardley's Director of Finance, faces an ethical dilemma. The company is about to report Q4 2023 earnings, and the numbers are below the **earnings target** that analysts expect. Missing the target would likely cause Yardley's stock price to drop, reduce executive bonuses, and potentially trigger a review by the Board of Directors. Lisa's supervisor, **CFO David Park**, suggests "adjusting" the numbers to meet the target — specifically, by recording $1.2 million in revenue from a large order that has not yet been shipped to the customer.

Lisa knows that recording revenue before the product is shipped violates **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** and SEC regulations. But she also knows that the order is confirmed and the shipment will go out in early January — just a few weeks later. David tells her: "We're not lying — the sale is real. We're just moving it forward by a few weeks."

|---|---|---|---|

## Background

**Canva** is an online graphic design platform that allows anyone — from professional designers to students to small business owners — to create visual content including presentations, social media graphics, posters, videos, and documents. Founded in 2013 in Sydney, Australia, by **Melanie Perkins**, **Cliff Obrecht**, and **Cameron Adams**, Canva has grown into one of the world's most valuable private technology companies, valued at approximately **$26 billion** as of 2024. Canva's mission is to **"empower everyone in the world to design anything and publish anywhere."** This mission shapes management decisions — from hiring, to internal communication, to compensation and retention. Canva operates in a competitive market against Adobe, Microsoft, Google, and dozens of smaller design tools.

Canva's **management structure** has evolved as the company scaled from a small startup to a global enterprise. In 2024, Canva reorganized its 4,000+ employees into **"groups"** — small, autonomous teams of 8–12 people, each with ownership of a product area. This structure replaces the traditional corporate hierarchy with a model designed to keep teams agile, accountable, and aligned with the company's mission.

## Background

**ExpressionMed** is a small business founded in **2018** by **Meghan Sharkus** in **Chicago, Illinois**, when she was 17 years old. The company designs and manufactures **decorative adhesive patches and accessories** for people who wear **continuous glucose monitors (CGMs)** — medical devices used by people with diabetes to track their blood sugar levels 24/7.

CGMs like the Dexcom G6/G7 and Abbott FreeStyle Libre are small sensors worn on the abdomen or arm that continuously measure glucose levels. While these devices are medically essential, many users — especially children and teenagers — feel self-conscious about wearing them. The devices look clinical and can draw unwanted questions or stares. Meghan, who was diagnosed with Type 1 diabetes at age 4, experienced this firsthand and created ExpressionMed to solve the problem.

ExpressionMed's products are **thin, colorful, waterproof adhesive overlays** that stick over the CGM sensor, allowing users to express their personality while wearing their medical device. The patches come in hundreds of designs — from sports teams and florals to abstract art and holiday themes. They also serve a practical function: the adhesive helps the CGM stay attached to the skin longer, reducing the frequency of sensor reattachments.

As of **2024**, ExpressionMed:
- Serves **150,000+ customers** in the U.S. and 30+ countries
- Offers **500+ designs** across CGM patches, insulin pump skins, and medical device accessories
- Employs **12 full-time employees** and 8 part-time production staff
- Operates from a **4,500 sq ft production facility** in Chicago
- Revenue: **$3.2 million** (2024)
