## ANSWERS (teacher · students stop here)

### A (8)
**A1.** Revenue 288.73 (269.05) **+7.32%**; pretax profit 85.95 (75.13) **+14.41%**; profit attributable 70.38 (63.44) **+10.95%**.

**A2.** Gross profit 116.13 (2024: 99.48); gross margin **40.2%** (2024: **37.0%**, +3.25 pt).

**A3.** The 40% in condiments comes from **brand, recipe and distribution barriers** on inputs (soybean, sugar, salt, packaging) whose costs are high but predictable; the 40% in phones comes from technology, supply chain and ecosystem. Same number, different cost structure and durability.

### B (12)
**B1.** Revenue +19.68; cost of sales +3.03; **gross profit 116.13 (2024: 99.48), +16.65**; selling +3.04; administrative +1.20; R&D +0.68; **operating profit 86.64, +11.58**; pretax +10.82; tax +3.93; **net profit 70.45, +6.90**.

**B2.** Gross margin **40.2% / 37.0%**; operating margin **30.0% / 27.9%**; net margin **24.4% / 23.6%**; selling expense ratio **6.7% / 6.1%**.

**B3.** **Gross profit +16.65** (revenue +19.68 while cost of sales only +3.03) — partially eaten by **selling expenses +3.04, administrative +1.20 and income tax +3.93**. Net effect +6.90. The key is that gross profit grew (16.7%) faster than revenue (7.3%).

### C (10)
**C1.** **No numeric target.** The plan is qualitative: user satisfaction first, break into new growth through core categories, improve value-for-money and "emotional value per yuan", dig deeper into consumption occasions.

**C2.** **Yili** gives numbers (1,215 / 136); Haitian does not. Neither can be read as a forecast: Yili's can serve as a **base case**, Haitian's only as a **direction** — the revenue estimate has to be built independently from volume, price and trend.

**C3.** (a) **Sales forecast → procurement plan → cost of sales.** Get the order wrong and the cost is not controlled.
(b) **Supply chain (procurement)** and **planned expenses (a purchase budget built on a forecast)**.
(c) Because a raw-material price rise hits **cost of sales and therefore gross profit** first; net profit also passes through expenses and tax, so the chain is longer and the claim less certain. "Gross margin" is the honest, narrower statement.

### D (10)
**D1.** Not a total-dollar issue, a **ratio** issue: the selling expense ratio moved **6.1% → 6.7%** (+0.6 pt). Against a 3.25 pt gross-margin gain, that spend bought share and brand and is acceptable. The test: **is the gross-margin gain larger than the expense-ratio increase?** (3.25 pt > 0.6 pt — yes.)

**D2.** **Administrative** expenses are more likely to be lumpy (+20.7%): management pay, consulting and audit fees, share-based schemes and reorganisations jump in a single year. R&D tends to follow projects smoothly (+8.0%, close to revenue growth).

**D3.**
| Possible cause | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Input costs falling** (soybean, sugar, packaging, energy) | Cost of sales grew only 1.8% while revenue grew 7.3%; check the raw-material share in the cost breakdown |
| **Mix upgrade or price increases** | Revenue by product and its margins; if prices rose, volume growth should lag revenue growth |

### Common errors
- Using total operating costs as cost of sales (that line also includes taxes and all four expense categories)
- Calling any rise in selling expenses an efficiency problem (look at the ratio against the gross-margin change)
- Reading "no numeric target" as "no plan" (the plan here is a set of strategic actions)
- Forgetting that expenses and tax sit between gross margin and net margin
