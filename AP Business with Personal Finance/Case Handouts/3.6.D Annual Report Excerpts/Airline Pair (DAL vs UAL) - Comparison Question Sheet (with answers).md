## ANSWERS (teacher · students stop here)

### A (10)
**A1.**
| | DAL | UAL |
|---|---|---|
| Operating expense ÷ revenue | 90.8% | 92.0% |
| Operating margin | **9.2%** | **8.0%** |
| Net margin | **7.9%** | **5.7%** |
| Effective tax rate | **19.1%** | **22.1%** |

**A2.** Delta on both — but the gap *widens* at the net line (9.2% vs 8.0% operating; 7.9% vs 5.7% net) because Delta also pays less interest and books positive non-operating income while United's non-operating line is negative. Same winner, larger margin — the gap below operating profit is financing, not flying.

### B (12)
**B1.**
| | DAL | UAL |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel ÷ revenue | **15.5%** | **19.3%** |
| Fuel ÷ operating expense | **17.1%** | **21.0%** |

**B2.** United, by 3.8 percentage points of revenue (19.3% − 15.5%) — and in absolute terms United burned $11,396 of fuel against Delta's $9,819, $1,577 more on slightly less revenue.
**B3.** An airline's cost structure is *more* fixed than a restaurant's: salaries, aircraft ownership, landing fees and maintenance continue whether the seats are full or empty, while fuel and some handling costs fall with flying. In a demand collapse, revenue → 0 but most of the cost base stays — losses compound faster than in a restaurant, which can at least cut food purchases and hourly labour immediately.
**B4.** Both fuel bills fell (price effect) while revenue rose (volume effect): airlines grew *volume* while paying *less* per gallon. It shows the two levers of a variable cost separately — quantity × price — and why a falling fuel bill with rising revenue is a margin double-gift.

### C (8)
**C1.** United carries roughly twice the interest burden relative to revenue — consistent with a more leveraged balance sheet (or costlier debt) financing its fleet. Interest as a share of revenue is a quick proxy for how much of each ticket dollar is committed to lenders before owners see anything.
**C2.** Interest sits **below** operating profit: operating profit measures the business of flying (revenue − operating costs), and interest measures *how the aircraft were financed*. Mixing them in would make two airlines with identical operations look different because one borrowed and the other raised equity — which is exactly what 3.6.A.6–A.7 separates ("operating profit = net income before interest and taxes").

### D (10)
**D1.**
| | DAL | UAL |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue growth | **+2.8%** | **+3.5%** |
| Net income growth | **+44.8%** | **+6.5%** |

**D2.** The **non-operating line**, decisively: it swung from −1,337 (2024) to +363 (2025), a +1,700 swing — while operating income actually *fell* 173 (5,822 vs 5,995). Every dollar of Delta's +44.8% profit jump came from below the operating line (investment/other income and a smaller interest drag), not from flying more people. The tax line then trimmed it (1,180 vs 1,201).
**D3.** Answers vary — gains/losses on fuel hedges or investments, interest earned on cash, gains on asset sales, equity-method results. None of them is part of flying people: they are financing and investment outcomes, which is why the CED puts them outside operating income.

### One-line summary (for the board)
**Two airlines, same ladder, different exposures: read fuel as the variable cost, salaries as the fixed cost, and interest as the price of the fleet — then compare margins only after comparing those three ratios.**

### Common errors
- Comparing net margins without noticing one airline's non-operating line is positive and the other's is negative
- Treating a falling fuel bill as "cost cutting" — it is a price effect, not an efficiency gain
- Forgetting that interest sits *below* operating profit, so it cannot explain an operating-margin gap
- Ranking "better airline" on one year of margins — both are cyclical businesses
