## ANSWERS (teacher · students stop here)

### A (10)
**A1.** COGS is the direct cost of goods a business **produces** (3.4.B.3) and cost of sales is the direct cost of a **service** (3.4.B.4) — a bank's product is money itself, and the cost of that money (interest paid to depositors) is netted **inside revenue** as net interest income. There is no separate production step, so there is no COGS line.
**A2.**
| | JPM | BAC |
|---|---|---|
| Noninterest expense ÷ revenue | **52.4%** | **61.7%** |
| Net margin | **31.3%** | **27.0%** |

**A3.** JPM 14,212/182,447 = **7.8%**; BAC 5,675/113,097 = **5.0%**. "The product" is **credit** — loans the bank has made. The provision is the cost of the loans that will not be repaid: it rises with loan volume (variable, like COGS) and with deterioration in the loan book.

### B (12)
**B1.** At a normal company, interest is the price of **financing** the business — it has nothing to do with operations, so it sits below operating profit (3.6.A.6–A.7). At a bank, paying interest to depositors and creditors **is** the cost of acquiring the raw material (deposits) that the bank lends out. It is an operating cost of the business model, so it is netted against interest income inside revenue.
**B2.** revenue = net interest income + noninterest revenue. JPM: 95,443 + 87,004 = 182,447 — net interest income is slightly bigger (52.3%). BAC: 60,096 + 53,001 = 113,097 — net interest income is slightly bigger (53.1%). Both are close to an even split; JPM earns more from investment banking and markets, BAC from card and wealth fees.
| | NII share | Noninterest share |
|---|---|---|
| JPM | **52.3%** | **47.7%** |
| BAC | **53.1%** | **46.9%** |

**B3.** The claim does not survive. Absolute dollars ignore scale: JPM's revenue is 1.6× BAC's. As ratios, JPM's expense load is **52.4%** of revenue vs BAC's **61.7%** — JPM is the more efficient bank by 9.3 points. (This is close to the industry's "efficiency ratio"; accept that name if a student offers it.)

### C (12)
**C1.**
| | JPM | BAC |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue growth | **+2.8%** | **+6.8%** |
| Net income growth | **−2.4%** | **+13.1%** |

**C2.** (1) **Provision for credit losses**: JPM's rose 3,534 (10,678 → 14,212) while BAC's fell 146 — a 3,680 swing between them. (2) **Noninterest expense**: JPM's rose 3,843 (91,797 → 95,640) while BAC's rose 2,915 (66,812 → 69,727) on faster revenue growth. JPM grew revenue more slowly *and* spent more *and* provisioned more — hence the divergence.
**C3.** Because it is the bank's COGS-equivalent: it is where the loan book's quality shows up first, it swings harder than any other line, and it is management's biggest judgement call. Two banks with identical revenue and expense lines can post very different years purely on this line — which is exactly what happened to JPM vs BAC in 2025.

### D (6)
**D1.** The ladder breaks at the **first rung**: there is no "direct cost" to subtract, because the cost of money is already netted inside revenue. The missing rung is filled by *net interest income* (revenue after the cost of deposits) — or, if you keep gross interest income as revenue, by interest expense as the direct cost. Either construction is defensible; what is not defensible is forcing a gross-profit line onto a bank and pretending it means something.
**D2.** For a bank, interest expense is **operating**: it is the cost of the deposits the bank lends out — the core of the business model. (At a manufacturer it would be non-operating.) The CED's "interest is the only non-operating item" rule assumes a goods-producing business; reading a bank's statement is precisely the exercise in noticing when a rule's context changes.

### One-line summary (for the board)
**A bank's product is money: the cost of that money (interest paid) is netted inside revenue, the cost of its credit risk (provision for losses) is its COGS — so the textbook ladder has to be rebuilt, not just filled in.**

### Common errors
- Looking for a COGS line and concluding the statement is wrong
- Comparing absolute expenses across banks of different sizes (use ratios)
- Calling a bank's interest expense "non-operating" because a manufacturer's is
- Forgetting that provision for credit losses is a judgement call, not a fact
