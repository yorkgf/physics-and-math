# 3.9 Yardley — Ethics and Financial Reporting

> **Topic:** 3.9 Ethics and Financial Reporting
> **Company:** Yardley

---

## Background

**Yardley Manufacturing, Inc.** is a mid-sized manufacturer of office furniture (desks, filing cabinets, and ergonomic chairs) headquartered in Grand Rapids, Michigan. Founded in 1988 by **Richard Yardley**, the company has grown into a profitable business generating approximately **$48 million in annual revenue**, employing **210 people**, and selling products to office supply retailers, corporate buyers, and government agencies across the United States.

Yardley went public in 2005 and trades on the NASDAQ under the ticker **YARD**. As a publicly traded corporation, Yardley is required to file quarterly and annual financial reports with the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** and to have its financial statements audited by an independent accounting firm. The company's stock price is closely watched by investors, and executive compensation is tied to meeting quarterly earnings targets.

In early 2024, **Lisa Morales**, Yardley's Director of Finance, faces an ethical dilemma. The company is about to report Q4 2023 earnings, and the numbers are below the **earnings target** that analysts expect. Missing the target would likely cause Yardley's stock price to drop, reduce executive bonuses, and potentially trigger a review by the Board of Directors. Lisa's supervisor, **CFO David Park**, suggests "adjusting" the numbers to meet the target — specifically, by recording $1.2 million in revenue from a large order that has not yet been shipped to the customer.

Lisa knows that recording revenue before the product is shipped violates **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** and SEC regulations. But she also knows that the order is confirmed and the shipment will go out in early January — just a few weeks later. David tells her: "We're not lying — the sale is real. We're just moving it forward by a few weeks."

## The Ethical Dilemma — A Detailed View

### What Happened

| Step | Action | Date | Ethical Issue |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Corporate client places a $1.2M order for 400 ergonomic desks | Dec 5, 2023 | Order is real — no issue yet |
| 2 | Production is scheduled; desks will be ready to ship by Jan 8, 2024 | Dec 10, 2023 | Standard production timeline |
| 3 | CFO David Park tells Lisa: "Record the revenue now — the order is confirmed" | Dec 15, 2023 | **Request to violate GAAP revenue recognition rules** |
| 4 | Lisa records $1.2M in Q4 2023 revenue, increasing accounts receivable | Dec 20, 2023 | **Revenue recorded before product shipped — violates GAAP** |
| 5 | Yardley reports Q4 and FY2023 earnings that meet analyst targets | Jan 25, 2024 | **Financial statements are misleading** |
| 6 | Desks ship to the customer on January 12, 2024 | Jan 12, 2024 | Revenue should have been recognized here, in Q1 2024 |
| 7 | External auditor discovers the discrepancy during annual audit | Feb 2024 | **Fraud detected** |

### Why It Matters

The $1.2 million adjustment changed Yardley's financial picture:

| Metric | With Fraud (Reported) | Without Fraud (Actual) | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q4 2023 Revenue | $13.2M | $12.0M | +$1.2M |
| FY2023 Revenue | $48.5M | $47.3M | +$1.2M |
| FY2023 Net Income | $3.8M | $2.9M | +$0.9M |
| Earnings Per Share (EPS) | $1.52 | $1.16 | +$0.36 |
| Met analyst target? | ✅ Yes ($1.50) | ❌ No ($1.16) | — |

> **The fraud was not about the money — it was about the target.** Yardley did not invent a fake sale. The order was real. But by recording it in the wrong quarter, Lisa and David made the company appear more profitable than it actually was in FY2023, which directly affected stock price, executive bonuses, and investor decisions.

## 4. Evidence and Legal Framework

### Auditor's Evidence

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Discrepancy found** | $1.2M revenue entry traced to shipping records |
| **Actual ship date** | January 12, 2024 |
| **When revenue was recorded** | Q4 2023 (December 2023) |
| **Conclusion** | Revenue recorded in the wrong quarter — product was not shipped until Q1 2024 |

### Laws Violated

| Law | Provision | Penalties |
|---|---|---|
| **Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (Rule 10b-5)** | Fraud and misrepresentation in connection with buying or selling securities | Civil fines up to $5 million; criminal penalties up to 20 years imprisonment |
| **Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX)** | Falsifying financial records; CEO/CFO must personally certify financial statements | Criminal penalties for fraud |

### CPA Ethics Standards Violated by Lisa Morales

| Standard | How Violated |
|---|---|
| **Honesty** | Recorded revenue in the wrong period, creating a false financial picture |
| **Integrity** | Complied with David's request instead of refusing and reporting the issue |
| **Transparency** | Did not disclose to the Board or auditor that the $1.2M was recorded before shipment |
| **Objectivity** | Pressure to meet earnings targets compromised her professional judgment |

### Missing Internal Controls

| Control | Status | How It Would Have Helped |
|---|---|---|
| **Segregation of duties** | Missing — Lisa could both enter and approve journal entries | A second person would have reviewed the $1.2M entry and caught the discrepancy |
| **Revenue recognition matching** | Missing — no process to match revenue entries to shipping records before closing the books | System would have flagged the revenue entry with no corresponding shipping record |
| **Whistleblower hotline** | Missing — no anonymous reporting mechanism for employees | Lisa could have reported David's request without confronting him directly |

### Key Financial Data

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| **David Park's bonus** | $45,000 for "meeting financial targets" |

## Questions

**1. Identifying Unethical Practices**
Review the six types of unethical financial practices listed, identify which specific type(s) of unethical practice occurred at Yardley. David Park argued: "We're not lying — the sale is real. We're just moving it forward by a few weeks." Explain why this reasoning does not make the action ethical or legal.

**2. The Fraud Triangle**
Access to large sums of cash creates situations where individuals may seek to falsify information for personal gain, apply the **fraud triangle** (pressure, opportunity, rationalization) to Lisa Morales's situation. (a) What specific **pressure** did Lisa face? (b) What specific **opportunity** did her role provide? (c) What **rationalization** did David offer, and why is it flawed?

**3. Financial Incentives for Fraud**
Businesses or financial managers may influence stock prices, secure better loan terms, or avoid taxes by portraying financial situations as better or worse than reality, answer: (a) Who specifically benefited from Yardley's fraudulent earnings report? (b) If Yardley had **underreported** revenue instead of overreporting it, who might benefit and why? Give a specific example of when a business might want to appear **less** profitable than it actually is.

**4. Ethics of "Timing Adjustments"**
CFO David Park told Lisa: "The order is confirmed — the sale is real. We're just recording it a few weeks early." Many people would consider this a minor ethical issue compared to inventing a fake sale., explain why recording revenue in the wrong period is a form of **fraud**, not just a "timing adjustment." What specific harm does this cause to investors who relied on Yardley's Q4 2023 earnings report?

**5. Legal Consequences**
Misusing funds, tax evasion, embezzlement, bribery, and fraud are illegal, identify which specific U.S. laws Lisa and David violated. If the SEC investigates, what are the potential penalties for each person? Does it matter that Lisa was following David's instructions — or is she equally responsible?

**6. The Role of Auditing**
Publicly held corporations are required to submit financial records annually for auditing by independent firms, explain: (a) What specific evidence did the external auditor find that proved the revenue was recorded in the wrong period? (b) Why is it important that the auditing firm is **independent** — i.e., not employed directly by Yardley? (c) What would have happened to investors if Yardley were a private company with no external audit requirement?

**7. Professional Ethics**
Lisa Morales is a CPA bound by professional ethics codes emphasizing honesty, integrity, transparency, and objectivity., answer: (a) Which specific ethical standards did Lisa violate? (b) What should Lisa have done when David asked her to record the revenue in Q4? (c) What professional consequences could Lisa face beyond any legal penalties — such as loss of her CPA license?

**8. Internal Controls**
Businesses develop internal mechanisms such as codes of conduct, audit requirements, and cash-handling processes to prevent unethical behavior, answer: (a) Yardley had a code of conduct and an annual audit — but the fraud still occurred. Why were these controls insufficient? (b) Identify two specific internal controls that Yardley was **missing** and explain how each would have prevented or immediately caught the fraud. (c) Design a single policy change that would make it significantly harder for a CFO to pressure a finance director into falsifying revenue.

**9. The Whistleblower's Dilemma**
Imagine you are Lisa Morales in December 2023. David Park has asked you to record the $1.2 million in Q4 revenue. You know this is wrong. Access to financial systems creates opportunity for fraud; professional ethics codes emphasize honesty and integrity, describe: (a) What are your options? (b) What are the risks of each option — including the risk of refusing? (c) What internal mechanism would make it safer for you to report David's request without fear of retaliation?
