# 2.5 Costco (开市客) — Case Handout

**Topic:** 2.5 Price

---

## Background

Costco Wholesale Corporation is the world's third-largest retailer, operating over 890 warehouse clubs across 14 countries. Founded in 1983 in Seattle by **Jim Sinegal** and **Jeff Brotman**, Costco pioneered the membership warehouse model — customers pay an annual fee for the privilege of shopping at Costco's no-frills warehouses, where they find a limited selection of products at extremely low prices.

Costco's business model is radically different from traditional retailers. While most stores aim to maximize profit on each product sold, Costco **caps its markup at 14%** on most items and **15% on Kirkland Signature** (its private label). This means Costco intentionally limits how much money it makes on products — and instead generates almost all of its profit from **membership fees**.

As of 2025, Costco has over **135 million cardholders** worldwide, with a **membership renewal rate above 90%**. The company generates approximately **$4.8 billion annually** from membership fees alone — a figure that represents nearly all of its operating profit.

## 3. Costco's Loss-Leader Strategy — Key Facts

- Rotisserie chicken is priced at **$4.99** — this price has been in place since **2009** and has never been raised.
- The chicken is placed at the **very back of the store** — shoppers must walk through the entire warehouse to reach it, passing hundreds of products along the way.
- In 2019, Costco built a **$450 million poultry processing plant** in Fremont, Nebraska to control costs and protect the $4.99 price point.
- The Fremont plant ships **1 million chickens per week**.
- Costco loses an estimated **$30–40 million per year** selling rotisserie chicken at $4.99 — intentionally, to drive store traffic.
- Costco's **gross margin on products is approximately 12%**, compared to Walmart's approximately 24%. Costco compensates for lower margins through high sales volume and membership fees, which generate nearly all of its operating profit.
- Costco **membership fees vary by country**: $65/year in the US, £33.60/year in the UK, ¥299/year in China.

## Questions

**1. Loss-Leader Logic**
Costco loses $30–40 million per year on rotisserie chicken. Why doesn't Costco just raise the price of chicken to $7 or $8? What would be the consequences?

**2. Membership as the Real Product**
Costco's gross margin on products is ~12%, while Walmart's is ~24%. How does Costco make money with half the product margin?

**3. The 14% Cap**
Costco caps its markup at 14%. Most retailers mark up 25–50%. Why doesn't Costco raise its markup to 20% and make more profit on each sale?

**4. Pricing Power Comparison**
Xiaomi and Costco both sacrifice short-term profit for long-term gain. But their sources of pricing power are different. Compare how each company creates pricing power.

**5. Pricing Strategy Identification**
A new student says: "Costco uses penetration pricing because it sells products at low prices." Is this correct? Explain the difference between Costco's strategy and penetration pricing.

**6. Legal Constraints**
Costco charges $65 for a membership in the US but charges different membership fees in different countries (e.g., £33.60 in the UK, ¥299 in China). Is this price discrimination? Is it legal?
