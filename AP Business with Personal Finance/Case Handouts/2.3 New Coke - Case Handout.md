# 2.3 New Coke — Market Research

> **Topic:** 2.3 Market Research
> **Company:** Coca-Cola

---

## Background

In the 1980s, **Coca-Cola** was losing the "Cola Wars" to Pepsi. After World War II, Coca-Cola had owned **60% of the cola market**; by 1983, that share had fallen to **less than 24%**. Pepsi intensified the pressure by launching the **"Pepsi Challenge"** — blindfolded taste tests in which people overwhelmingly chose Pepsi over Coca-Cola. The public results were humiliating for a brand that had dominated the cola category for nearly a century.

Coca-Cola CEO **Roberto Goizueta** led the company's response. Born in Cuba and trained as a chemical engineer, Goizueta became president of Coca-Cola in 1979. Earlier in his career, he had successfully tweaked Coke's flavor for the Bahamas market — a past success that later created **confirmation bias** when he championed a much larger reformulation. Under his leadership, Coca-Cola launched **"Project Kansas"** — a secret plan to create a sweeter version of classic Coca-Cola that could defeat Pepsi in head-to-head taste tests.

---

## Key Events

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **1983** | Coca-Cola's market share falls below 24%, down from 60% after WWII. Pepsi's "Pepsi Challenge" campaign gains national attention |
| **1983–1985** | Project Kansas begins — Coca-Cola secretly develops a sweeter reformulation of classic Coca-Cola |
| **1984** | Coca-Cola spends approximately **$12 million** (in today's dollars) conducting market research on New Coke, enrolling **200,000 participants** in taste tests and focus groups. The taste test methodology gave participants only a small sip of each product — not a full can. This is significant because sip tests favor sweeter drinks (New Coke was sweeter), while drinking a full can favors less sweet, more familiar flavors (original Coca-Cola) |
| **April 23, 1985 — "Black Tuesday"** | Coca-Cola announces that New Coke will replace classic Coca-Cola. Pepsi had prepped **200+ reporters** with tough questions before the press conference. Media immediately frames it as a "massive misstep" |
| **April–July 1985** | Coca-Cola's Atlanta headquarters receives **8,000+ angry calls per day**. Demonstrations and letter-writing campaigns erupt across the country |
| **July 1985 — 3 months later** | Coca-Cola brings back the original formula as **"Coca-Cola Classic"** |

## Questions

**Q1.** Coca-Cola collected both quantitative data (200,000 taste tests) and qualitative data (focus groups) before launching New Coke. Businesses collect quantitative and qualitative data, explain how each type of data was collected in this case. Why was collecting both types insufficient to predict the outcome? What was the critical variable that neither data type measured?

**Q2.** Pepsi's market share growth (Coca-Cola declining from 60% to less than 24%) and the Pepsi Challenge results were both secondary data that Coca-Cola used to inform its strategy. Businesses interpret secondary data to assess opportunities and threats, explain how Coca-Cola interpreted these two secondary data sources. How did action bias shape the interpretation, and what alternative explanation for the market share decline did Coca-Cola fail to consider?

**Q3.** Coca-Cola's hypothesis was: "Consumers prefer a sweeter cola; therefore, they will accept New Coke replacing Classic." A hypothesis is a testable statement, evaluate whether this hypothesis was properly formulated. What was missing from the hypothesis, and how did the incomplete hypothesis lead to a flawed research design?

**Q4.** The taste test gave participants a small sip of New Coke, not a full can. Sample design must reflect real-world conditions, explain why this sample design was flawed. How might the results have been different if participants had been given a full can to drink in a real-life setting (e.g., with a meal at home)? What does this case teach about the difference between a "taste test" and actual consumption?

**Q5.** Goizueta "believed that New Coke had to work so he ignored information that might have contradicted that belief." Confirmation bias can distort research interpretation, explain how confirmation bias affected this research. Identify two specific ways a neutral researcher — one without Goizueta's personal investment in the outcome — might have designed the research differently to avoid this bias.

**Q6.** Coca-Cola's taste test results could be displayed as a bar chart showing New Coke beating both Pepsi and Coke Classic. Data visualization helps communicate research findings, explain why this bar chart would be technically accurate but strategically misleading. What additional data — and what type of visualization — would have given Coca-Cola a more complete picture before the launch?
