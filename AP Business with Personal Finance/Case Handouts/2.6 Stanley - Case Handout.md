# 2.6 Stanley — Case Handout

**Topic:** 2.6 Place and Channels

---

## Background

Stanley (officially Stanley 1913) is an American brand that has manufactured insulated drinkware and food storage products since **1913**, when inventor **William Stanley Jr.** patented the first all-steel vacuum bottle. For over a century, Stanley was known for its rugged, durable thermoses used by construction workers, military personnel, and outdoor enthusiasts.

By 2019, Stanley's annual revenue was approximately **$70 million** — respectable but stagnant. The brand had a loyal but narrow customer base: men who valued durability over style. Then, in the span of just four years, Stanley underwent one of the most dramatic channel transformations in consumer brand history — growing from $70 million to approximately **$750 million** in annual revenue by 2023.

The catalyst was not a new product. It was a **new channel strategy** — selling the same type of product through different places, to a completely different customer.

## Key Events

| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
| 1913 | William Stanley Jr. patents the first all-steel vacuum bottle; Stanley brand founded |
| 1913–2015 | Stanley builds a reputation as a rugged, utilitarian brand for construction workers, military, and outdoorsmen |
| ~2016 | Stanley launches the **Quencher** tumbler — a large, cup-holder-friendly insulated tumbler. It does not sell well in hardware stores. |
| 2019 | **The Buy Guide** (Ashlee LeSueur, Taylor Colange, Linley Hutchinson) — a lifestyle blog run by three women — discovers the Quencher and loves it |
| 2019 | The Buy Guide purchases **5,000 Quenchers** and resells them on their blog; sells out in hours |
| 2019 | The Buy Guide writes to Stanley urging them to keep making the Quencher; Stanley had been planning to **discontinue** it due to poor sales through existing channels |
| 2020 | Stanley hires **Terence Reilly** as president; Reilly previously transformed **Crocs** from an "ugly shoe" into a fashion brand |
| 2020–2021 | Reilly identifies the Quencher's untapped potential among women; begins channel transformation |
| 2021 | Stanley launches colorful Quencher editions; begins selling through **Target** — a retailer whose core shoppers match the new target customer |
| 2021 | Stanley builds its **DTC (direct-to-consumer)** website (stanley1913.com) for exclusive drops and full brand control |
| 2022 | Stanley uses **influencer marketing** on TikTok and Instagram; Quencher becomes a social media phenomenon |
| 2023 | Revenue reaches approximately **$750 million** — a 10x increase from 2019 |
| Nov 2023 | A woman's car catches fire; her Stanley Quencher survives with ice still inside. She posts a **TikTok video** that goes mega-viral. Stanley's president responds personally and offers to buy her a new car. |
| Dec 2023 | **Starbucks x Stanley** limited-edition red Quencher launched exclusively at Target; sells out in minutes; resale prices reach $300+ |
| 2024 | Stanley faces **market saturation** — too many colors, too many drops, resale market frustration, criticism of artificial scarcity |
| 2024 | Stanley launches in international markets including China, expanding its channel strategy globally |

---

## 3. Target Partnership — Key Facts

- Target's core shoppers are women aged 18–40 — exactly the Quencher's new target customer
- Target is known for trendy, design-forward, affordable products — aligns with Quencher's appeal
- Target's in-store displays and marketing aligned with Stanley's new brand image
- Hardware stores (traditional Stanley channel) cater to a male, construction-oriented demographic

## Questions

**1. Channel Type Identification**
Before 2019, Stanley sold Quenchers through hardware stores like Home Depot. After 2020, Stanley sold through Target and its own website. Classify each channel as direct or indirect, and explain how the channel type affected Stanley's control over the customer experience.

**2. Why Hardware Stores Failed**
Stanley's Quencher did not sell well in hardware stores. Using the channel selection factors (cost, profitability, customer experience, reach), explain why this channel was a poor fit for the Quencher.

**3. Why Target Worked**
Stanley chose Target as its primary retail partner. What specific factors made Target a better channel for the Quencher than hardware stores?

**4. Direct vs. Indirect Trade-off**
Stanley uses both Target (indirect) and stanley1913.com (direct). Why doesn't Stanley sell ONLY through its own website? What would it lose?

**5. The Buy Guide as an Unofficial Channel**
The Buy Guide bought 5,000 Quenchers from Stanley and resold them on their blog. Was The Buy Guide acting as a direct channel, an indirect channel, or something else? What does this story reveal about channel selection?

**6. When Channels Go Too Far**
By 2024, Stanley faced criticism for too many limited-edition drops, reseller markups, and artificial scarcity. How can a channel strategy become too aggressive? What is the risk to the brand?
