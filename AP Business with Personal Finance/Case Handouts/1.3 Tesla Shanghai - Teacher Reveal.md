# Tesla's China Decision — Teacher Reveal & Answer Key

> **TEACHER ONLY — do not distribute.** Students must commit to a recommendation before this is revealed.

---

## How to run (~35–40 min suggested)

1. **Pre-reading:** students read the handout the day before.
2. **Individual (10–12 min):** Question 1 — complete the PESTEL table.
3. **Groups of 3–4 (12 min):** Questions 4–5 — rank the factors and choose A/B/C/D. Each group writes its choice on a sticky note / shared doc **before** hearing other groups.
4. **Whole class (10 min):** groups state choices and their single decisive factor.
5. **Reveal:** read "What actually happened" below → debrief questions.

> The mechanism that makes this case work: **commitment before hindsight.** Never reveal the outcome while groups are still deciding.

## Answer key — the PESTEL table (Q1)

| Factor | Key facts from the brief | Read for Tesla |
|---|---|---|
| **Political** | NEV ownership cap removed (Apr 2018); trade war → 40% tariff on U.S. cars; subsidies & purchase-tax break for locally made NEVs; Shanghai land/loans offer; policy windows can close | **Two-edged:** the tariff punishes exports (risk); the reform + subsidies + Shanghai offer pull Tesla in (opportunity) |
| **Economic** | World's largest car market (28M vehicles) and largest EV market (777k NEVs in 2017); rising urban middle class; lower wages and dense supplier cluster; $2B+ plant cost while Tesla burns ~$1B/quarter; freight + duty costs on imports; FX/trade uncertainty | **Two-edged:** demand and cost savings are opportunities; capital intensity and cash position are risks |
| **Social** | Free green plates vs. lottery/auction plates; smog-driven public support for EVs; Tesla as status symbol; young urban buyers expecting apps, big screens, OTA updates | **Opportunity** — demand side is strongly favorable; Tesla's brand and software fit the buyer profile |
| **Technological** | ~300k public chargers (~half the world's total); CATL battery supply chain; super-app (WeChat/Alipay) integration expectations; HD-mapping constraints on self-driving | **Two-edged:** infrastructure enables; mapping/data limits constrain FSD specifically |
| **Environmental** | Smog → pro-EV policy; dual-credit mandate forcing every automaker to sell EVs; battery traceability/recycling rules; grid still ~2/3 coal | **Opportunity** for market creation; **minor risk** (compliance cost, green-positioning caveat) |
| **Legal** | 50% JV rule (being lifted); weak IP enforcement; mapping licensing; tightening vehicle-data rules; catalog/subsidy eligibility rules | **Two-edged:** ownership liberalization opens the door; IP and data rules are long-term exposure |

## Classification traps to press (Q1C, Q2A)

- **The 40% tariff = Political.** Money moves, but the *source* is a government policy decision in a trade dispute — a tariff is a policy tool (like a tax), not the condition of the economy. Inflation, income, and exchange rates would be economic.
- **Free green plates = Political (with social/environmental drivers).** Smog (environmental) and public demand (social) explain *why* the policy exists; the force acting on the market is the policy itself. Classify by source.
- **The ownership-cap reform = Political/Legal overlap.** The 50% cap sitting on the books is a legal rule; the April 2018 decision to remove it is a political choice. Best answer: the *change* hitting the market comes from a government decision → political, operating through legal change.
- **Charging infrastructure = Technological** (availability of infrastructure); **smog red alerts = Environmental** (a condition), whose effects then flow through social and political channels.
- **Q4C ("all six equally important" = wrong):** here the decisive weights are Political (tariff timing + reform window) and the plate-driven demand engine (Social effect, Political source). Environmental is the market's origin story but Tesla doesn't depend on it day-to-day the way a solar installer would.

## What actually happened — THE REVEAL

- **July 10, 2018:** Tesla signed an agreement with the Shanghai government to build **Gigafactory Shanghai — 100% owned by Tesla**, the first wholly foreign-owned car plant in modern China. (Option **C**.)
- **October 2018:** Tesla secured an ~86-hectare site in Lingang at a favorable price. In 2019, four major Chinese banks (China Construction Bank, Agricultural Bank, ICBC, Shanghai Pudong Development Bank) lent roughly **US$1.6 billion** for construction, with Shanghai authorities' backing.
- **January 7, 2019:** groundbreaking. **December 30, 2019:** first China-made Model 3s delivered — **under 12 months** from dirt to cars, a speed no global automaker had achieved in China.
- The locally built Model 3 started around **RMB 320,000 after subsidies** — roughly 30–40% below the imported version — and qualified for green plates immediately.
- **2021:** Shanghai produced about **484,000 cars — over half of Tesla's global output** — and became Tesla's export hub for Europe, Japan, and Australia.
- **But the flagged risks materialized too:** in **2021**, amid China's data-security crackdown, Teslas were barred from military complexes and some government sites over their cameras and sensors; Tesla committed to storing all China data locally (a Shanghai data center); its most advanced self-driving software (FSD) remained **unapproved in China for years** — the mapping/data rules from the fact pack, exactly as students could have predicted.
- The local supply chain Shanghai built up also strengthened rivals: **BYD (比亚迪) overtook Tesla as China's top NEV seller**, and Tesla was drawn into repeated price wars from 2023 onward.
- On the "window" debate (Option D): the liberalization did generalize later — BMW and Volkswagen secured majority-owned Chinese operations by 2022 — but Tesla's 2018 first-mover position let it lock in land, loans, supplier relationships, and brand momentum on terms no later entrant matched.
- On Option A: China briefly paused the extra auto tariffs in late 2018 — but the structural disadvantages remained (no subsidies, no Shanghai green-plate access for imports, no local cost base).

**Bottom line for students:** the real decision was **C** — arguably the most successful factory bet in modern auto history, *and* the IP/data risks identified in a careful PESTEL came true on schedule. The framework didn't predict the future perfectly; it forced the right risks onto the table.

## Debrief questions

1. Poll the room: A / B / C / D. Which single fact flipped your group's choice?
2. Groups that chose **B**: note that *no* global automaker was offered the 100% deal — the wholly-owned route existed only for Tesla, only in that window. Does that change the JV trade-off?
3. Groups that chose **A** or **D**: the tariff did pause briefly in late 2018 — but which disadvantages were structural, not cyclical? (Subsidy/catalog access, green plates, cost base.)
4. The same **legal** factor category that *enabled* entry (ownership liberalization) later *constrained* the product (data/mapping rules → FSD delayed for years). How does this connect to "PESTEL factors move"?
5. Which Q6C "trigger signals" would have been the right ones to watch? (Data-security legislation, NEV-credit and subsidy rule changes, local-rival market share, tariff escalation.)
6. Viability check: did the six factors decide *whether* Tesla could survive in China, or *how* it had to operate there? (Both — entry was viable **only** through local production.)
