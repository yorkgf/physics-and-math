## ANSWERS (teacher · students stop here)

### A (12)
**A1.**

| | WMT 26 | WMT 25 | TGT 26 | TGT 25 | DAL 25 | DAL 24 | UAL 25 | UAL 24 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Working capital | **−22,595** | −17,126 | **−1,225** | −1,345 | **−16,656** | −16,826 | **−9,276** | −4,431 |
| Current ratio | **0.79** | 0.82 | **0.94** | 0.94 | **0.40** | 0.37 | **0.65** | 0.81 |

**A2.** **Zero of eight.** Every one of these large, solvent companies fails the textbook test in at least one year — which is the point of Part B.

**A3.** Working capital = **current assets − current liabilities**; the CED's phrase is "**sufficient working capital to fund day-to-day operations**" (current assets meet or exceed current liabilities).

### B (10)
**B1.** **No.** 3.7.B.3 describes businesses that *cannot access sufficient current assets to fund operations* — a liquidity crisis. Retailers and airlines run negative working capital **by design**: Walmart sells inventory for cash (or near-cash card sales) *before* paying its suppliers, so payables (63,061) legitimately exceed current assets; airlines collect ticket cash (Delta's air traffic liability 7,157; United's advance ticket sales 8,131) *before* flying the customer. The cash arrives continuously through operations; the balance sheet date simply catches the pipeline full. The test matters for a business whose inventory sits and whose customers pay slowly.

**B2.** **Walmart, by ratio** — current ratio 0.79 vs Target's 0.94. (The dollar gap overstates it: Walmart's current liabilities are five times Target's, but its scale and supplier terms are also five times Target's. The honest answer names the ratio, not the dollars — "comparable" means relative.)

**B3.** Delta must fund next year's obligations (27,624 due within twelve months) largely from **cash the business will collect during the year** — ticket sales on flights not yet flown, plus refinancing or repaying 1,605 of current debt — rather than from assets already on the books. That is normal for an airline, but it means a demand collapse hits liquidity immediately.

### C (10)
**C1.**

| | WMT 26 | TGT 26 | DAL 25 | UAL 25 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total liabilities (assets − equity) | 178,781 | 43,325 | 60,464 | 61,166 |
| Liabilities ÷ assets | **62.8%** | **72.8%** | **74.4%** | **80.0%** |

**C2.** Retail: **Target is more leveraged** (72.8% vs 62.8%). Airlines: **United is more leveraged** (80.0% vs 74.4%). A lender reads this as "United must earn more from each revenue dollar to service its claims, and has less room to absorb a shock before equity is wiped out" — the same reading for Target within its pair. Note both airline ratios are *above* both retailer ratios: fleets are financed with debt, stores are financed more with supplier credit and equity.

**C3.** Because different industries carry different *normal* leverage — fleets are debt-financed long-lived assets, retail inventory is financed by suppliers — so cross-industry ratios compare business models, not management quality. 3.7.B.2 says "comparable to **similar** businesses": Walmart's true benchmark is Target, Delta's is United.

### D (8)
**D1.** Can we fund operations → **managers/owners**. Equity cushion and repayment → **lenders**. Is the business worth the price → **investors**.

**D2.** Internal: managers and owners. External: lenders and investors. Typical first reads: managers → current assets and working capital; lenders → total liabilities and the current-liability block; investors → owners' equity and its trend.

**D3.** Because the equation must hold: assets grew 23,845 while equity grew 8,466, so **liabilities did the rest** — Walmart's total liabilities rose 178,781 − 163,402 = 15,379 (payables, short-term borrowings and debt all grew). Assets growing faster than equity means the growth was part-funded by lenders.

### Common errors
- Calling negative working capital "insolvency" — the retailers and airlines are the counter-examples
- Comparing dollar working capital across companies of different size instead of the ratio
- Building total liabilities as "current + long-term" from memory instead of assets − equity (the statement's own mezzanine lines, e.g. Walmart's redeemable noncontrolling interest, sit between the two)
- Forgetting that "comparable debt" means *within* an industry
