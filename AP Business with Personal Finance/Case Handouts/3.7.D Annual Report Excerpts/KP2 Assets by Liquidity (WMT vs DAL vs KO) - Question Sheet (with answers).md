## ANSWERS (teacher · students stop here)

### A (10)
**A1.**

| Line | C / L / I | Flag? |
|---|---|---|
| Inventories (Walmart) | **C** | must be *sold* then collected — least liquid current asset |
| Operating lease right-of-use assets | **L** | a right to use property for years — not sellable for cash |
| Goodwill | **I** (printed inside long-term assets) | non-physical, but sits in the long-term block on all three statements |
| Trademarks with indefinite lives | **I** | "indefinite lives" = not amortised; still an intangible |
| Equity method investments (Coca-Cola) | **L** (long-term investments) | held beyond a year; a stake in other companies, not inventory |

> Accept either label for goodwill/lease assets **if the student says where it is printed** — the teaching point is that "intangible" describes the *nature* of the asset while the statement files it in the long-term block.

**A2.** Liquidity = **the ease with which an asset can be converted into cash** (3.7.A.2.i). **Current assets are always first** because they fund day-to-day operations — the ordering is a liquidity ladder, most liquid at the top.

**A3.** An **asset** (accounts receivable). The arrow points *toward* the business: customers owe Walmart. Money Walmart owes *suppliers* is accounts payable — a liability. Direction of the arrow decides the side.

### B (12)
**B1.**

| | Walmart | Delta | Coca-Cola |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current ÷ total | **29.8%** | **13.5%** | **29.6%** |
| PP&E ÷ total | **47.8%** | **48.9%** | **9.2%** |
| Intangibles ÷ total | **10.1%** | **19.3%** | **26.7%** |

(Intangibles = Walmart: 28,735; Delta: 9,753 + 5,966 = 15,719; Coca-Cola: 15,491 + 12,531 = 28,022. Coca-Cola's "prepaid + held for sale" is left inside current assets, as printed.)

**B2.** Walmart = **retailer** — a fifth of assets in inventory waiting to be sold, heavy stores. Delta = **capital-intensive operator** — half the balance sheet is aircraft and equipment it operates for years. Coca-Cola = **brand business** — over a quarter of assets are intangible (brands and goodwill from acquisitions), very little plant.

**B3.** Liquidity. Aircraft are *held to operate*, not held for sale: converting them to cash means selling the fleet — months of work at distressed prices, and the airline would lose the ability to fly. Current assets are the pool that funds operations; PP&E is the tool the operations run on.

### C (8)
**C1.** Cash → accounts receivable → inventories → aircraft and equipment → trademarks. (Cash is already cash; receivables are collected in weeks; inventory must be sold *then* collected; equipment must find a buyer; a trademark is valuable but not sellable in a pinch — it represents *future* revenue, not present cash.)

**C2.** Because Friday's wages are paid from **current assets**, not from the grand total. A company with 81,317 of assets can still miss payroll if only 10,968 is current — the *order* of the list is the warning system.

**C3.** The **current assets** block — the first four lines of Walmart's statement. She is answering 3.7.B.2's second question: *does the business have sufficient working capital to fund day-to-day operations?*

### D (10)
**D1.** Accounting records only what a company **paid for**: McDonald's built its brand internally over decades, and internally generated brands are never capitalised — the marketing spend went through the income statement as it was incurred.

**D2.** They were **bought** (acquired brands), not built — purchased intangibles are recorded at their cost, which is why goodwill exists too (the premium paid over the acquired net assets).

**D3.** Coca-Cola's — but only because of *how* the assets were acquired, not because its brand is more valuable. The general lesson: **a balance sheet records transactions, not market value**. McDonald's brand may be worth more than Coca-Cola's trademarks and still print nothing.

**D4.** Any example where the name commands a premium or repeat purchase: customers pay $5 instead of $3 for a branded cup, choose the familiar chain in a new city, or buy the branded bag of beans at a premium — revenue that exists *because* the trademark is recognised and protected.

### Common errors
- Calling goodwill a "current asset" because it appears early in some statements — check the block headers
- Treating lease right-of-use assets as inventory-like (they are a multi-year right, not goods for sale)
- Saying McDonald's brand "isn't valuable" because it isn't on the balance sheet — the statement records cost, not worth
- Ranking inventory above receivables (inventory must be sold *and* collected — two steps, not one)
