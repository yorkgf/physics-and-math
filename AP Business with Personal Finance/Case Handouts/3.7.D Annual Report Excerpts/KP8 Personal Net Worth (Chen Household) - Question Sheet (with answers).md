# KP8 — From business to household: personal net worth (with answers)

**Pairs with:** `3.7 DK Coffee Lab - Case Handout.md` (Daniel's personal net worth statement) — and any of the balance sheet PDFs for the business-equity link.
**Time:** 25 min　**Marks:** 35

> **Why there is no 10-K here:** the SEC requires **public companies** to file a Form 10-K. A household files nothing with anyone — so this knowledge point is taught with a household statement built the same way: assets minus liabilities. The *formula* is identical to owners' equity; only the unit changes.

**Given data — the Chen household, December 31, 2025**

Two earners, applying jointly for a mortgage.

| Assets | | Liabilities | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Savings and investments | 48,000 | Mortgage | 295,000 |
| Retirement accounts | 96,000 | Car loan | 9,500 |
| Home (market value) | 420,000 | Student loans | 38,000 |
| Car (market value) | 18,000 | Credit card balances | 6,500 |
| Personal possessions | 22,000 | | |
| **100% ownership of a coffee shop (book equity)** | **85,000** | | |

---

## A. Build the statement (8 marks)

**A1.** Compute total assets, total liabilities, and the household's **net worth**. (3)

**A2.** 3.7.C.1 says personal net worth is calculated for a **household**, which "may include more than one individual." Whose balances appear in the table above — one earner's or both? How do you know? (2)

**A3.** Which two lines would disappear if the household followed the *business* balance-sheet vocabulary and dropped "personal possessions"? Why does the CED keep them? (3)

---

## B. The business line (8 marks)

**B1.** The 85,000 coffee-shop line is an **asset** of the household. Where does that number come from — write it as a formula. (2)

**B2.** Why is the coffee shop's *gross* equipment value not listed as a household asset? (2)

**B3.** Daniel Kim (DK Coffee Lab) reports business equity of **157,675** on his personal net worth statement, against total personal assets of 338,950. What percentage of Daniel's net worth is his business? (2)

**B4.** If the coffee shop takes out a new 20,000 loan and holds the cash, what happens to the Chen household's net worth — and why? (2)

---

## C. Why lenders ask (9 marks)

**C1.** 3.7.C.2 says lenders may require household net worth during a loan application. What does the Chen net worth figure measure that an income statement cannot? (3)

**C2.** The Chens' net worth is positive but 61% of their assets are the family home (420,000 of 689,000). Give one reason a lender still cares about the composition, not just the total. (3)

**C3.** A second household has the same income but **negative** net worth of −40,000. In one sentence, what has the lender learned? (3)

---

## D. Why retirement planning asks (10 marks)

Two households, **same gross assets of 620,000**, same income:

| | Household A | Household B |
|---|---|---|
| Assets | home 500,000 · savings 120,000 | savings 560,000 · home 60,000 |
| Liabilities | mortgage 410,000 | 10,000 |
| **Net worth** | **210,000** | **610,000** |

**D1.** Compute both net worths and state which household is better prepared for retirement. (3)

**D2.** 3.7.C.3 says planners use net worth to judge "sufficient savings to retire." Why is gross assets the wrong test? Answer using Household A's numbers. (3)

**D3.** Which line on Household A's statement will shrink slowly over a 20-year retirement without any new transaction — and why does that matter? (2)

**D4.** Name the two stakeholders (besides the household itself) that this lesson says use a net worth statement, and what each uses it for. (2)

## ANSWERS (teacher · students stop here)

### A (8)
**A1.** Assets = 48,000 + 96,000 + 420,000 + 18,000 + 22,000 + 85,000 = **689,000**. Liabilities = 295,000 + 9,500 + 38,000 + 6,500 = **349,000**. Net worth = 689,000 − 349,000 = **340,000**.

**A2.** **Both** — the household is the unit: two earners' savings, retirement accounts and possessions are pooled on the asset side, and both spouses' loans and cards on the liability side. "Two people, one number." A per-person calculation would double-count shared assets and misstate the mortgage.

**A3.** The **personal possessions** line (22,000) — and nothing else; every other line has a business twin. The CED keeps it because 3.7.C.1 names "savings and investments, property, and personal possessions" explicitly: a household's furniture, electronics and belongings have resale value and are part of what could be sold to repay a debt. (If the student also flags the car, accept it — the lesson's example list treats vehicles as property.)

### B (8)
**B1.** **Business equity = business assets − business liabilities** — the same formula as owners' equity. The household owns 100% of the coffee shop, so that residual value is a household asset.

**B2.** Because the equipment is partly **claimed by the shop's own lenders**: what the household owns is the residual after the shop's debts, which is exactly what the equity formula computes. Listing gross equipment would count money the business owes to someone else.

**B3.** Net worth first: 338,950 − 18,175 = **320,775**; 157,675 / 320,775 = **49.1%**. Nearly half of Daniel's personal net worth is an illiquid business he cannot sell quickly — a useful contrast with the Chen household, whose business is 25% of net worth (85,000/340,000 = 25.0%).

**B4.** **Unchanged.** Borrowing adds 20,000 of cash (asset) and 20,000 of loan (liability): both sides rise by the same amount, so net worth — the difference — does not move. (Same logic as the business balance sheet equation: every transaction touches two places.)

### C (9)
**C1.** Net worth is the **cushion behind the loan**: assets that could be sold or drawn on to keep paying if income stops. An income statement shows annual flow; the net worth statement shows the stock of claims the household can absorb a shock and still repay.

**C2.** Any of: a home is **illiquid** — it cannot be sold in pieces to cover a missed payment, and selling it means moving; its market value can fall below the mortgage (negative equity on that asset); retirement accounts may be penalised or taxed if tapped early; possessions resell for a fraction of value. The lender is testing whether the *liquid* part of the cushion is adequate.

**C3.** The household owes more than it owns — there is no cushion at all, so any income interruption (job loss, illness) goes straight to default. Positive net worth = the household can absorb a shock and still repay; negative = nothing to fall back on.

### D (10)
**D1.** A: 620,000 − 410,000 = **210,000**. B: 620,000 − 10,000 = **610,000**. **Household B** is far better prepared — same gross assets, three times the net worth.

**D2.** Gross assets ignore the mortgage. Household A's 500,000 home is largely the **lender's** asset: after paying off the 410,000 mortgage, only 90,000 of it is actually the household's. Retirement is funded from what you *keep*, not what you *hold* — which is exactly why 3.7.C.3 asks for net worth, not asset value.

**D3.** The **savings/investments line** — it is drawn down to pay living costs in retirement, shrinking every year without any new transaction. (The home line also "shrinks" in usefulness if it must eventually be sold or reversed into income.) A's 120,000 of savings must cover the gap between retirement income and spending; B's 560,000 covers it many times over.

**D4.** **Lenders** — as part of the loan application process, to measure the cushion behind the loan (3.7.C.2). **Financial planners / consumers themselves** — to judge whether savings are sufficient to retire (3.7.C.3).

### Common errors
- Computing net worth per person instead of per household
- Including the coffee shop's gross assets instead of its equity (double-counts the shop's lenders)
- Thinking a new loan changes net worth (it changes both sides equally)
- Comparing households on gross assets — the A/B table exists to kill that habit
