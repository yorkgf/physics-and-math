## Course Description

AP Physics 1 is an algebra-based, introductory college-level physics course. Students cultivate their understanding of physics by developing models of physical phenomena through inquiry-based investigations. The course is equivalent to the first semester of an introductory college algebra-based physics sequence.

Students build understanding across eight content areas: kinematics, forces and translational dynamics, work/energy/power, linear momentum, torque and rotational dynamics, energy and momentum of rotating systems, oscillations, and fluids. Throughout, students practice three science practices — creating representations, applying mathematical routines, and scientific argumentation — by designing experiments, analyzing data, and justifying claims with evidence.

**Laboratory Requirement:** A minimum of 25% of instructional time is dedicated to hands-on, inquiry-based laboratory work. Students maintain a laboratory notebook throughout the year; colleges may request lab materials as part of the credit-granting process.

**Prerequisites:** Completed Geometry; concurrently enrolled in Algebra II or equivalent. Basic trigonometry (sin, cos, tan) is used throughout and can be learned concurrently.

---

## Course Objectives

Upon completion of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Create Representations** — Construct free-body diagrams, motion graphs, energy bar charts, and other physical representations
2. **Apply Mathematical Routines** — Derive symbolic expressions algebraically, calculate numerical results with appropriate units, compare quantities across scenarios, and predict functional dependencies
3. **Design and Argue** — Plan experimental procedures, apply physical laws to make claims, and justify those claims using evidence and reasoning
4. **Solve Across Domains** — Connect and apply concepts from all eight content areas to complex, multi-step problems
5. **Conduct Lab Investigations** — Design, execute, and write up inquiry-based experiments; identify and quantify sources of experimental uncertainty

Write-up: graph sketches, slope calculations, percent error analysis.

**Lab 2 – Newton's Second Law (2 periods)**
Cart on low-friction track connected via string over pulley to a hanging mass. Vary (a) hanging mass with fixed total mass and (b) total mass with fixed hanging mass. Measure acceleration with motion sensor. Plot F vs. a; extract slope = total mass. Compare to theoretical value; calculate percent error. Write-up in FRQ-style format.

**Lab 3 – Friction and Spring Forces (1–2 periods)**
(a) Measure μk by pulling a block at constant speed across two surface types; compare surfaces. (b) Measure spring constant k by hanging masses and plotting F vs. Δx; extract k from slope. Save k for Unit 3 energy lab.

Write-up requires energy bar chart diagrams.

Two carts on a low-friction track with motion sensors. Measure pre- and post-collision velocities for: (a) elastic collision (magnets repelling) and (b) perfectly inelastic collision (Velcro catch). Verify conservation of momentum in both cases. Calculate KE before and after each; confirm KE is conserved (elastic) and lost (inelastic). Lab report in FRQ-format.

**Lab 6 – Rotational Inertia (2 periods, spanning Jan 4–8 and Mar 1–5)**
A disk, ring, and solid cylinder are released from rest at the top of a ramp. (a) Predict finishing order using energy reasoning (rolling without slipping). **Lab 9 – Buoyancy (1–2 periods)**
Submerge objects of known mass and volume in water; measure buoyant force using spring scale (weight in air – apparent weight in water). Verify F_B = ρ_water V g. Determine density of irregular objects. Lab report: data table, sample calculation, conclusion about Archimedes' Principle.

**Lab 10 – Fluid Flow (1–2 periods)**
Use a tapered tube or a pipe system with valves. (a) Measure flow speed at wide and narrow sections using a flow meter or by timing dye; verify continuity (A₁v₁ ≈ A₂v₂). (b) Use a Bernoulli apparatus or water column manometer to verify pressure decreases where speed increases. Lab report: FRQ-style experimental design write-up.

### Free-Response Question Types

| FRQ | Type | Description |
|-----|------|-------------|
| Q1 | **Mathematical Routines (MR)** | Derive symbolic expressions, calculate quantities, compare results across scenarios |
| Q2 | **Translation Between Representations (TBR)** | Move between graphical, mathematical, and verbal descriptions of physical situations |
| Q3 | **Experimental Design and Analysis (EDA)** | Design an experiment, identify data to collect, analyze hypothetical data, identify errors |
| Q4 | **Qualitative/Quantitative Translation (QQT)** | Use physical reasoning to justify a quantitative claim, or predict qualitative behavior from a relationship |

**Project Options**

4. **Computational Model** — Simulate a physical system (Python/Desmos) and compare to real measurements
5. **Research and Analysis** — Analyze a real-world data set or phenomenon using course physics (e.g., tidal cycles, sports biomechanics)

## Course Policies

### Attendance
- Physics concepts build sequentially and labs cannot be made up individually — attendance is critical
- Students absent for a lab must complete a written alternative analysis assignment
- More than 3 unexcused absences may result in grade deductions

### Late Work
- 10% deduction per day late; maximum 50% after 3 days late
- Lab reports submitted more than one week late receive a maximum of 40%
- Exceptions for excused absences and emergencies

### Lab Notebook
- Students maintain a bound lab notebook throughout the course
- Each entry must include: date, question/hypothesis, procedure, data table, analysis, conclusion, and error discussion
- Notebooks are checked during lab write-up submissions and at the end of year
- Colleges may request lab materials as evidence for credit — keep all materials

### Calculator Policy
- A graphing or scientific calculator is required for this course and permitted on all assessments
- The College Board permits four-function, scientific, and graphing calculators on both sections of the AP Exam
- No CAS (Computer Algebra System) calculators permitted on AP Exam day

### Academic Integrity
- All submitted work must be your own
- Collaboration on lab data collection is allowed; all analysis and write-ups must be completed individually
- AI tools may be used to check understanding or explore concepts, but never to generate solutions for graded work
